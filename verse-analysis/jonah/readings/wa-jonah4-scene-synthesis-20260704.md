## JON-anger-at-mercy · Jonah 4:1–4 · [D, multi] — the resentment of grace shown to enemies

**The reading.** God's mercy to Nineveh detonates Jonah's real motive: "**it displeased Jonah exceedingly, and he was angry**" (v.1). His prayer is a confession of the true reason he fled: "**I knew that you are a gracious God and merciful, slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love, and relenting from disaster** — that is why I made haste to flee" (v.2). And the death-wish returns: "**it is better for me to die than to live**" (v.3). God's gentle question: "**Do you do well to be angry?**" (v.4). The finding is **a heart that *resents God's mercy* — that fled not from Nineveh's evil but from God's *goodness*, and would rather die than see grace given to enemies** *(stated)*. The operation is **the interior whose problem is God's compassion itself** *(inferred: the great reveal — Jonah quotes the classic creed of God's grace (Exod 34:6, "gracious, merciful, slow to anger, abounding in chesed") not in praise but in *complaint*: "*that is why* I fled" (v.2); the finding is a shocking inner-being fault — not doubt of God's mercy but *resentment* of it, when it is extended to those the self hates; Jonah believes God is gracious and *cannot forgive God for it*; the death-wish (v.3) shows how deep the resentment runs — he would rather not exist than live in a world where his enemies are spared; the finding exposes the narrow, ungenerous heart that wants grace for itself (ch.2) but not for others — and God meets it not with rebuke but with a *question* ("do you do well to be angry?"), inviting the interior to examine itself)*.

**Characteristics surfaced:** *the resentment of grace shown to enemies (fleeing God's goodness, not the task)* (new — a gem: the heart that cannot forgive God his mercy); *the ungenerous heart (grace for me, not for them)* (new — w/ the psalm's self-received salvation, 2:9); *God meeting resentment with a question, not a rebuke* (new).

## JON-plant-lesson · Jonah 4:5–11 · [D, multi] — the narrow heart vs the divine compassion

**The reading.** God stages a parable with a plant, a worm, and a scorching wind. Jonah is "**exceedingly glad because of the plant**" (v.6), then furious when it dies — "**it is better for me to die than to live**" (v.8); "I do well to be angry, **angry enough to die**" (v.9). God's closing question ends the book: "**You pity the plant**, for which you did not labour… **And should not I pity Nineveh, that great city, in which there are more than 120,000 persons who do not know their right hand from their left**?" (vv.10–11). The finding is **the self's *misplaced pity* — grieving a plant while begrudging a city — exposed against God's vast compassion for the ignorant multitudes** *(stated)*. The operation is **the narrow interior confronted by the breadth of God's mercy** *(inferred: the parable turns Jonah's own affections against him — he *pitied* (chus) a plant he did not make and that lasted a night; God argues from the lesser to the greater: if Jonah can feel for a plant, "*should not I pity* Nineveh?" (v.11) — 120,000 people "who do not know their right hand from their left" (morally ignorant, helpless); the finding is the exposure of a *disordered compassion* — the self can spend real feeling on its own comfort (the shade) while withholding it from human beings; and the book *ends on God's question*, unanswered — the interior is left (and the reader with it) to feel the gap between its narrow pity and God's wide compassion; the master-finding is the contrast between the resentful, self-referential human heart and the God whose deepest inclination (as Jonah himself confessed, 4:2) is mercy toward even the enemy and the ignorant)*.

**Characteristics surfaced:** *misplaced/disordered pity (grieving a plant, begrudging a city)* (new — a gem: real feeling spent on self-comfort, withheld from people); *the divine compassion for the ignorant multitude ("should not I pity Nineveh?")* (new — God's wide mercy vs the narrow heart); *the book ending on God's unanswered question (the interior left to examine itself)* (new).

## Chapter-level / book-level notes
- **Surfaced:** the resentment of mercy; misplaced pity; God's vast compassion; the open-ended question.
- **Book-level synthesis (Jonah):** Jonah is a narrative study of the **reluctant, resentful interior against God's compassion**. The prophet *flees* his calling and God's face (1:3), is *asleep* to God while pagans pray (1:5), cries from the pit that "**salvation belongs to the LORD**" (2:9) — yet cannot bear that same salvation given to Nineveh: he *resents* God's grace (4:2), preferring death to a world where enemies are spared, and spends real *pity* on a plant while begrudging a city (4:10–11). Against him stand the *responsive outsiders* — sailors who come to fear the LORD (1:16) and a city that *turns* wholesale (3:5–10) — and, above all, the **God whose deepest inclination is mercy** (4:2, the Exodus 34 creed) and who *pities* the ignorant multitude. Its master-finding for the corpus: **the narrow, ungenerous heart that wants grace for itself but resents it for others**, set against **the wideness of God's compassion** — the book ending on God's question, left for the interior to answer.
- **Method note:** narrative-scene processing (6 scenes) fits Jonah; genre is prose narrative (cross-verse causality carried in the scene-readings rather than the per-verse lexical).
