## Psa 1:1-6

> Psa 1:1 Blessed is the man who walks not in the counsel of the wicked , nor stands in the way of sinners , nor sits in the seat of scoffers ; Psa 1:2 but his delight is in the law of the Lord , and on his law he meditates day and night . Psa 1:3 He is like a tree planted by streams of water that yields its fruit in its season , and its leaf does not wither . In all that he does , he prospers . Psa 1:4 The wicked are not so , but are like chaff that the wind drives away. Psa 1:5 Therefore the wicked will not stand in the judgment , nor sinners in the congregation of the righteous ; Psa 1:6 for the Lord knows the way of the righteous , but the way of the wicked will perish .

**sits** — Psa 1:1  ·  `H3427:sit#1`

_Story_

The psalm opens by praising a man for what he will not do. He will not walk along with the wicked, will not stop and stand where sinners gather, and - deepest of all - will not sit down and settle among those who mock. Sitting means belonging, and he refuses to belong there; it is a firm choice of the will, a settled inner No, not something forced on him. The psalm names nothing that tempts him and promises no reward in the moment - it simply shows which way his inner life leans: away from the company of scorn. In a group of passages otherwise full of defiance against God, this one stands apart, because here it is defiance itself that is being turned down.

_Reading_

The sense (101) is 'sits not in the seat of scoffers', read as a volition (type 102): an act the will settles, not an affect that arrives. The bearer (105) is the blessed man, and the operation (106) is the third and deepest of three refusals - he does not sit in the seat of scoffers, declining the settled fellowship of scorn - with its target (107) marked 'refusal-3', the climax of a descent the interior turns down. The coupling (112) 'sit-not' binds the verb to its own negation: the finding is the refusal itself, the will's fixed No to belonging. No source is recorded (103 absent) - the passage names nothing that provokes or pushes the refusal; it stands as free, un-triggered volition. No organ seats it (104 none): this is a whole-self posture, not lodged in heart or eyes. Manner is unmarked (108 none), and intensity, specifier, effect, and prohibition are all unrecorded (109/110/111/113 absent) - the verse grades no degree, adds no qualifier, names no in-verse result, and frames no command; it simply shows the will's settled refusal. The discovery (114) reads the triad walk->stand->sit as the whole slide the interior declines, sitting being permanent belonging and scoffers the most corrosive company. Role is characteristic (115); locus is the interior's own state (116). In a family otherwise built on defiance against God, this reading is the inverse pole - here rebellion is the very thing refused, and the wicked/sinners/scoffers are the arena the inner life turns from.

**rebelled** — Psa 5:10  ·  `H4784:rebell#1`

_Story_

Surrounded by treacherous enemies, the psalmist asks God to let them collapse under their own schemes, and he names why: they have rebelled against God. The point is not just their lies against him but what those lies expose - an inner life set in opposition to God. Their deceit toward a man turns out to be, at root, revolt against God. The psalm does not explain what made them this way or measure how far their rebellion goes; it simply reads their treachery as the surface of a deeper defiance. What we see is an interior bent against God, betraying itself in how it treats others.

_Reading_

The sense (101) is that the enemies have rebelled, read as a volition (type 102) - a set of the will, not merely a deed. The bearer (105) is the enemies, and the operation (106) is that they have rebelled against God, so the self asks God to let them fall by their own counsels: revolt is diagnosed as their bent. The target (107) is 'rebellion' itself and the coupling (112) 'rebelled-against-God' fixes the direction of the revolt Godward. No source is recorded (103 absent) - no cause or trigger is assigned; the rebellion stands as their own inward orientation. No organ seats it (104 none): it is a whole-self direction, not lodged in an organ. Manner is unmarked (108 none); intensity, specifier, effect, and prohibition are unrecorded (109/110/111/113 absent) - the verse grades no degree, adds no qualifier, names no in-verse consequence, frames no command. The discovery (114) is the finding: beneath the enemies' deceit toward the psalmist lies a deeper diagnosis - their interior is set against God himself, so their treachery to the man is really revolt against God. Role is characteristic (115); locus is the interior's own state (116). The lens stays on the enemies' inner orientation, with God as the arena their revolt is aimed at.

*Why this reading differs:* This item (H4784:rebell) has five readings. Here (#1) the bearer is the psalmist's enemies and the type is volition - rebellion diagnosed as the settled Godward bent beneath their deceit. In the four wilderness readings (#2 78:17, #3 78:40, #4 106:7, #5 107:11) the same marah is typed as action and the bearer is Israel's own fathers rebelling against God in the desert or against his words. The shift in bearer (outside enemies vs. the covenant people) and type (volition vs. repeated action) is the finding: one word names both the outsider's fixed hostility and the insider's recurring defiance.

## Psa 22:1-6

> To the choirmaster : according to The Doe of the Dawn . A Psalm of David . Psa 22:1 My God , my God , why have you forsaken me? Why are you so far from saving me, from the words of my groaning ? Psa 22:2 O my God , I cry by day , but you do not answer , and by night , but I find no rest . Psa 22:3 Yet you are holy , enthroned on the praises of Israel . **forsaken** — Psa 22:1  ·  `H5800:forsaken#1`

_Story_

The psalmist cries out to God with the hardest question there is: why have you left me? He feels utterly abandoned - God seems far off, deaf to his groaning, out of reach of any rescue. This is not doubt about whether God is real; it is the raw pain of God's felt absence. The psalm gives no reason for the abandonment and offers no comfort in the moment - the sufferer cannot even say why it has come, and no answer is supplied. What we see is a whole self sinking into the agony of feeling forsaken by the one it still calls 'my God'.

_Reading_

The sense (101) is 'why have you forsaken me', read as affect (type 102): this forsakenness is felt, an emotional condition, not a chosen act. The bearer (105) is the psalmist, and the operation (106) is the self's cry that God has forsaken it and is far from saving, far from the words of its groaning - the extremity of felt abandonment. The target (107) is 'forsakenness' and the coupling (112) 'why-forsaken' binds the movement to its own unanswered question. No source is recorded (103 absent) - and here the silence is itself the wound: no cause for the abandonment is given, and the sufferer cannot name why. No organ seats it (104 none): the desolation fills the whole self rather than lodging in a named seat. Manner is unmarked (108 none); intensity, specifier, effect, and prohibition are unrecorded (109/110/111/113 absent) - the cry is neither graded, subtyped, given an in-verse resolution, nor framed as command; it is left raw and open. The discovery (114) sharpens the finding: this is the interior's rawest desolation - not doubt that God exists but the agony of his felt absence. Role is characteristic (115); locus is the interior's own state (116). God is the one the cry is flung toward, the arena; the movement is the inner life's collapse into felt abandonment.

*Why this reading differs:* This item (H5800:forsaken) has three readings. Here (#1) forsakenness is an affect suffered - God's felt abandonment of the psalmist. In #2 (27:10) it is again affect but the forsakers are father and mother, and the movement resolves upward into trust (the LORD takes me in). In #3 (119:87) it becomes a disposition and is negated - the psalmist has NOT forsaken God's precepts though nearly destroyed. Across the three the bearer and direction invert: from being forsaken (by God-felt, by parents) to refusing to forsake.

**worm** — Psa 22:6  ·  `H8438:worm#1`

_Story_

Battered by contempt, the psalmist looks at himself and sees not a man but a worm - despised, mocked, treated as less than human. This is not a mood that comes and goes but a settled sense of who he now is: his worth has caved in under the scorn of others. The psalm does not spell out exactly what did this or how far it goes; it simply lets the crushed self-estimate stand. The scorn of the crowd is the pressure, but what we actually see is a person whose inner sense of himself has sunk below being human at all.

_Reading_

The sense (101) is 'I am a worm, not a man', read as a state (type 102): a condition of the self, not an act or a passing feeling. The bearer (105) is the psalmist, and the operation (106) is that he reckons himself a worm and not a man, scorned and despised by all - a self-estimate crushed below humanity. The target (107) is 'self-abasement' and the coupling (112) 'worm-not-man' binds the self-reckoning to its own degradation. No source is recorded (103 absent) - though mockery presses from every side, the field assigns no cause; the state simply stands. No organ seats it (104 none): the collapse is a whole-self condition, not seated in a named organ. Manner is unmarked (108 none); intensity, specifier, effect, and prohibition are unrecorded (109/110/111/113 absent) - the abasement is not graded, subtyped, given an in-verse outcome, or commanded. The discovery (114) reads it as a collapse of self-worth: mockery has driven the interior below the human. Role is characteristic (115); locus is the interior's own state (116). Mankind and the people are the arena whose scorn presses in, but the reading is of the interior's own reckoning sinking beneath manhood.

## Psa 27:10

> Psa 27:10 For my father and my mother have forsaken me, but the Lord will take me in.

**forsaken** — Psa 27:10  ·  `H5800:forsaken#2`

_Story_

The psalmist imagines the most painful abandonment of all - even a father and mother turning away - and answers it with quiet confidence: the LORD will take me in. The felt loss is real, but it is met and overtaken by a deeper security. The psalm does not explain where this confidence comes from or how strongly it is held; it simply rests there, unshaken by the worst human failure. What we see is an inner life that has found a footing beyond even the closest family bonds.

_Reading_

The sense (101) is 'though parents forsake, God takes me in', read as affect (type 102) - a felt orientation, not a deliberate act. The bearer (105) is the psalmist, and the operation (106) is that even if father and mother forsake it, the self is confident the LORD will take it up: security reaching beyond the closest human bonds. The target (107) is 'abandonment-answered' and the coupling (112) 'God-takes-me-in' binds the felt abandonment to its resolution. No source is recorded (103 absent) - the psalm does not trace where this confidence comes from, only that it holds when parents fail. No organ seats it (104 none): the security is a whole-self stance, not organ-lodged. Manner is unmarked (108 none); intensity, specifier, effect, and prohibition are unrecorded (109/110/111/113 absent) - no degree, no qualifier, no in-verse outcome beyond the assurance, no command. The discovery (114) reads it as trust that outlasts the failure of the deepest human belonging: the interior rests where even parents cannot be relied on. Role is characteristic (115); locus is the interior's own state (116). Parents are the arena whose forsaking is imagined; the movement is the inner life anchored in God past that loss.

*Why this reading differs:* This item (H5800:forsaken) has three readings. Here (#2) forsakenness is an affect, but the forsakers are father and mother and the movement resolves into trust that God receives the abandoned self. In #1 (22:1) it is the felt abandonment by God himself, left raw and unanswered. In #3 (119:87) it is a disposition, negated - the psalmist has not forsaken God's precepts. The same word swings from being forsaken by God, to being forsaken by parents yet held, to refusing to forsake God: bearer and direction shift each time.

**Refrain** — Psa 37:8  ·  `H7503:refrain#1`

_Story_

The psalm urges the listener to let go of anger and put wrath aside, warning that stewing over things leads only to harm. This is presented as something to choose and do, not a feeling to wait out: deliberately release the anger. The verse does not say what stirred the anger in the first place; it cares only about setting it down. And though it warns that brooding does damage, its whole weight falls on the act of letting go. What we see is an inner life being coached to release its own resentment before it turns into something worse.

_Reading_

The sense (101) is 'refrain from anger, forsake wrath', read as a volition (type 102): an act the will must perform, not a feeling that simply comes. The bearer (105) is the hearer being addressed, and the operation (106) is the deliberate release of anger - the self is told to let go of anger and forsake wrath, for fretting tends only to evil. The target (107) is 'anger-release' and the coupling (112) 'refrain-from-anger' binds the command to its object. No source is recorded (103 absent) - the verse does not trace where the anger arose; it addresses only its letting-go. No organ seats it (104 none): the release is a whole-self act, not lodged in a named seat. Manner is unmarked (108 none); intensity, specifier, effect, and prohibition are unrecorded (109/110/111/113 absent) - though the verse warns that fretting 'tends only to evil', the effect field itself is empty, and no degree, qualifier, or formal command-prohibition is marked. The discovery (114) reads it as the letting-go of anger, the interior warned that nursed wrath breeds only harm. Role is characteristic (115); locus is the interior's own state (116). This reading, like the refusals in the family, is a turning-from - here the inner life is urged to release its own anger before it curdles into evil.

**leave** — Psa 49:10  ·  `H5800:leav#1`

_Story_

The psalm observes a hard fact: wise and foolish alike must die, and when they do they leave their wealth to others. The word for it is the same one used elsewhere for forsaking or abandoning - here it is the forced letting-go of everything a person hoarded, handed off to strangers who did not earn it. Nothing is welded to this leaving; it stands alone and stark, as death does. The psalm does not dress it up or soften it. What we see is the inner life brought face to face with the emptiness of piling up things that cannot follow it past the grave.

_Reading_

The sense (101) is 'leave / abandon' (azab - wealth to others), read as an action (type 102) rather than a state or affect. The bearer (105) is the dying, wise and fool alike, and the operation (106) is to leave behind, its target (107) their wealth, passed to others. Here the coupling is explicitly 'none' (112, resolution none): no companion movement is welded to it - the leaving stands bare and solitary, which fits its meaning, since death strips a person of every tie to what they held. No source is recorded (103 absent): the verse does not trace a cause beyond the plain fact of dying. No organ seats it (104 none): it is not an organ's act but the whole person's forced release at death. Manner is unmarked (108 none); intensity, specifier, effect, and prohibition are unrecorded (109/110/111/113 absent) - no degree, qualifier, in-verse outcome, or command. The discovery (114) reads it as the futility of hoarding: azab here is the forced abandonment of wealth at death, the riches passing to strangers, possessions that cannot follow. Role is characteristic (115); locus is the interior's own state (116). Uniquely in this family the target is not God or law but wealth - 'forsaking' turned outward onto possessions; the inner-being finding is the interior's confrontation with the emptiness of what it cannot keep.

## Psa 66:1-9

> To the choirmaster . A Song . A Psalm . Psa 66:1 Shout for joy to God , all the earth ; Psa 66:2 sing the glory of his name ; give to him glorious praise ! Psa 66:3 Say to God , “ How awesome are your deeds ! So great is your power that your enemies come cringing to you. Psa 66:6 He turned the sea into dry land ; they passed through the river on foot . There did we rejoice in him , Psa 66:7 who rules by his might forever , whose eyes keep watch on the nations — let not the rebellious exalt themselves. Selah Psa 66:8 Bless our God , O peoples ; let the sound of his praise be heard , Psa 66:9 who has kept our soul among the living and has not let our feet slip . Psa 66:10 For you, O God, have tested us; you have tried us as silver is tried. Psa 66:11 You brought us into the net ; you laid a crushing burden on our backs ;

**rebellious** — Psa 66:7  ·  `H5637:rebelliou#1`

_Story_

As the psalm celebrates God's rule over the nations, it adds a warning: let the rebellious not lift themselves up. What marks these defiant ones is that God's eyes are on them - their revolt takes place under his constant watch, and that is what hems it in. Their self-exaltation has no real object outside themselves; it is pride curving back on itself. The psalm does not measure their defiance or spell out a punishment; it simply sets them under the gaze of the God who rules everything. What we see is a rebellious inner life warned not to raise itself against the One who is watching.

_Reading_

The sense (101) is 'let not the rebellious exalt themselves' (sarar), read as a status (type 102): a settled condition of the person, not a single deed. Unusually for this family, a source IS recorded (103, a span pair): the rebellious are 'watched by God's eyes on the nations' - their defiance is set under God's surveillance, the origin-context that hems it in. The bearer (105) is the rebellious, and the operation (106), also a span pair, is 'exalt themselves' - the self-lifting God's watching eyes restrain. The target (107) is 'none' (resolution none): the self-exaltation is aimed at no named object beyond the self - it curves back on itself, self-referential pride. The coupling (112, pair) binds the rebellious to their self-exalting. No organ seats it (104 none). Manner is unmarked (108 none); intensity, specifier, effect, and prohibition are unrecorded (109/110/111/113 absent) - the verse neither grades nor forbids in a formal sense, though the jussive 'let not' hovers over it. The discovery (114) reads it as the defiant whom God's watching eyes restrain, warned not to lift themselves against the God who rules the nations. Role is characteristic (115); locus is the interior's own state (116). The presence of a source here - divine watching - is itself the finding: this rebellion is defined by the God who oversees it.

*Why this reading differs:* This item (H5637:rebelliou, sarar) has three readings, all typed as status. Here (#1) a source is present - God's watching eyes on the nations - and the operation is self-exaltation restrained. In #2 (68:6) the operation is dwelling in a parched land, a scorched exile marked as their manner - rebellion's consequence. In #3 (68:18) the astonishing turn: God receives gifts and dwells even among the rebellious - grace reaching the defiant. Across the three the same word moves restraint -> judgment -> grace: how the rebellious are handled shifts entirely with context.

**rebellious** — Psa 68:6  ·  `H5637:rebelliou#2`

_Story_

The psalm pictures God as one who gives the lonely a home and leads prisoners out into freedom - and then sets the rebellious against that picture: they are left to live in a parched, sun-scorched land. Their defiance does not get its own dramatic scene here; instead we see where it ends up - a barren, homeless waste. That dry exile is the exact opposite of the homes God provides for others. The psalm does not say how their rebellion began, only how it turns out. What we see is a defiant inner life that ends up shut out of the shelter God freely gives.

_Reading_

The sense (101) is 'the rebellious dwell in a parched land' (sarar), read as a status (type 102): a settled condition, not a single act. The bearer (105) is the rebellious, and the operation (106) is to dwell in a parched land - here the movement is not their defiance directly but its outcome-place. The manner (108) is marked and load-bearing: 'in a sun-scorched land' - the one graded qualifier in the reading, coloring their dwelling as barren exile. The target (107) is 'none' (resolution none): the dwelling is aimed at no object; it is simply where rebellion lands. The coupling (112, span pair) is the finding: the rebellious are set against 'the solitary God settles in a home and the prisoners he leads out to prosperity' - their scorched homelessness is the deliberate foil to God's home-giving. No source is recorded (103 absent): the verse does not trace the origin of their rebellion, only its reward. No organ seats it (104 none). Intensity, specifier, effect, and prohibition are unrecorded (109/110/111/113 absent). The discovery (114) reads it as the defiant left in barren exile - rebellion's reward a scorched, homeless waste. Role is characteristic (115); locus is the interior's own state (116). God is the arena whose home-giving they are contrasted against; the inner-being reading is of an orientation that ends outside the shelter God gives.

*Why this reading differs:* This item (H5637:rebelliou, sarar) has three readings, all status. Here (#2) the operation is dwelling in a scorched land - rebellion's barren consequence, uniquely carrying a marked manner ('sun-scorched'), and paired as the foil to God's home-giving. In #1 (66:7) the rebellious are under God's watching eyes and restrained from self-exaltation. In #3 (68:18) God dwells even among them - grace. The same word moves restraint -> judgment (here) -> grace across the set.

**despised** — Psa 119:141  ·  `H0959:despis#1`

_Story_

The psalmist describes himself as small and despised - looked down on, treated as of no account - and yet, he says, he does not forget God's precepts. Here contempt is something he receives, not something he hands out; he is on the wrong end of others' scorn. The psalm does not say exactly who despises him or why; it simply lets the lowly condition stand. What matters is that even in this diminished, scorned state, his hold on God's word stays unbroken. What we see is a belittled inner life that refuses to loosen its grip on God.

_Reading_

The sense (101) is 'be despised' (bazah), read as a state (type 102): a condition the self endures, not an act it performs. The bearer (105) is 'the psalmist', and the operation (106) is to be small and despised. The target (107) is 'none' (resolution none): the despising has no object the psalmist aims at - he is its recipient, not its agent, so no target is fitting. The coupling (112) is marked 'paired within its char-arc across the psalm' - one beat in Psalm 119's larger thread. No source is recorded (103 absent): though foes and the insolent surround him, the field names no cause; the lowly state simply stands. No organ seats it (104 none): it is a whole-self condition, not organ-lodged. Manner is unmarked (108 none); intensity, specifier, effect, and prohibition are unrecorded (109/110/111/113 absent). The discovery (114) reads it as the low, despised estate held with unbroken memory - 'I am small and despised, yet I do not forget your precepts'. Role is characteristic (115); locus is the interior's own state (116). Distinct from the fathers' scorn of God's gift (maas, 106:24) and the enemy's contempt of God's name (naats, 74:10), this bazah is contempt received, not given: the inner life is the object of others' scorn, yet holds its grip on God's word unbroken. Others are the arena whose contempt presses; the finding is a small, despised self that does not let go of God.
