## 1. The fallen face: denied, the body withdraws (FF) — Gen 4:5, 4:6; 1Ki 20:43; 21:4

- **What it is.** Anger that does not go out at the one who caused it, but **sinks into the body**: the face falls, the person lies down, turns the face away, and will not eat.
- **What it does.**
  - "Cain was very angry, and his face fell" (Gen 4:5).
  - Ahab went home "vexed and sullen … lay down on his bed and turned away his face and would eat no food" (1Ki 21:4).
  - In 1Ki 20:43 the same pair, "vexed and sullen", takes him home to Samaria.
  - The movement is **inward and downward**: face, bed, refusing food.
- **Where it comes from.** From **a denial the person cannot reverse**:
  - no regard from God for Cain's offering while Abel's is regarded (Gen 4:4–5)
  - the prophet's verdict, "your life shall be for his life, and your people for his people" (1Ki 20:42)
  - Naboth's refusal, "I will not give you the inheritance of my fathers" (1Ki 21:3–4)

  In each case the denier is out of the angry person's reach: God, a prophet speaking for God, and a man protected by the law of inheritance.
- **How it co-exists.**
  - **Someone else reads it off the body and names it.** God asks Cain, "Why are you angry, and why has your face fallen?" (Gen 4:6). Jezebel asks Ahab, "Why is your spirit so vexed that you eat no food?" (1Ki 21:5). **The angry person names nothing. The inner state is put into words by the questioner.**
  - **The questioner then offers a way forward, and the two ways are opposites.**
    - God: "If you do well, will you not be accepted? … sin is crouching at the door … you must rule over it" (Gen 4:7). This is **mastery of the anger from within**.
    - Jezebel: "Arise and eat bread and let your heart be cheerful; I will give you the vineyard" (1Ki 21:7). This is **removing the denial by a crime**.
- **What it leads to.** In both passages, **killing**.
  - "Cain rose up against his brother Abel and killed him" (Gen 4:8).
  - Naboth is falsely charged and "stoned to death" (1Ki 21:13). "As soon as Ahab heard that Naboth was dead, Ahab arose to go down to the vineyard … to take possession of it" (21:16). **Ahab rises from the bed he lay down on in 21:4.**

  The anger that sank inward comes out as a death: **Cain's by his own hand, Ahab's by another's.**
- **Seat.**
  - **In the verse:** none of the four.
  - **Whose seat:** in context, **named by the other party.** 1Ki 21:5 "spirit" is Ahab's own, but Jezebel names it, not the narrator (C v3 §9.7: "spirit state stops appetite"; pilot §1.4). **Genuine.**
  - **Outside the verse:**
    - **Before:** 1Ki 20:42 *nephesh* "your life shall be for his life". **Life-sense only.** It is the verdict on Ahab's life that sends him home sullen (H v1 §2.2, "the price of a life, again").
    - **After:** 1Ki 21:7 "let your heart be cheerful". **Genuine.** The heart is offered as the place where the anger will be undone, and the price is Naboth (C v3 §1.6).
    - **Gen 4:** no seat word in Gen 4:2–9. God's question and the image of sin "crouching at the door" do the seat's work: **the inner struggle is given as a scene at a door, not as an organ.** This agrees with pilot §1.4 and does not change it.

## 2. Strife among kin and brothers, refused or fought over (KS) — Gen 13:8; Deu 2:9, 2:19; 2Sa 19:42 · (v2) Pro 18:19; Song 1:6; Eze 35:11; Amo 1:11; Mat 5:22; Act 15:39

- **What it is.** Contention between parties who are **kin**, where kinship is named as the reason to stop it, or as the ground of the quarrel.
- **What it does.**
  - Abram **refuses** it: "Let there be no strife between you and me … for we are kinsmen" (Gen 13:8).
  - God **forbids** it: "Do not harass Moab … because I have given Ar to the people of Lot" (Deu 2:9). Likewise Ammon, "the sons of Lot" (Deu 2:19).
  - The men of Judah and Israel **fight over the king** with kinship as the weapon: "Because the king is our close relative. Why then are you angry over this matter?" (2Sa 19:42).
- **Where it comes from.**
  - **Scarcity:** "the land could not support both of them" (Gen 13:6), and "strife between the herdsmen" (13:7).
  - **Rival claims on a person:** "Why have our brothers the men of Judah stolen you away?" (2Sa 19:41).
- **How it co-exists.**
  - In Gen 13 strife is **ended by giving the other the choice**: "Is not the whole land before you? … If you take the left hand, then I will go to the right" (13:9). **The refusal of strife is carried out as a yielding.** Lot then "lifted up his eyes and saw" (13:10) and chose.
  - In 2Sa 19 kinship **feeds** the quarrel. Each side counts its share: "We have ten shares in the king" (19:43), and it ends with "the words of the men of Judah were fiercer than the words of the men of Israel" (19:43). **The same ground, kinship, stops strife in Gen 13 and inflames it in 2Sa 19.**
- **What it leads to.** Separation in peace (Gen 13:11), a boundary kept (Deu 2), or **words growing fiercer** (2Sa 19:43).
- **Seat.** None in the verses or their ±3 context. Kinship does the seat's work of grounding the movement. Compare E v2 R7, kinship and conflict words in M44.
- **(v2) Part 2 verses: what they add.**
  - **Kin offence hardens.** "A brother offended is more unyielding than a strong city, and quarreling is like the bars of a castle" (Pro 18:19). **The closer the tie, the harder the wall.** This is the reverse of Gen 13's reason for yielding ("for we are kinsmen").
  - **Brothers' anger takes the form of imposed labour:** "My mother's sons were angry with me; they made me keeper of the vineyards, but my own vineyard I have not kept!" (Song 1:6). **The anger is felt by the one it lands on as the loss of her own vineyard.** The next verse turns straight to "you whom my soul loves" (1:7).
  - **Edom against his brother: anger that never turns away.** Edom "pursued his brother with the sword and cast off all pity, and **his anger tore perpetually, and he kept his wrath forever**" (Amo 1:11). God will deal with Edom "according to the anger and envy that you showed because of your hatred against them" (Eze 35:11), with its speech: "They are given us to devour" (35:12).
    - **This is exactly the opposite of Gen 27:44–45.** There Rebekah waits for Esau's anger to "turn away" and for him to "forget", and it does (Gen 33:4). **Esau the man let his anger pass. Edom, his nation, "kept his wrath forever".** The same bloodline shows anger's two courses (§3).
    - Amo 1:9 names the same failure for Tyre: "did not remember the covenant of brotherhood".
  - **Anger at a brother judged like murder, and the remedy is reconciliation before worship:** "everyone who is angry with his brother will be liable to judgment" (Mat 5:22); "leave your gift there before the altar and go. First be reconciled to your brother" (5:24). **The worship waits on the relationship.**
  - **Brothers in the work part without the yielding:** "there arose a sharp disagreement, so that they separated from each other. Barnabas took Mark … but Paul chose Silas" (Act 15:39–40). It began over Mark, "who had withdrawn from them" (15:38). **Separation, as in Gen 13:11, but here neither party gives the other the choice.** Both are "commended … to the grace of the Lord" (15:40) and the work goes on in two directions.
  - **Seat (v2):** none in-verse or ±2. The next verse after Song 1:6 has "you whom my soul loves" (Song 1:7). **Genuine. The bearer's own** (F v4 §1.2; E v2 §7.3). **The one who bears her brothers' anger turns at once to the one her soul loves.**

## 3. Anger's own course: turning away, cherished, lodged, fatal (AL) — Gen 27:44, 27:45; Job 5:2; 36:13; 36:18 · (v2) Pro 6:34; 11:23; 12:16; 19:19; 27:3; 27:4; Ecc 7:9; Eph 4:26

- **What it is.** Anger treated as **something with a life of its own**. It lasts, turns away, is cherished, entices, and kills the one who carries it.
- **What it does.**
  - It **runs a course and passes**, with time and forgetting: "until your brother's fury turns away … until your brother's anger turns away from you, and he forgets what you have done to him" (Gen 27:44–45).
  - It is **held and fed**: "The godless in heart cherish anger" (Job 36:13).
  - It **draws the bearer on**: "Beware lest wrath entice you into scoffing" (Job 36:18).
  - It **kills its bearer**: "Surely vexation kills the fool, and jealousy slays the simple" (Job 5:2).
- **Where it comes from.**
  - For Esau, from the stolen blessing: "Esau hated Jacob because of the blessing" (Gen 27:41).
  - Job 36:13 gives its source in the heart: "the godless **in heart**".
- **How it co-exists.**
  - **With a murder plan spoken inwardly:** "Esau said to himself, '… then I will kill my brother Jacob'" (Gen 27:41). And Rebekah's reading of it: "your brother Esau **comforts himself** about you by planning to kill you" (27:42). **The anger is kept going by a plan that soothes it.**
  - **With the refusal to cry out:** "they do not cry for help when he binds them" (Job 36:13). Cherished anger shuts off the cry.
  - **Rebekah's movement is the counter-weight:** removing the object (flight) and waiting for the anger's course to end. Her own inner state follows at once: "Why should I be bereft of you both in one day?" (27:45), and "I loathe my life" (27:46).
- **What it leads to.**
  - For Esau: the anger turns away over years, and there is no killing. When they meet, "Esau ran to meet him and embraced him … and they wept" (Gen 33:4). **Rebekah's "until he forgets" comes true, beyond embrace.**
  - For the godless: "They die in youth" (Job 36:14).
  - For the fool: death by his own vexation (Job 5:2).
  - Eliphaz sets this beside "man is born to trouble as the sparks fly upward" (Job 5:7). **Trouble is universal, but dying of vexation is the fool's.**
- **Seat.**
  - **In the verse:** Job 36:13 "heart" (*lev*). **The bearer's own. Genuine.** A v3 §2.1 read it as human anger in the inner seat. G v3 §16.5 read it with 36:14 as death as accountability. Both stand. The phenomenon adds only that **the heart here is where anger is kept, not where it flares.**
  - **Outside the verse:** Gen 27:41 "said to himself" (Hebrew *in his heart*). **Genuine, the bearer's own** (F v4 §8.2, "hatred spoken in the heart, and leaking out"). The seat arrives **three verses before** the anger word, as the place where the plan is spoken.
  - Job 36:18: no seat. Whose wrath it is was settled in §36.
- **(v2) Part 2 verses: what they add.**
  - **Anger lodges, and where it lodges is named.** "Be not quick in your spirit to become angry, for **anger lodges in the heart of fools**" (Ecc 7:9). This joins Job 36:13 ("cherish anger"). **Anger that stays has a lodging, and the lodging is the fool's.** The verse before sets up the pair: "the patient in spirit is better than the proud in spirit" (7:8).
  - **Its duration is limited to a day:** "Be angry and do not sin; do not let the sun go down on your anger, and give no opportunity to the devil" (Eph 4:26–27). **Where Gen 27 waited years for anger to turn away, the NT sets a limit of one day.** The reason given is what anger left overnight opens: "opportunity to the devil".
  - **It repeats:** "A man of great wrath will pay the penalty, for if you deliver him, you will only have to do it again" (Pro 19:19). **Great wrath is a pattern, not an event.** Rescue does not end it.
  - **It is weighed:**
    - "A stone is heavy, and sand is weighty, but a fool's provocation is heavier than both" (Pro 27:3). This echoes Job's "heavier than the sand of the sea" (Job 6:3, §8).
    - "Wrath is cruel, anger is overwhelming, but who can stand before jealousy?" (Pro 27:4). **Anger is measured, and jealousy is found to be worse.**
  - **Jealousy makes it unappeasable:** "jealousy makes a man furious, and he will not spare when he takes revenge. He will accept no compensation" (Pro 6:34–35). **This is the one anger in Proverbs that a gift will not avert.** Compare §23: "A gift in secret averts anger" (Pro 21:14).
  - **It shows at once:** "The vexation of a fool is known at once, but the prudent ignores an insult" (Pro 12:16). **The fool's anger cannot be hidden, while the prudent one lets the insult pass unanswered.**
  - **It is where the wicked end up:** "the expectation of the wicked [ends] in wrath" (Pro 11:23, B5, settled here tentatively, §36).
- **Seat (v2).**
  - **In the verse:** Ecc 7:9 "spirit" (*ruach*) and "heart" (literally *bosom*). **The bearer's own. Genuine** (A v3 §2.1: "two stages, two seats"; F v4 §9.5, "what rests in or on a person"; B v3 §2.4). Nothing new is needed.
  - **Outside the verse:** Ecc 7:8 "patient in spirit … proud in spirit". **Genuine** (C v3 §5.2; D v2 §9.2). Pro 6:32 "he who does it destroys himself" (*nephesh*). **Genuine. The adulterer's own self**, two verses before the husband's fury (D v2 §3.2; G v3 §9.5). **The fury of Pro 6:34 is the other party's answer to one who has already destroyed his own soul.**

## 4. Anger when one's will is blocked, exposed or refused (TH) — 17 verses

Verses: Gen 30:2; Num 22:27; 24:10; 2Ki 5:11, 5:12; 6:11; 2Ch 16:10; 25:10; 26:19; Est 1:12, 1:18 · (v2) Dan 2:12; 3:13; 3:19; 11:30; 11:44; Mat 2:16.

- **What it is.** Anger in someone whose **purpose meets resistance**. Four strands emerged, and they work differently.
- **(a) A demand the angry one cannot meet.** Gen 30:2: "Jacob's anger was kindled against Rachel, and he said, 'Am I in the place of God, who has withheld from you the fruit of the womb?'"
  - It comes from Rachel's "Give me children, or I shall die!" (30:1), which itself came from envy: "she envied her sister" (30:1).
  - **The anger answers an impossible demand by naming who alone could meet it.** Rachel then turns to a workaround: "Here is my servant Bilhah" (30:3).
- **(b) Thwarted and made a fool of.**
  - Balaam: "Balaam's anger was kindled, and he struck the donkey with his staff" (Num 22:27). When the donkey speaks, he gives his reason: "Because you have made a fool of me. I wish I had a sword in my hand, for then I would kill you" (22:29). **The anger is about being shamed, and it wishes to kill.**
  - Balak: "Balak's anger was kindled against Balaam, and he struck his hands together … 'I called you to curse my enemies, and behold, you have blessed them these three times'" (Num 24:10). It ends in dismissal and the withholding of honour: "the Lord has held you back from honor" (24:11).
  - Naaman: "Naaman was angry and went away, saying, 'Behold, I thought that he would surely come out to me …'" (2Ki 5:11), and "he turned and went away in a rage" (5:12). **What the anger releases is a disappointed expectation of honour**, quoted as his own thought. Pilot §1.4 read the whole movement: rage → persuasion → obedience → restoration.
  - The Ephraimite army, discharged, "became very angry with Judah and returned home in fierce anger" (2Ch 25:10), and later "raided the cities of Judah" (25:13). **Anger at being sent away returns as violence against those who sent it.**
- **(c) Rebuked by a holy person.**
  - Asa, told by the seer that he "relied on the king of Syria", "was angry with the seer and put him in the stocks … for he was in a rage with him" (2Ch 16:10). The rage spreads: "Asa inflicted cruelties upon some of the people at the same time" (16:10).
  - Uzziah, withstood by the priests at the altar: "Uzziah was angry … and when he became angry with the priests, leprosy broke out on his forehead" (2Ch 26:19).
  - **In both, the anger is against the messenger of a boundary, and the story ends with the angry king's own body struck:** Asa "diseased in his feet … Yet even in his disease he did not seek the Lord" (16:12); Uzziah "a leper to the day of his death" (26:21).
- **(d) Refused by a subordinate, or exposed.**
  - Vashti's refusal: "the king became enraged, and his anger burned within him" (Est 1:12). The counsellor at once generalises the fear to every household: "there will be contempt and wrath in plenty" (1:18). **One refusal is read as a threat to the whole order of husbands**, and answered by decree (1:19–20).
  - The king of Syria, whose plans Elisha keeps disclosing: "the mind of the king of Syria was greatly troubled because of this thing" (2Ki 6:11). He suspects a traitor: "Will you not show me who of us is for the king of Israel?" (6:11). The servant's answer shows how completely he is exposed: Elisha "tells the king of Israel the words that you speak in your bedroom" (6:12).
- **Where it comes from, across the strands.** In every case the resistance comes from **someone who stands under God or for God**: God himself (Rachel's closed womb), the angel (Balaam), God's word (Balak), the prophet (Naaman, the king of Syria, Asa), or the priests (Uzziah). Two exceptions: the discharged army (strand b) and Vashti (strand d).
- **How it co-exists.**
  - **With a prior high state:** Uzziah "when he was strong, he grew proud" (2Ch 26:16), and the king's "heart … merry with wine" (Est 1:10). **Anger comes where a lifted state is checked.** F v4 §7 already read Est 1:10 as "the merry heart as the threshold"; this adds Uzziah.
  - **With striking** (Num 22:27; 24:10, hands; 2Ch 16:10), and with going away (2Ki 5:12; 2Ch 25:10).
- **What it leads to.**
  - Violence against the obstacle: the donkey, the seer in stocks, cities raided.
  - Or **being turned round by the lowly.** The donkey speaks (Num 22:28). Naaman's servants say "My father … will you not do it?" (2Ki 5:13), and he is restored.
  - Or the angry one's own **body struck** (Uzziah, Asa).
- **Seat.**
  - **In the verse:** 2Ki 6:11 "mind" (*lev*). **The bearer's own. Genuine.** A v3 §2.1 read it as "the mind [heart] of the king … troubled [*sāʿar*, storm]". H v1 §1.1 read it as "the most powerful heart is the least its own". Both stand. **New here:** in this phenomenon the heart's storm is **the anger of exposure**. What troubles it is that its bedroom words are known (6:12).
  - **Outside the verse:**
    - **Before:**
      - 2Ch 16:9 "those whose heart is blameless toward him". **Genuine.** It is the seer's diagnosis just before Asa's rage (G v3 §3.4; C v3 §5.1). **The rage is at being told where his heart failed.**
      - 2Ch 26:16 "grew proud" (*gāvah lēv*, the heart high). **Genuine. The bearer's own**, and it comes before the anger (G v3 §9.1; D v2 §9.1).
      - Est 1:10 "heart … merry". **Genuine** (F v4 §7).
    - **Beside:** Num 24:13, Balaam: "to do either good or bad of my own will [*heart*]". **Genuine**, but **the other party's**. Balak's anger meets a heart **subordinated to the word** (D v2 §2.6).
    - 2Ki 5:10, 14 "flesh" is Naaman's skin restored. **Spurious** as a seat (D v2 R3).
- **(v2) Part 2 verses: what they add.** The four strands hold, and each gains an instance.
  - **Strand (a), a demand no one can meet, with the angry one now the demander.** "There is not a man on earth who can meet the king's demand … no one can show it to the king except the gods, whose dwelling is not with flesh" (Dan 2:10–11). Then "Because of this the king was angry and very furious, and commanded that all the wise men of Babylon be destroyed" (2:12). **This is the mirror of Gen 30:2.** Jacob, faced with an impossible demand, named God as the only one who could meet it. Nebuchadnezzar hears the same answer ("except the gods") and orders a massacre.
    - The anger is answered by "prudence and discretion" (Dan 2:14): Daniel's.
  - **Strand (b), thwarted and made a fool of.** "Herod, when he saw that he had been tricked by the wise men, became furious, and he sent and killed all the male children" (Mat 2:16). **"Tricked" is Balaam's "you have made a fool of me"** (Num 22:29). This time the killing is carried out, and it ends in "Rachel weeping for her children; she refused to be comforted" (Mat 2:18).
  - **Strand (d), refused by subordinates.**
    - Nebuchadnezzar "in furious rage" at the three who "do not serve your gods" (Dan 3:12–13).
    - After their answer, "But if not … we will not serve your gods" (3:18), "Nebuchadnezzar was filled with fury, and **the expression of his face was changed** against Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego. He ordered the furnace heated seven times more" (3:19).
    - **The face changes outward, against them.** This is the reverse of §1's fallen face, which turns inward.
    - The outcome reverses the anger: "Blessed be the God of Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego … who trusted in him, and set aside the king's command" (3:28). **The king blesses the very refusal that enraged him.**
  - **A new instance, thwarted from outside and turned against the weaker:** "ships of Kittim shall come against him, and he shall be afraid and withdraw, and shall turn back and be enraged and take action against the holy covenant" (Dan 11:30). "News from the east and the north shall alarm him, and he shall go out with great fury to destroy" (11:44). **Fear comes first, then anger** (compare §13's anger then fear). The anger is turned on a weaker target than the one that thwarted him.
- **Seat (v2).** No in-verse seat in the six.
  - **Outside the verse:** Dan 11:28 "his heart shall be set against the holy covenant". **Genuine. The bearer's own, two verses before the rage** (D v2 §7.4; E v2 §7.1; M20 v3 §12 Seat). **The heart was set before the thwarting. The anger only releases it.**
  - Dan 2:11 "not with flesh". **Spurious** as a seat: flesh as the human realm.

## 5. The anger of the one with power, and those beneath it (PW) — 23 verses

Verses: Gen 31:35; 39:19; 40:2; 41:10; 44:18; Judg 9:30; 1Sa 20:7; 29:4; 2Sa 11:20; Est 2:1; 2:21; 7:7; 7:10 · (v2) Pro 14:35; 19:12; 20:2; 22:8; Isa 14:6; Jer 37:15; Dan 6:14; 8:6; 11:20; Act 12:20.

- **What it is.** The anger of a master, king or commander, which **decides other people's fate**, and the **inner work of those beneath it**: reading it, pleading against it, anticipating it, waiting it out.
- **What it does to the one who holds it.** It turns at once into custody or death:
  - Potiphar, hearing his wife's story, "his anger was kindled", and "Joseph's master took him and put him into the prison" (Gen 39:19–20)
  - "Pharaoh was angry with his two officers … and he put them in custody" (Gen 40:2–3; recalled at 41:10)
  - the king "arose in his wrath" and says "Hang him on that" (Est 7:7, 10)

  The pilot's §1.3 point, "no interval in which a seat would be named", holds here.
- **What it does to those beneath it:**
  - **Pleading against it before it rises:**
    - Judah: "let not your anger burn against your servant, for you are like Pharaoh himself" (Gen 44:18). The plea is a long retelling of the father's love (44:19–21 onward).
    - Rachel: "Let not my lord be angry that I cannot rise before you, for the way of women is upon me" (Gen 31:35). **The plea for no anger covers a concealment:** she is sitting on the stolen gods (31:34).
  - **Reading it as a sign:** "If he says, 'Good!' it will be well with your servant, but if he is angry, then know that harm is determined by him" (1Sa 20:7). **Saul's anger is to be read as the evidence of Saul's intent.** Jonathan's knowledge follows the same path: after the spear, "Jonathan knew that his father was determined to put David to death" (20:33).
  - **Anticipating it and scripting a reply:** Joab to the messenger: "if the king's anger rises, and if he says to you, 'Why did you go so near the city?' … then you shall say, 'Your servant Uriah the Hittite is dead also'" (2Sa 11:20–21). **The anger is foreseen, and so is the one sentence that will turn it.** That sentence works because the king had wanted Uriah dead. The line manages the king's anger by exposing the king's own secret.
  - **Plotting against it from below:** "Bigthan and Teresh … became angry and sought to lay hands on King Ahasuerus" (Est 2:21). This is anger rising **from** the servants, discovered through Mordecai (2:22).
  - **Anger acting covertly:** Zebul, "when [he] heard the words of Gaal … his anger was kindled. And he sent messengers to Abimelech secretly" (Judg 9:30–31). **Anger turned into an ambush plan**, "set an ambush in the field" (9:32).
  - **A group overrules its king:** "the commanders of the Philistines were angry with him … 'Send the man back … how could this fellow reconcile himself to his lord?'" (1Sa 29:4). The king Achish yields: "Nevertheless, the lords do not approve of you" (29:6). **The anger comes from suspicion**: they fear a loyalty they cannot see.
- **Where it comes from.** A report heard (Gen 39:19; Judg 9:30), an offence by servants (Gen 40:1–2), or suspicion (1Sa 29:4). **In Gen 39 the anger is kindled by a lie**, and the text lets the reader see that the anger is misdirected.
- **How it co-exists.** **With abating and remembering:** "when the anger of King Ahasuerus had abated, he remembered Vashti and what she had done and what had been decreed against her" (Est 2:1), and "Then the wrath of the king abated" (7:10).
  - **Anger's time-course (§3) shows in the powerful as abating after the act, not before.**
  - **Memory returns once the anger is gone**, and the decree cannot be undone.
- **What it leads to.** Imprisonment (Joseph, the officers), hanging (the baker, Haman, the eunuchs), a friend sent away (David from the Philistines).
  - **In Joseph's story the anger that imprisons also positions.** "The Lord was with Joseph" in the prison (Gen 39:21), and the officers imprisoned by Pharaoh's anger bring about Joseph's release (41:9–13).
- **Seat.**
  - **In the verse:** Est 7:7 "life" (*nephesh*): "Haman stayed to beg for his life". **The other party's. Life-sense only.** C v3 §7.5 read it under "the soul-life hunted". In this phenomenon the seat word names **what the powerful one's anger puts at stake in the one beneath**, as M20 §2's Seat found for the hunted.
  - **Outside the verse:**
    - Est 7:5 "dared" (literally "whose **heart has filled him** to do this"). **The offender's heart**, as the king asks about it. **Genuine. New here** (no batch cites it). The king's wrath two verses later is aimed at the heart that dared.
    - 1Sa 20:4 "Whatever you say [*your soul says*], I will do for you". **Genuine. The other party's** (David's), pledged to by Jonathan. **New here.** It stands three verses before 1Sa 20:7: **Jonathan binds himself to David's soul just before they plan how to read Saul's anger.** This adds to F v4 §1.1 (Jonathan and David) without changing it.
    - Gen 41:8 "his spirit was troubled". **Genuine. The bearer's own** (Pharaoh's), though in a different scene from his anger (A v3 §3.2; B v3 §7). **The same king appears once with an angry act and once with a troubled spirit.** The seat word comes with the trouble, not with the anger.
- **(v2) Part 2 verses: what they add.**
  - **The narratives become sayings.** Proverbs states as general truths what Genesis and Esther showed:
    - "A king's wrath is like the growling of a lion, but his favor is like dew on the grass" (Pro 19:12)
    - "The terror of a king is like the growling of a lion; whoever provokes him to anger forfeits his life" (20:2)
    - "A servant who deals wisely has the king's favor, but his wrath falls on one who acts shamefully" (14:35)

    **The lion's growl is the image. The subject's life is what is at stake**, as it was for Haman (Est 7:7).
  - **Anger kindled by a false charge, again:** "the officials were enraged at Jeremiah, and they beat him and imprisoned him", after the sentry's "You are deserting to the Chaldeans" and Jeremiah's "It is a lie" (Jer 37:13–15). **This is Potiphar's pattern** (Gen 39:19). The text lets the reader know the charge is false, and the anger acts on it anyway. The next verse turns: "King Zedekiah sent for him … questioned him secretly … 'Is there any word from the Lord?'" (37:17). **The one who holds power seeks secretly what his officials punished openly.**
  - **The powerful one trapped by his own word:** "the king, when he heard these words, **was much distressed** and set his mind to deliver Daniel. And he labored till the sun went down to rescue him" (Dan 6:14). The law "cannot be changed" (6:15). **Here the powerful one's displeasure is at his own decree**, and it turns into effort to save the one it condemns. This is the one PW case where the displeasure works **for** the one beneath.
  - **Those beneath reach it through an intermediary:** "Herod was angry with the people of Tyre and Sidon, and they came to him with one accord, and **having persuaded Blastus, the king's chamberlain, they asked for peace, because their country depended on the king's country for food**" (Act 12:20). **Dependence governs how the anger is approached.** The sequel is the ruler's own fall: "an angel of the Lord struck him down, because he did not give God the glory" (12:23), after the crowd's "The voice of a god, and not of a man!" (12:22).
  - **A new strand: the oppressor's wrath, and its end.**
    - The king of Babylon "struck the peoples in wrath with unceasing blows, that ruled the nations in anger with unrelenting persecution" (Isa 14:6). The taunt begins "How the oppressor has ceased, the insolent fury ceased!" (14:4) and ends "The whole earth is at rest and quiet" (14:7).
    - "The rod of his fury will fail" (Pro 22:8).
    - In the vision, the goat "ran at him in his powerful wrath … struck the ram … but **when he was strong, the great horn was broken**" (Dan 8:6–8).
    - One is "broken, neither in anger nor in battle" (Dan 11:20).
    - **Every oppressor's wrath in these verses is shown with its end in view.** The strength that carries the wrath is what breaks. Compare Uzziah, "when he was strong, he grew proud" (2Ch 26:16, §4).
- **Seat (v2).**
  - **In the verse:** Pro 20:2 "forfeits his life" (*nephesh*). **The other party's (the provoker's). Life-sense only** (D v2 §3.2, "sin against one's own soul"; H v1 §1.4, "the king's life, the subject's life"). **This matches Est 7:7 exactly: the king's wrath puts the subject's *nephesh* at stake.**
  - **Outside the verse:** Dan 8:8 "four winds". **Spurious.**

## 6. The held grievance released (GH) — Gen 31:36; 2Sa 3:8; Job 32:2, 32:3, 32:5 · (v2) Luk 15:28

- **What it is.** Anger that **breaks out after a long restraint** and releases a store of grievance in speech.
- **What it does.** It **speaks at length**, and the speech counts years or services:
  - Jacob: "Jacob became angry and berated Laban … 'What is my offense? … These twenty years I have been with you … I bore the loss of it myself'" (Gen 31:36–39).
  - Abner: "Am I a dog's head of Judah? **To this day I keep showing steadfast love** to the house of Saul … And yet you charge me today with a fault concerning a woman" (2Sa 3:8).
  - Elihu burns with anger three times (Job 32:2, 3, 5). Then comes a long speech (32:6 onward).
- **Where it comes from.**
  - **A final provocation** on top of the store: Laban's search (31:33–35), Ish-bosheth's accusation (3:7).
  - For Elihu, **an argument that has failed**: at Job "because he justified himself rather than God", at the friends "because they had found no answer, although they had declared Job to be in the wrong" (32:2–3).
- **How it co-exists.**
  - **With enforced silence first.** "Elihu had waited to speak to Job because they were older than he" (32:4), and "I was timid and afraid to declare my opinion" (32:6). **Fear and deference hold the anger in, until the others fall silent.**
  - **Elihu describes the pressure in bodily terms:** "I am full of words; the spirit within me constrains me. Behold, my belly is like wine that has no vent; like new wineskins ready to burst. I must speak, that I may find relief" (32:18–20). **The held anger is felt as pressure in the belly, and speech is its release.**
- **What it leads to.**
  - Abner: a sworn switch of allegiance, "God do so to Abner … if I do not accomplish for David what the Lord has sworn" (2Sa 3:9). The one who charged him is silenced: "Ish-bosheth could not answer Abner another word, because he feared him" (3:11).
  - Jacob: a demand for judgment, "that they may decide between us two" (Gen 31:37).
  - Elihu: six chapters of speech.
- **Seat.**
  - **In the verse:** Job 32:2 "himself" (*nephesh*): Job "justified himself rather than God". **Genuine, as the reflexive self.** But it is **the other party's** (Job's), not Elihu's. **The anger is at a self that has made itself right** (H v1 §6.2, "the heart as judge of the self").
  - **Outside the verse:**
    - **After**, Job 32:8: "it is the spirit in man, the breath of the Almighty, that makes him understand". **Genuine** (B v3 §1.6). Elihu justifies speaking despite his youth.
    - **Further on**, Job 32:18: "the spirit within me constrains me". **Genuine, the bearer's own. New here** (outside the ±3 window; read in this session). **The seat of Elihu's released anger arrives as a spirit under pressure, with the belly as its bodily image.**
- **(v2) Luk 15:28: the elder brother.** "But he was angry and refused to go in. His father came out and entreated him, but he answered his father, '**Look, these many years I have served you**, and I never disobeyed your command, yet you never gave me a young goat …'" (15:28–29).
  - **The phenomenon's shape holds exactly.** A final provocation (the feast for the returned brother, 15:27) is laid on a store that is counted in years ("these many years"), just like Jacob's "these twenty years" and Abner's "to this day". **Three texts, three speakers, one form: the anger releases a ledger.**
  - **What the parable adds is the answer.** Jacob asked for judgment, and Abner switched sides. The father **goes out and entreats**, then reframes the ledger: "Son, you are always with me, and all that is mine is yours. It was fitting to celebrate … for **this your brother** was dead, and is alive" (15:31–32). The son had said "**this son of yours**" (15:30). **The father gives the brother back to him.** This is the reframing of §9 (Joseph's "not you … but God"), used on held anger. The parable does not say whether the son goes in.
  - **The body refuses** ("refused to go in"). This links to §1's fallen face (pilot §1.3).
  - **Seat:** none in-verse or ±2. As the pilot noted (§1.3), the inner state was **held behind service** and comes out through anger.

## 7. Indignation at an outrage done to another (OU) — Gen 34:7; Exo 11:8; 1Sa 20:34; 2Sa 12:5; 13:21; Neh 5:6 · (v2) Psa 119:53; Act 16:18; 17:16

- **What it is.** Anger **on behalf of someone else** who has been wronged: a sister, a friend, the poor, a people.
- **What it does.**
  - "The men were indignant and very angry, because he had done an outrageous thing in Israel … for such a thing must not be done" (Gen 34:7). **The anger states a norm.**
  - "Jonathan rose from the table in fierce anger and ate no food … **for he was grieved for David**, because his father had disgraced him" (1Sa 20:34).
  - "David's anger was greatly kindled against the man … 'the man who has done this deserves to die'" (2Sa 12:5).
  - "I was very angry when I heard their outcry and these words" (Neh 5:6).
  - "He went out from Pharaoh in hot anger" (Exo 11:8).
- **Where it comes from.** **Hearing or seeing the wrong:**
  - "as soon as they heard of it" (Gen 34:7)
  - "when I heard their outcry" (Neh 5:6)
  - "When King David heard of all these things" (2Sa 13:21)
  - Jonathan seeing the spear thrown at himself (1Sa 20:33)
  - David hearing a parable (2Sa 12:1–4)
- **How it co-exists.**
  - **With grief:** Jonathan's anger is joined to being "grieved for David" (1Sa 20:34). Pilot §1.4 noted "anger, grief and fasting together".
  - **With deliberation:** Neh 5:6 → 5:7, "I took counsel with myself" (pilot §2.1).
  - **With a hidden self-accusation:** David's verdict on the rich man is turned on himself: "You are the man!" (2Sa 12:7). **Indignation at another's outrage becomes the instrument of the angry man's own conviction.**
  - **With inaction:** 2Sa 13:21 says only "he was very angry". Nothing follows from David, and Absalom acts instead. "Absalom hated Amnon" (13:22). "After two full years" (13:23) he orders, "Mark when Amnon's heart is merry with wine … then kill him" (13:28). **The verse sets the king's anger that does nothing beside the brother's hatred that waits.** Read further on (2Sa 13:39): "the spirit of the king longed to go out to Absalom, because he was comforted about Amnon, since he was dead." **The king's anger passes into longing for the killer.**
- **What it leads to.**
  - Public action: Nehemiah's "great assembly" and charge (Neh 5:7–9).
  - Vengeance with deceit: the brothers "answered Shechem … deceitfully" (Gen 34:13), and "Simeon and Levi … took their swords and came against the city while it felt secure and killed all the males" (34:25). This is the deed Jacob's curse names in §10.
  - Self-conviction: David.
  - Nothing: David at 13:21.
  - Fasting: Jonathan.
- **Seat.**
  - **In the verse:** none of the six.
  - **Outside the verse:**
    - Neh 5:7 "I took counsel with myself [*my heart*]". **Genuine. The bearer's own. It arrives in the next verse, with the deliberation** (pilot §2.1; B v3 §7; E v2 §7.4; C v3 §5.4).
    - Gen 34:8 "The soul of my son Shechem longs for your daughter". **Genuine, but the offender's** (C v3 §1.1). **The indignation of the brothers and the desire of the offender's soul stand side by side in consecutive verses.**
    - Exo 11:10 "the Lord hardened Pharaoh's heart". **Genuine. The offender's heart, hardened. New here** (no batch cites it). Moses's hot anger (11:8) is set between God's announcement and the heart that will not yield.
    - 2Sa 13:20 "do not take this to heart" (Absalom to Tamar). **Genuine. The victim's, told to suppress** (B v3 §0; E v2 R7). 2Sa 13:39 "spirit of the king". **Genuine. The bearer's own**, the angry king's spirit after the killing, turned to longing (G v3 §16.6; H v1 §1.3, "the Absalom arc"). **New here is only the link back to the anger at 13:21.**
    - 2Sa 12:3, 8 "arms" (*chêq*, bosom): the lamb "lie in his arms", and "your master's wives into your arms". **Relational and bodily, not inner** (D v2 R6; E v2 R8). The parable's bosom and David's bosom are the same word: **that is how the parable catches him.** This is noted as a link, not as a seat.
    - Neh 5:5 "our flesh is as the flesh of our brothers" is **kinship-sense.** **Spurious** as a seat (H v1 §1.10 fringe).
- **(v2) Part 2 verses: what they add.** The one wronged is now **God, or God's law**, rather than a sister, a friend or the poor.
  - **The indignation seizes its bearer:** "Hot indignation **seizes me** because of the wicked, who forsake your law" (Psa 119:53). **The bearer is its object as well as its subject.** It is framed between comfort in God's rules (119:52) and "Your statutes have been my songs" (119:54). **The indignation sits inside a life held by the law it defends.**
  - **The spirit provoked by what it sees:** "while Paul was waiting … **his spirit was provoked within him as he saw that the city was full of idols**. So he reasoned in the synagogue … and in the marketplace every day" (Act 17:16–17). **This is the same movement as Neh 5:6–7: indignation, then an inner act, then public speech.** Here the seat is named in the indignation verse itself.
  - **Annoyance that finally acts:** the slave girl "kept doing this for many days. Paul, having become greatly annoyed, turned and said to the spirit, 'I command you … come out of her'" (Act 16:18).
    - What annoys him is a **true** proclamation ("These men are servants of the Most High God", 16:17) from a wrong source.
    - The anger builds over "many days" before the act (§6's shape, on a small scale).
    - The act sets off anger in others: "when her owners saw that their hope of gain was gone, they seized Paul and Silas" (16:19).
- **Seat (v2).**
  - Act 17:16 "spirit" (*pneuma*, "within him"). **The bearer's own. Genuine** (A v3 §2.1; G v3 §9.4, "the spirit reacts to idols"; C v3 §6.5). Nothing new is needed.
  - Act 16:18 "spirit" is **the spirit of divination addressed**, not Paul's. **Spurious for the bearer. The other party's (demonic)** (compare M20 v3's Rev 2:17 row). **New here**, and not in any batch.

## 8. Vexation suffered: the provoked one (VX) — Gen 40:6; 1Sa 1:6, 1:7, 1:16; Job 6:2; 17:7 · (v2) Psa 6:7; 10:14; 31:9; Pro 17:25; Ecc 1:18; 2:23; 5:17; 7:3; 11:10; Eph 6:4; Col 3:21

- **What it is.** Here the M02 word names **what is done to a person, or what a person bears**, not the anger they show. It is *ka'as* as the sufferer's vexation, and *za'aph* as a downcast state.
- **What it does.**
  - It **wears down over time**: "her rival used to provoke her grievously to irritate her … So it went on year by year" (1Sa 1:6–7).
  - It **stops eating and brings weeping**: "Therefore Hannah wept and would not eat" (1:7).
  - It **dims the eye and wastes the body**: "My eye has grown dim from vexation, and all my members are like a shadow" (Job 17:7).
  - It **cannot be weighed**: "Oh that my vexation were weighed … it would be heavier than the sand of the sea; therefore my words have been rash" (Job 6:2–3).
  - It **shows in the face**: Joseph "saw that they were troubled", and asks "Why are your faces downcast today?" (Gen 40:6–7).
- **Where it comes from.**
  - A rival's provocation, "because the Lord had closed her womb" (1Sa 1:6)
  - calamity and God's "arrows" (Job 6:2, 4)
  - being made "a byword of the peoples" (Job 17:6)
  - dreams with "no one to interpret them" (Gen 40:8)
- **How it co-exists.**
  - **With the sufferer's own speech that explains it.** Hannah: "I am a woman troubled in spirit … I have been pouring out my soul before the Lord … I have been speaking out of my great anxiety and vexation" (1Sa 1:15–16). Job: "therefore my words have been rash" (Job 6:3). **The vexation is given as the reason the sufferer's speech looks the way it does**: drunk to Eli, rash to the friends.
  - **With misreading by the onlooker:** Eli takes Hannah "to be a drunken woman" (1:13–14).
- **What it leads to.**
  - Resolution through a word given: Eli's "Go in peace", and then "the woman went her way and ate, and her face was no longer sad" (1Sa 1:17–18). **The body's signs reverse**: she eats, and her face changes.
  - Or a plea to be allowed to die: "that it would please God to crush me, that he would let loose his hand and cut me off!" (Job 6:9).
  - Or interpretation: Joseph's "Please tell them to me" (Gen 40:8).
- **Seat.**
  - **In the verse:** none of the six.
  - **Outside the verse**, dense in 1Sa 1:
    - 1Sa 1:8 "why is your heart sad?". **Genuine. The bearer's own, named by the husband. New here** (no batch cites it).
    - 1Sa 1:10 "deeply distressed [*bitter of soul*]" (A v3 §3.1; E v2 §1.1).
    - 1Sa 1:13 "speaking in her heart" (B v3 §3.4; E v2 §1.1; G v3 §8.2).
    - 1Sa 1:15 "troubled in spirit … pouring out my soul" (pilot §2.1, §3).

    **All genuine. All the bearer's own.** The sequence runs: **named by another (1:8) → the narrator (1:10) → the heart as the place of voiceless prayer (1:13) → named by herself (1:15).** The M02 word comes last (1:16), in her own summary.
  - Job 6:4 "my spirit drinks their poison". **Genuine. The bearer's own, as receiver** (A v3 §1; pilot §2). The vexation of 6:2 is located in the next-but-one verse as a spirit poisoned by God's arrows. That verse is unit 2's (divine), and is cited here only as the seat of Job's vexation.
  - Job 6:11 "that I should be patient" (literally *lengthen my nephesh*). **Genuine. The bearer's own** (G v3 §3.5; C v3 §5.2). **New here is only the pairing with vexation: vexation shortens the soul.** Compare "slow to anger" = "long of nostrils" (pilot §2.2).
  - Job 17:4 "you have closed their hearts to understanding". **Genuine, but the friends'** (B v3 §1.3).
- **(v2) Part 2 verses: what they add.** Three new strands.
  - **(a) The eye wasting, as in Job 17:7.**
    - "My eye wastes away because of grief; it grows weak because of all my foes" (Psa 6:7), after "every night I flood my bed with tears" (6:6).
    - "My eye is wasted from grief; my soul and my body also. For my life is spent with sorrow" (Psa 31:9–10).
    - **The eye is where *ka'as* shows in the body, in all three texts** (Job 17:7; Psa 6:7; 31:9).
    - **What turns Psa 6 round is being heard:** "the Lord has heard the sound of my weeping. The Lord has heard my plea" (6:8–9). In Psa 10:14, "you do see, for you note mischief and vexation, that you may take it into your hands; to you the helpless commits himself". **The vexation is seen by God, and the helpless hands it over.**
  - **(b) Provocation from those closest.**
    - "A foolish son is a grief to his father and bitterness to her who bore him" (Pro 17:25).
    - The reverse direction: "Fathers, do not provoke your children to anger" (Eph 6:4), and "do not provoke your children, **lest they become discouraged**" (Col 3:21).
    - **Vexation passes both ways between parent and child.** Col 3:21 names its outcome as losing heart (M20 v3 §12, pilot §1.3). Hannah's rival (1Sa 1:6) was a co-wife. These are the family members.
  - **(c) Qoheleth's vexation: the vexation of the thinker and the worker.**
    - **Knowing brings it:** "in much wisdom is much vexation, and he who increases knowledge increases sorrow" (Ecc 1:18). This comes after "I applied my heart to know wisdom … this also is but a striving after wind" (1:17).
    - **Work brings it, and it keeps the heart awake:** "his work is a vexation. **Even in the night his heart does not rest**" (2:23); "all his days he eats in darkness in much vexation and sickness and anger" (5:17).
    - **Its answer is enjoyment received as God's gift:** "There is nothing better … than that he should eat and drink and find enjoyment in his toil. This also, I saw, is from the hand of God" (2:24), and "this is the gift of God" (5:19).
    - **It is to be removed:** "Remove vexation from your heart, and put away pain from your body" (11:10), after "Rejoice, O young man … let your heart cheer you" (11:9).
    - **It is sometimes better than laughter:** "Sorrow [*ka'as*] is better than laughter, for by sadness of face the heart is made glad" (7:3). **The one place where *ka'as* is valued.** The face is sad and the heart made good. Face and heart go in opposite directions.
- **Seat (v2).** Four in-verse, all genuine and all the bearer's own:
  - Psa 31:9 "soul" (E v2 §10.1)

## 9. Anger turned on oneself (SS) — Gen 45:5; Job 18:4

- **What it is.** Anger whose object is **the self**.
- **What it does.**
  - Joseph to his brothers: "do not be distressed or angry **with yourselves** because you sold me here" (Gen 45:5). **The brothers' self-directed anger is expected**, and Joseph forbids it.
  - Bildad to Job: "You who **tear yourself** in your anger" (Job 18:4). **Anger that wounds its bearer.**
- **Where it comes from.**
  - Joseph's brothers: guilt, now uncovered. "They were dismayed at his presence" (45:3), and "I am your brother, Joseph, whom you sold" (45:4).
  - Job: his protest, as Bildad hears it.
- **How it co-exists.** **With a reframing that removes its ground.** "For God sent me before you to preserve life … So it was not you who sent me here, but God" (45:5, 8).
  - Joseph does not deny the act ("you sold me"). He **re-assigns its meaning**, and the anger at oneself is released by the new meaning.
  - Bildad offers the opposite of a reframing: the world will not change for Job, "shall the earth be forsaken for you, or the rock be removed?" (18:4).
- **What it leads to.** Joseph: reconciliation, and a summons to the father (45:9). Job: Bildad goes on to the fate of the wicked (18:5–7).
- **Seat.**
  - **In the verse:** Job 18:4 "yourself" (*nephesh*). **The bearer's own. Genuine, as the self that anger harms** (A v3 §2.1; pilot §2).
  - Gen 45:5 "with yourselves" is **literally "in your eyes"** [lexical, not in DB]. It is not tagged as a seat in the DB. [verify] if the researcher wants the Hebrew checked.
  - **Outside the verse:** no seat in Gen 45:2–9 or Job 18:1–7.

## 10. Anger cursed, its company refused (CC) — Gen 49:6, 49:7 · (v2) Pro 22:24

- **What it is.** A **judgment on anger by a third party**: Jacob on his sons Simeon and Levi.
- **What it does.**
  - **It separates the speaker's self from the angry:** "Let my soul come not into their council; O my glory, be not joined to their company" (49:6).
  - **It curses the anger, not the men:** "Cursed be their anger, for it is fierce, and their wrath, for it is cruel!" (49:7).

- **How it co-exists.** **With violence named as the brothers' very identity:** "weapons of violence are their swords" (49:5).
- **What it leads to.** Scattering: "I will divide them in Jacob and scatter them in Israel" (49:7).
- **Seat.**
  - **In the verse:** Gen 49:6 "soul" (*nephesh*), and "glory" (*kāvôd*) in parallel. **Genuine. The speaker's own**, not the angry ones'. **The seat is the self that withdraws from the anger** (A v3 §2.1, "Soul withdrawing from others' anger"; E v2 §3.2, *kāvôd* = the self; E v2 §7.3, fellowship).
  - **Outside the verse:** none in 49:3–10.
- **(v2) Pro 22:24–25: the same refusal as teaching.** "Make no friendship with a man given to anger, nor go with a wrathful man, **lest you learn his ways and entangle yourself in a snare**."

  - **The reason is stated: anger is learned from company**, and what it catches is the self.
  - **Seat.** Pro 22:25 "yourself" (*nephesh*). **Genuine. The learner's own self, as what the snare catches.** It is not in any batch: **new**.

## 11. The leader's anger at those he leads (LD) — Exo 16:20; 32:19; 32:22; Lev 10:16; Num 16:15; 31:14; 2Ki 13:19

- **What it is.** Anger in the one responsible for a people, a priesthood or a king, **at their failure to do what was commanded**.
- **What it does.**
  - It **flares at the sight**: "as soon as he came near the camp and saw the calf and the dancing, Moses' anger burned hot" (Exo 32:19).
  - It **breaks what was given**: "he threw the tablets … and broke them" (32:19).
  - It **destroys the object**: the calf "burned … ground it to powder … made the people of Israel drink it" (32:20).
  - It **rebukes by question**: "Why have you not eaten the sin offering …?" (Lev 10:17); "Have you let all the women live?" (Num 31:15).
  - It **states the limit that was missed**: "You should have struck five or six times … now you will strike down Syria only three times" (2Ki 13:19).
- **Where it comes from.** Disobedience to a given word:
  - "they did not listen to Moses" (Exo 16:20)
  - the calf (32:19)
  - the sin offering burned instead of eaten (Lev 10:16)
  - the women of Midian spared "on Balaam's advice" (Num 31:14–16)
  - the king striking only three times (2Ki 13:18)
  - rebels refusing to come: "We will not come up" (Num 16:12–14)
- **How it co-exists.**
  - **With appeal to God** against those who resist: "Moses was very angry and said to the Lord, 'Do not respect their offering. I have not taken one donkey from them'" (Num 16:15). **The anger is carried straight into a plea of innocence before God.**
  - **With the one beneath deflecting it:** Aaron, "Let not the anger of my lord burn hot. You know the people, that they are set on evil … I threw it into the fire, and out came this calf" (Exo 32:22–24). **Deflection by blaming the people and making the act passive.**
  - **With being answered:** Aaron's reply to the Lev 10 rebuke ("such things as these have happened to me! If I had eaten the sin offering today, would the Lord have approved?", 10:19) meets "And when Moses heard that, he approved" (10:20). **The leader's anger yields to a reason.**
- **What it leads to.** Destruction of the object (Exo 32:20), purge and execution (Num 31:17), a lesser victory (2Ki 13:19), or **approval once the explanation is heard** (Lev 10:20).
- **Seat.** None in the seven verses or their ±3 context. **The leader's anger is carried by the act and the question.** Pilot §3 items 1 and 3 read this as act and speech.

## 12. Quarrelling with God: Meribah (MB) — Exo 17:7; Num 20:13, 20:24; 27:14; Deu 32:51 · (v2) Psa 95:8

## 13. Anger at what God has done (AG) — Num 11:10; 1Sa 15:11; 2Sa 6:8; 1Ch 13:11 · (v2) Pro 19:3; Isa 8:21; Jon 4:1, 4:2, 4:4, 4:9

- **What it is.** A human's anger or displeasure **whose occasion is an act or decision of God**.
- **What it does.**
  - "David was angry **because the Lord had broken out against Uzzah**" (2Sa 6:8 = 1Ch 13:11).
  - "the anger of the Lord blazed hotly, and Moses was displeased" (Num 11:10).
  - "'I regret that I have made Saul king' … And Samuel was angry, and he cried to the Lord all night" (1Sa 15:11).
- **Where it comes from.** From God's anger or regret **falling on something the human was carrying**: the ark procession, the people Moses must bear, the king Samuel anointed.
- **How it co-exists.** **With fear, straight after:** "David was afraid of the Lord that day, and he said, 'How can the ark of the Lord come to me?'" (2Sa 6:9; 1Ch 13:12). **Anger at God and fear of God follow one another in consecutive verses.** A new pairing, not read in the batches.
  - **With complaint to God:** Moses, "Why have you dealt ill with your servant? … Did I conceive all this people? … 'Carry them in your bosom'" (Num 11:11–12).
  - **With all-night crying:** Samuel (1Sa 15:11). Later, Samuel "grieved over Saul" (15:35). **Anger turns to crying out, and then to grief.**
- **What it leads to.**
  - **Withdrawal from what God struck:** David will not take the ark home (2Sa 6:10). **The anger ends in avoidance**, and the ark's blessing goes to Obed-edom instead (6:11).
  - **A burden shared:** Moses (H v1 §3.3, "Moses' burden, then the Spirit shared", Num 11:10–17).
  - **Obedience with grief:** Samuel goes to meet Saul the next morning (15:12).
- **Seat.**
  - **In the verse:** none.
  - **Outside the verse:** Num 11:12 "Carry them in your bosom, as a nurse carries a nursing child". **The bearer's own, relational and bodily.** Moses's displeasure (11:10) comes out as a protest about **the bosom he is asked to carry the people in** (E v2 data note). Num 11:13 "meat" is **spurious**.
  - **The unit-2 side** of these four verses is God's own anger (Num 11:10; 2Sa 6:7; 1Ch 13:10), for unit 2.
- **(v2) Part 2 verses: what they add.** The occasion widens from God **striking** to God's **mercy**, and to the consequences of one's own folly.
  - **Jonah: anger at God's mercy.**
    - The occasion: "When God saw what they did, how they turned from their evil way, God relented of the disaster" (Jon 3:10). "But it displeased Jonah exceedingly, and he was angry" (4:1).
    - **He quotes God's own character against him:** "I knew that you are a gracious God and merciful, slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love, and relenting from disaster" (4:2). Pilot §2.1 read this. **The anger is at the very attribute that is the opposite of anger.**
    - **It asks to die:** "please take my life from me, for it is better for me to die than to live" (4:3). After the plant withers, "It is better for me to die than to live" (4:8), and "Yes, I do well to be angry, angry enough to die" (4:9).
    - **God asks a question, as he did with Cain:** "Do you do well to be angry?" (4:4, 9). Compare "Why are you angry?" (Gen 4:6). **Twice in Scripture God meets a man's anger by asking about it.** Both times, the question comes before a decisive act or teaching, and both times the answer is left open.
    - **God reframes by comparison:** "You pity the plant … And should not I pity Nineveh …?" (4:10–11). **The anger is set beside the pity the angry man feels for a plant.** The book ends on God's question. Jonah's answer is not given.
  - **The heart rages at God for one's own ruin:** "When a man's folly brings his way to ruin, **his heart rages against the Lord**" (Pro 19:3). **The anger is misdirected**: the cause is his own folly, and the target is God.
  - **Deprivation turns to cursing God, then darkness:** "when they are hungry, they will be enraged and will speak contemptuously against their king and their God, and turn their faces upward. And they will look to the earth, but behold, distress and darkness" (Isa 8:21–22). **The face turns up in anger and finds nothing, then down and finds darkness.** It is set after "should not a people inquire of their God?" (8:19).
- **Seat (v2).**
  - **In the verse:** Pro 19:3 "heart" (*lev*). **The bearer's own. Genuine** (A v3 §2.1; H v1 §4.1, "heart and way, which leads?"; D v2 §5). **New here:** the only in-verse seat in §13 locates anger **at God** in the heart, and the verse says it is **misdirected**.
  - **Outside the verse:** Jon 4:3 "my life" and Jon 4:8 "that he might die" (*nephesh*). **The bearer's own. Life-sense, as the thing asked to be ended** (G v3 §16.2; C v3 §1.7; F v4 §3.4). **Jonah's anger takes his own *nephesh* as its object.** This links to §9: anger turned on oneself.

## 14. Who may pronounce anger (JV) — Num 23:7, 23:8; Job 19:29; 40:11 · (v2) Pro 24:24

- **What it is.** Anger as a **verdict or curse**, and the question of **who has the right to pronounce it**.
- **What it does.**
  - Balaam **cannot** pronounce it: "How can I curse whom God has not cursed? How can I denounce whom the Lord has not denounced?" (Num 23:8). **Human denunciation is shown to be derivative: it cannot go beyond God's.**
  - God **offers it ironically to Job**: "Pour out the overflowings of your anger, and look on everyone who is proud and abase him" (Job 40:11). This comes inside the challenge, "Have you an arm like God …?" (40:9), and "Then will I also acknowledge to you that your own right hand can save you" (40:14). **Anger that abases the proud belongs to the one who can save by his own right hand.**
  - Job warns his friends that **their own wrath is answerable**: "be afraid of the sword, for wrath brings the punishment of the sword, that you may know there is a judgment" (Job 19:29). This comes after "If you say, 'How we will pursue him!'" (19:28).
- **Where it comes from.**
  - For Balaam, **a hired commission**: "Come, curse Jacob for me, and come, denounce Israel!" (Num 23:7).
  - For the friends, **pursuit of a sufferer.**
- **How it co-exists.**
  - **With a turned wish:** the hired curser ends by wishing for the end of those he was hired to curse, "Let me die the death of the upright" (Num 23:10; G v3 §16.2; H v1 §6.5).
  - **With Job's own hope, just before:** "yet in my flesh I shall see God … My heart faints within me!" (Job 19:26–27).
- **What it leads to.** Blessing instead of curse (Num 23:11). The judgment turned back on the pursuers (Job 19:29).
- **Seat.**
  - **In the verse:** none.
  - **Outside the verse:**
    - Num 23:10 "Let me [*my nephesh*] die the death of the upright". **Genuine. The would-be pronouncer's own** (G v3 §16.2).
    - Job 19:27 "My heart [*kidneys*] faints within me". **Genuine. The bearer's own**, Job's, just before his warning. **New here.** Job 19:26 "in my flesh" is **bodily**, the flesh in which he will see God.
- **(v2) Pro 24:24: human denunciation rightly placed.** "Whoever says to the wicked, 'You are in the right,' will be cursed by peoples, **abhorred** [*za'am*, the Balaam word] by nations, but those who rebuke the wicked will have delight" (24:24–25). **Here the nations' denunciation is in line with justice**, so it can fall. Balaam's could not fall on those God had not denounced (Num 23:8). **The same verb is valid when it follows the right verdict and void when it goes against God's.** No seat in-verse or ±2.

## 15. Anger that comes with God's Spirit or zeal, and anger measured against God's (SP) — Num 25:11; Judg 14:19; 1Sa 11:6; 2Ch 28:9 · (v2) Psa 76:10; Jer 6:11; 15:17; Eze 3:14

- **What it is.** Human anger that **the text relates to God's own**: given by the Spirit, shared as God's jealousy, or found to exceed God's commission.
- **What it does.**
  - **Given by the Spirit:** "the Spirit of God rushed upon Saul when he heard these words, and his anger was greatly kindled" (1Sa 11:6); "the Spirit of the Lord rushed upon him … In hot anger he went back to his father's house" (Judg 14:19).
  - **Shared as God's jealousy:** Phinehas "has turned back my wrath from the people of Israel, **in that he was jealous with my jealousy among them**" (Num 25:11). **A human's zeal is counted as God's own, and it stops God's wrath.**
  - **Exceeding God's commission:** "because the Lord … was angry with Judah, he gave them into your hand, **but you have killed them in a rage that has reached up to heaven**" (2Ch 28:9). **Human rage goes beyond the divine anger it served, and becomes its own guilt:** "Have you not sins of your own …?" (28:10); "the fierce wrath of the Lord is upon you" (28:11).
- **Where it comes from.**
  - Hearing (1Sa 11:5–6: "they told him the news of the men of Jabesh").
  - Betrayal (Judg 14:16–18: the riddle given away through his wife).
  - Seeing: an Israelite brought a Midianite woman "in the sight of Moses and … the whole congregation … while they were weeping" (Num 25:6). "When Phinehas … saw it, he rose … and took a spear" (25:7), and killed them both (25:8).
  - Victory in battle (2Ch 28:6–8).
- **How it co-exists.**
  - **Directions of flow:** Batch A §2.4 read 1Sa 11:5–7 as "hearing → Spirit → anger → act → dread spreading to the people", in contrast with 1Sa 18:8–10: "jealous anger → a harmful spirit → a spear". Both stand. **New here:** Judg 14:19 runs **Spirit → killing → hot anger afterwards**. The anger is **the aftermath of a Spirit-driven act**, not its cause, and it comes out as going home, not as more violence.
  - **Saul's anger ends in mercy that same day:** "Not a man shall be put to death this day, for today the Lord has worked salvation" (1Sa 11:13).
- **What it leads to.**
  - A people united (1Sa 11:7).
  - A covenant of peace for Phinehas (Num 25:12–13).
  - **Captives released and cared for**, after the rebuke of rage: "they clothed them, gave them sandals, provided them with food and drink, and anointed them … brought them to their kinsfolk" (2Ch 28:15). **Rage beyond commission is answered by a reversal toward kin**, which links to §2.
  - Samson's withdrawal to his father's house, and the loss of his wife (Judg 14:20).
- **Seat.**
  - **In the verse:**
    - Judg 14:19 and 1Sa 11:6 "Spirit" (*ruach*). **Divine, the other party's, received by the human.** The human anger follows in the same verse (A v3 §2.4 [D1]; B v3 §3.3 for "what is heard moves the inner seat"; G v3 R-V).
    - Num 25:11 and 2Ch 28:9 have no seat.
  - **The seat-sense question these verses raise:** where the Spirit is the seat word, **the seat is God's, and the anger is the human's.** This matches M20 v3 §20's Eze 39:29 row: "God's, received by Israel: the human side is the receiving".
- **(v2) Part 2 verses: the prophet as the vessel of God's anger** (pilot §2.3), plus human wrath taken up into God's praise.
  - **Filled, and worn out by holding it:** "I am full of the wrath of the Lord; **I am weary of holding it in**" (Jer 6:11). **The cause is the hearers' closed ears:** "their ears are uncircumcised, they cannot listen … the word of the Lord is to them an object of scorn" (6:10). **The wrath fills the prophet because the word it belongs to has nowhere to go.**
  - **Filled after being fed:** "Your words were found, and I ate them, and your words became to me a joy and the delight of my heart" (Jer 15:16). Then "I sat alone, because your hand was upon me, **for you had filled me with indignation**" (15:17), and "Why is my pain unceasing, my wound incurable?" (15:18).
    - **The same words that delighted the heart fill the prophet with indignation and isolate him.** The next movement is God's call to return (15:19) and a promise: "I will make you … a fortified wall of bronze … I am with you" (15:20).
  - **Carried by the Spirit, in the heat of his own spirit:** "The Spirit lifted me up and took me away, and **I went in bitterness in the heat of my spirit, the hand of the Lord being strong upon me**. And I came to the exiles … and I sat there overwhelmed among them seven days" (Eze 3:14–15).
    - **Two spirits in one verse**: the Spirit that carries, and the prophet's own spirit, heated.
    - **The heat ends in seven days of silence among the people** before the word comes again (3:16).
  - **Across the three prophets, God's anger in the human does not come out as violence.** It comes out as weariness (Jer 6:11), isolation and pain (Jer 15:17–18), bitterness and being overwhelmed (Eze 3:14–15). **The human bearing of divine anger is suffering, not action.** Compare Samson and Saul (Judg 14:19; 1Sa 11:6), whose Spirit-linked anger acted. Those were deliverers; these are prophets.
  - **Human wrath taken up into God's praise:** "Surely the wrath of man shall praise you; the remnant of wrath you will put on like a belt" (Psa 76:10, B2). **Human wrath is neither aligned with God's nor exceeding it. It is used by God.** This is a fourth relation, beside §15's aligned (Phinehas), exceeding (2Ch 28:9) and following (Samson). The context is God "who cuts off the spirit of princes" (76:12; H v1 §1.1).
- **Seat (v2).**
  - **In the verse:** Eze 3:14 "Spirit" (divine, received) + "spirit" (**the bearer's own. Genuine**) (A v3 §2.1, §3.1).
  - **Outside the verse:** Jer 15:16 "the delight of my heart". **Genuine. The bearer's own** (A v3 §4.2; B v3 §3.3). **The heart that delighted in the words is the same person filled with indignation in the next verse.** Pilot §2.3 noted this; it is confirmed here.

## 16. Provocation among Israel, God and the nations (SM) — Deu 32:21, 32:27, 32:33 · (v2) Eze 32:9; Rom 10:19

- **What it is.** In the Song of Moses, **provocation passes in a circle**: Israel provokes God, God provokes Israel through the nations, and the enemy's provocation and venom are described.
- **What it does.**
  - Measure for measure: "They have made me jealous with what is no god; they have provoked me to anger with their idols. **So I will make them jealous with those who are no people; I will provoke them to anger with a foolish nation**" (Deu 32:21). **Human anger is induced by God as the mirror of the anger they induced in him.**
  - The enemy's taunt: "had I not feared provocation by the enemy, lest their adversaries should misunderstand, lest they should say, 'Our hand is triumphant, it was not the Lord who did all this'" (32:27). The enemy's *ka'as* is **a misreading of events**, claiming God's work as their own.
  - The enemy's nature: "their wine is the poison of serpents and the cruel venom of asps" (32:33).
- **Where it comes from.** Idolatry (32:16–21), and the enemy's lack of understanding: "a nation void of counsel, and there is no understanding in them" (32:28).
- **How it co-exists.** **With understanding withheld:** "If they were wise, they would understand this; they would discern their latter end!" (32:29).
- **What it leads to.** God's restraint because of the enemy's likely boast (32:26–27), and vengeance kept "in store" (32:34–35).
- **Seat.** None in the verses or ±3. The human inner operations here (being made jealous and angry, misunderstanding) are carried by the verbs and by "understanding" (32:28–29).
  - **Pointer:** Rom 10:19 quotes Deu 32:21 and belongs to part 2.
- **(v2) Part 2 verses: what they add** (both were B8: human vexation induced by God).
  - **Paul reads Deu 32:21 as a statement about understanding:** "But I ask, did Israel not understand? First Moses says, 'I will make you jealous of those who are not a nation; with a foolish nation I will make you angry'" (Rom 10:19). Then "I have been found by those who did not seek me" (10:20), and "All day long I have held out my hands to a disobedient and contrary people" (10:21).
    - **The induced anger is set inside God's outstretched hands.** It is a provocation meant to bring them back, not only a punishment.
    - Compare Deu 32:29, "If they were wise, they would understand this".
  - **God troubles the nations' hearts at another's fall:** "I will trouble [*ka'as*] the hearts of many peoples, when I bring your destruction among the nations" (Eze 32:9), and "They shall tremble every moment, every one for his own life" (32:10). **The vexation God induces comes from seeing a great power fall. It is a trouble of the onlooker**, and it turns to fear for one's own life.
- **Seat (v2).**
  - **In the verse:** Eze 32:9 "hearts". **The nations' own. Genuine, as the place where the induced vexation lands.** Not in any batch: **new**.
  - **Outside the verse:** Eze 32:10 "his own life". **Life-sense** (A v3 §1).

## 17. Anger at a rival's rise (RV) — 1Sa 17:28; 18:8; 20:30; Neh 4:1; 4:7; Est 3:5; 5:9 · (v2) Psa 112:10; Mat 20:24; Mar 10:41

- **What it is.** Anger at **another person's gain in standing**, or at their refusal to give honour.
- **What it does.**
  - It **reads the rival's heart as evil**: Eliab, "I know your presumption and the evil of your heart, for you have come down to see the battle" (1Sa 17:28).
  - It **reckons the rival's future**: Saul, "They have ascribed to David ten thousands, and to me they have ascribed thousands, and what more can he have but the kingdom?" (18:8).
  - It **attacks one's own son as the rival's ally**: "You son of a perverse, rebellious woman … as long as the son of Jesse lives … neither you nor your kingdom shall be established" (20:30–31).
  - It **jeers**: Sanballat "was angry and greatly enraged, and he jeered at the Jews … 'What are these feeble Jews doing?'" (Neh 4:1–2).
  - It **enlarges its target**: Haman "disdained to lay hands on Mordecai alone … sought to destroy all the Jews" (Est 3:6).
- **Where it comes from.** **Seeing or hearing the rival's rise or refusal:**
  - the women's song (1Sa 18:7)
  - David's question in the camp (17:26–28)
  - "when Sanballat heard that we were building" (Neh 4:1), and "that the breaches were beginning to be closed" (4:7)
  - "when Haman saw that Mordecai did not bow down" (Est 3:5), and "that he neither rose nor trembled before him" (5:9)
- **How it co-exists.**
  - **With surveillance:** "Saul eyed David from that day on" (1Sa 18:9).
  - **With joy that flips.** Haman "went out that day joyful and glad of heart. But when Haman saw Mordecai … he was filled with wrath" (Est 5:9). Then he goes home and recounts "the splendor of his riches" (5:11). A v3 §4.4 read this ("Haman's swing"), and H v1 §2.1 read it as "wealth does not satisfy a heart set on a grievance". Both stand.
  - **With restraint that is only postponement:** "Haman restrained himself and went home" (5:10).
  - **With plotting:** "they all plotted together to come and fight" (Neh 4:8).
- **What it leads to.**
  - The spear, twice (1Sa 18:11; 20:33).
  - A plan of genocide (Est 3:6–8).
  - Armed plotting (Neh 4:8).
  - On the other side, **prayer and a guard** (Neh 4:9), and building on regardless: "the people had a mind to work" (4:6).
- **Seat.**
  - **In the verse:**
    - 1Sa 17:28 "heart" (*levav*). **Genuine, but the rival's heart as the angry man reads it, and misread** (D v2 §2.8, §9.5: "a misreading of another's heart"). The angry man's own seat is not named.
    - Est 5:9 "heart" (*lev*). **Genuine. The bearer's own, in the state just before the anger** (A v3 §4.4; H v1 §2.1; C v3 §1.6; pilot §2.1).
  - **Outside the verse:**
    - 1Sa 18:10 "a harmful spirit from God rushed upon Saul". **The other party's (God's) sending, received by Saul, the day after the anger** (A v3 §2.4 contrast; F v4 §14).
    - Neh 4:6 "the people had a mind [*heart*] to work". **Genuine. The heart of the rival party**, the builders. **New here.** **Between the enemy's two outbursts (4:1, 4:7) stands the builders' heart set on the work.** The angry party has no seat named, and the ones they are angry at do. This fits M20 v3 §20 point 4, "the seat named in the contrast figure".
- **(v2) Part 2 verses: what they add.**
  - **The body's end of rivalrous anger:** "The wicked man sees it and is angry; he gnashes his teeth and **melts away**; the desire of the wicked will perish!" (Psa 112:10). What he sees is the righteous man, whose "horn is exalted in honor" (112:9). **Seeing the rival's rise ends in the angry one dissolving**, not in the spear of 1Sa 18.
    - **Contrast seat:** "His heart is steady; he will not be afraid" (112:8), said of the righteous one, two verses before. **The seat is again named in the one the anger is aimed at** (see Neh 4:6 above). Genuine (A v3 §1; F v4 §8.4).
  - **Rivalry among the disciples:** "when the ten heard it, they were indignant at the two brothers" (Mat 20:24 = Mar 10:41). What they heard was Jesus's answer about the two brothers' place: "to sit at my right hand and at my left is not mine to grant" (Mat 20:23; Mar 10:40).
    - **Jesus answers by redefining greatness:** "You know that the rulers of the Gentiles lord it over them … It shall not be so among you. But whoever would be great among you must be your servant" (Mat 20:25–26; Mar 10:42–43).
    - **The anger at a rival's rise is not rebuked directly. The standing it is about is re-defined**, so the rise it resented no longer counts. This is a reframing, the §9 and §6 move used on rivalry.
  - **Seat (v2):** none in-verse.

## 18. Bearer not given (UB) — 2Ki 3:27

- "Then he took his oldest son who was to reign in his place and offered him for a burnt offering on the wall. **And there came great wrath against Israel.** And they withdrew from him and returned to their own land."
- **Context (3:24–27) was read and gives no bearer.** The wrath could be divine, Moabite, or a terror in Israel. The verse does not say, and this reading does not supply one.
- **The human movement that is shown is Israel's withdrawal** after seeing the king of Moab's act. **Recorded, not interpreted.**
- **Seat:** none.

# Part 2 (v2): new phenomena from Psalms–Revelation

## 19. Strife in the community, traced to an inner source (CS) — Rom 16:17; 1Cor 1:11; 3:3; Gal 5:26; Phili 1:15; 1Ti 2:8; 6:4, 6:5; 2Ti 2:23; Tit 3:9; Jam 4:1

- **What it is.** Quarrelling, division and rivalry **among believers**, where the letter names **where it comes from inside the person**.
- **What it does.**
  - It **divides by allegiance**: "there is quarreling among you … each one of you says, 'I follow Paul,' or 'I follow Apollos'" (1Cor 1:11–12)
  - it **creates obstacles**: "those who cause divisions and create obstacles contrary to the doctrine" (Rom 16:17)
  - it **breeds**: "they breed quarrels" (2Ti 2:23)
  - it **spoils prayer**: "lifting holy hands without anger or quarreling" (1Ti 2:8)
- **Where it comes from.** **Every verse, or the one next to it, names a source, and the sources are inner:**
  - **Passions at war within:** "What causes quarrels and what causes fights among you? Is it not this, that your passions are at war within you? You desire and do not have, so you murder. You covet and cannot obtain, so you fight and quarrel" (Jam 4:1–2). **The outer fight is the inner war carried outward.**
  - **The flesh:** "for you are still of the flesh. For while there is jealousy and strife among you, are you not of the flesh and behaving only in a human way?" (1Cor 3:3), after "I could not address you as spiritual people, but as people of the flesh" (3:1).
  - **Conceit:** "Let us not become conceited, provoking one another, envying one another" (Gal 5:26). "He is puffed up with conceit and understands nothing. He has an unhealthy craving for controversy" (1Ti 6:4).
  - **Appetite:** the dividers "do not serve our Lord Christ, but their own appetites [*belly*]" (Rom 16:18).
  - **Envy and selfish ambition behind a good act:** "Some indeed preach Christ from envy and rivalry, but others from good will … The former proclaim Christ out of selfish ambition, not sincerely but thinking to afflict me" (Phili 1:15–17). **The same outward act, preaching, has opposite inner sources.**
  - **A depraved mind:** "constant friction among people who are depraved in mind and deprived of the truth, imagining that godliness is a means of gain" (1Ti 6:5).
  - **Controversies themselves:** "foolish, ignorant controversies … breed quarrels" (2Ti 2:23); "avoid foolish controversies, genealogies, dissensions, and quarrels about the law" (Tit 3:9).
- **How it co-exists.**
  - **With envy, often** (5 of the 11 verses). Envy is paired with strife in Gal 5:26; Phili 1:15; 1Ti 6:4; 1Cor 3:3 ("jealousy"); in Jam 4:2 it is "covet".
  - **With its named opposites:** "that there be no divisions among you, but that you be united in the same mind and the same judgment" (1Cor 1:10); "godliness with contentment is great gain" (1Ti 6:6); "the Lord's servant must not be quarrelsome but kind to everyone … correcting his opponents with gentleness" (2Ti 2:24–25); "keep in step with the Spirit" (Gal 5:25).
- **What it leads to.** Separation from the divider: "avoid them" (Rom 16:17); "after warning him once and then twice, have nothing more to do with him … he is self-condemned" (Tit 3:10–11). "You do not have, because you do not ask" (Jam 4:2), so **strife blocks asking.**
- **Seat.** None in-verse.
  - **Outside the verse:**
    - Rom 16:18 "deceive the hearts of the naive". **Genuine, the victims'** (B v3 §4.4; D v2 §4.3).
    - 1Cor 3:1 "spiritual". **Not an inner seat of the quarrellers.** Not in any batch.
    - Gal 5:24–25 "flesh … Spirit" (C v3 §3.4).
    - 2Ti 2:22 "a pure heart". **Genuine. The remedy's site** (D v2 §1.3). **The seat appears as the remedy (the pure heart, the Spirit) or as the victim (the naive heart). The quarreller's own source is named as passions, flesh or conceit, not as a seat word.** This parallels M20 v3 §20 point 2: the seat as the site of the remedy.

## 20. The quarrelsome house (DQ) — Pro 19:13; 21:9; 21:19; 25:24; 27:15

- **What it is.** Quarrelling that has settled into a household, carried by one person and borne by another.
- **What it does.** It **never stops**: "a wife's quarreling is a continual dripping of rain" (19:13); "A continual dripping on a rainy day and a quarrelsome wife are alike" (27:15). **The image is duration: dripping, not a storm.**
- **Where it comes from.** It is not stated. The sayings describe it from **the one who lives with it.**
- **How it co-exists.**
  - **With fretfulness:** "a quarrelsome and fretful woman" (21:19; *ka'as*, the vexation word of §8).
  - **With being unrestrainable:** "to restrain her is to restrain the wind or to grasp oil in one's right hand" (27:16).
  - The same saying appears three times: "It is better to live in a corner of the housetop than in a house shared with a quarrelsome wife" (21:9; 25:24), and "better to live in a desert land" (21:19).
- **What it leads to.** **The one who bears it withdraws:** to the roof corner, or to the desert. A form of §1's withdrawal, chosen by the one who bears the quarrel.
  - A foolish son sits beside it in 19:13: "A foolish son is ruin to his father". **The household sayings group the two sources of domestic vexation**, and §8's Pro 17:25 is the same pairing.
- **Seat.** None in-verse. Pro 27:16 "wind" (*ruach*). **Spurious as a seat: wind**, as an image of what cannot be held.

## 21. How strife is kindled and quenched (SK) — Pro 6:14; 15:18; 18:18; 23:29; 25:23; 26:17; 26:21; 29:22; 30:33

- **What it is.** The **mechanics** of strife in Proverbs: what kindles it, what feeds it, what ends it.
- **What it does, in the sayings' own images:**
  - **Fuel:** "As charcoal to hot embers and wood to fire, so is a quarrelsome man for kindling strife" (26:21), and the verse before: "For lack of wood the fire goes out, and where there is no whisperer, quarreling ceases" (26:20).
  - **Pressure:** "pressing milk produces curds, pressing the nose produces blood, and pressing anger produces strife" (30:33). The "nose" is *aph* itself: **squeeze the anger-nose and blood comes.**
  - **Weather:** "The north wind brings forth rain, and a backbiting tongue, angry looks" (25:23).
  - **Danger to the meddler:** "Whoever meddles in a quarrel not his own is like one who takes a passing dog by the ears" (26:17).
- **Where it comes from.**
  - **The angry person:** "A hot-tempered man stirs up strife" (15:18); "A man of wrath stirs up strife, and one given to anger causes much transgression" (29:22).
  - **The evil heart:** "with perverted heart devises evil, continually sowing discord" (6:14).
  - **The tongue:** a backbiting tongue (25:23), and the whisperer (26:20, 22).
  - **Wine:** "Who has strife? Who has complaining? … Those who tarry long over wine" (23:29–30).
- **How it co-exists.** **Its opposites are named in the same sayings:** "he who is slow to anger quiets contention" (15:18; §22). "The lot puts an end to quarrels and decides between powerful contenders" (18:18). **When neither side yields, the decision is handed outside both**, to the lot, which "is from the Lord" (16:33).
- **What it leads to.** Sudden ruin for the sower of discord: "calamity will come upon him suddenly" (6:15), and he is on the list of what "the Lord hates" (6:16–19, 6:16 read).
- **Seat.**
  - **In the verse:** Pro 6:14 "heart". **The sower's own. Genuine** (D v2 §2.2, "the evil heart"). **The heart is where discord is devised before it is sown.** Pro 25:23 "wind". **Spurious** (pilot §0).
  - **Outside the verse:** Pro 26:23 "fervent lips with an evil heart". **Genuine. The whisperer's** (D v2 §2.2; M20 v3 §14).

## 22. Slow to anger: anger governed from within (SL) — Pro 14:17; 14:29; 16:32; 19:11; 1Cor 13:5; Tit 1:7; Jam 1:19; 1:20

- **What it is.** The **governed** form of anger: **"long of nostrils"** (pilot §2.2), the human side of the idiom God uses of himself (Exo 34:6).
- **What it does.**
  - It **rules the self**: "Whoever is slow to anger is better than the mighty, and **he who rules his spirit** than he who takes a city" (Pro 16:32). **Governing one's spirit is set above taking a city.**
  - It **overlooks**: "Good sense makes one slow to anger, and it is his glory to overlook an offense" (19:11).
  - It **orders speech**: "let every person be quick to hear, slow to speak, slow to anger" (Jam 1:19).
- **Where it comes from.** **Understanding and good sense**:
  - "Whoever is slow to anger has great understanding, but he who has a hasty temper exalts folly" (Pro 14:29)
  - "Good sense makes one slow to anger" (19:11)
  - in 1Cor 13:5, **love**: "it is not irritable or resentful"
- **How it co-exists.**
  - **Its opposite is quickness,** which acts foolishly: "A man of quick temper acts foolishly" (Pro 14:17). The overseer "must not be … quick-tempered" (Tit 1:7).
  - **In the next verses, with a tranquil heart:** "A tranquil heart gives life to the flesh, but envy makes the bones rot" (Pro 14:30).
- **What it leads to.**
  - Understanding (14:29), glory (19:11), and **the righteousness that human anger cannot produce**: "for the anger of man does not produce the righteousness of God. Therefore put away all filthiness … and receive with meekness the implanted word, which is able to save your souls" (Jam 1:20–21). **Slowness to anger opens the way to receiving the word.**
  - A "cool spirit" belongs to the "man of understanding" (Pro 17:27).
- **Seat.**
  - **In the verse:**
    - Pro 16:32 "spirit" (*ruach*). **The bearer's own, as what is ruled. Genuine** (A v3 §2.1 [H]; H v1 §1.5, "rule turned inward"; C v3 §1.6).
    - Pro 14:29 "temper" (*ruach*, "short of spirit"). **The bearer's own. Genuine** (A v3 §2.1 [T]; B v3 §1.6).
  - **Outside the verse:**
    - Pro 14:30 "a tranquil heart gives life to the flesh". **Genuine** (A v3 §4.3; G v3 §8.1; C v3 §1.8).
    - Pro 17:27 "a cool spirit". **Genuine** (B v3 §1.6, §4.3).
    - Jam 1:21 "save your souls". **Genuine** (B v3 §4.4; F v4 §10).
  - **Across §22 the seat is the *ruach*, as the thing ruled or kept cool. The anger word and the spirit word stand side by side, and "slow/long" and "short" describe both.**

## 23. Turning another's anger away (TA) — Pro 15:1; 16:14; 21:14; 25:15; 29:8

- **What it is.** Anger **governed from outside**, by the person facing it (pilot §2.2).
- **What it does.** It turns the other's anger:
  - "A soft answer turns away wrath, but a harsh word stirs up anger" (15:1)
  - "a wise man will appease" a king's wrath (16:14)
  - "A gift in secret averts anger, and a concealed bribe, strong wrath" (21:14)
  - "With patience a ruler may be persuaded, and **a soft tongue will break a bone**" (25:15)
  - "the wise turn away wrath" while "scoffers set a city aflame" (29:8)
- **Where it comes from.** From **wisdom** in the one facing the anger (16:14; 29:8), and from **patience** (25:15, literally *length of nostrils*, the §22 word, used here of the one **not** angry).
- **How it co-exists.** **Softness is set against its opposite each time**: a soft answer against a harsh word (15:1), wise men against scoffers (29:8).
  - **The gift is the ambiguous instrument.** "A gift in secret averts anger" (21:14) sits in a chapter that also knows "A wicked man accepts a bribe in secret to pervert … justice" (17:23, read in the §8 context). The saying reports that it works. It does not approve it.
  - **The narrative parallels in part 1:**
    - Judah's plea (Gen 44:18) and Joab's scripted line (2Sa 11:20) are §5's pleading and scripting strands.
    - Naaman's servants turn his rage by a soft question (2Ki 5:13).
- **What it leads to.** Anger turned (15:1; 29:8), or a ruler persuaded (25:15). **The contrary answer makes anger:** the harsh word (15:1), the scoffers' fire (29:8).
- **Seat.** None in-verse.
  - **Outside the verse:** Pro 25:13 "he refreshes the soul of his masters" (a faithful messenger), two verses before 25:15. **Genuine. The master's soul, as what is refreshed** (E v2 §5.3). **The one who turns a ruler's anger by patience is the one who refreshes the ruler's soul.** This is a neighbourhood observation, marked [Claude reading].

## 24. Fretting over the wicked who prosper (FN) — Psa 37:1, 37:7, 37:8; Pro 24:19

- **What it is.** *Charah* in the reflexive: "**heat yourself**", rendered "fret". It is **the anger of the righteous at the wicked's success.**
- **What it does.** **It heats the self, and "it tends only to evil"** (Psa 37:8). The anger turns inward on its bearer, and outward into wrongdoing.
- **Where it comes from.** **Seeing others prosper:** "fret not yourself over the one who prospers in his way, over the man who carries out evil devices!" (37:7). **Envy is paired with it:** "be not envious of wrongdoers" (37:1; Pro 24:19).
- **How it co-exists.** **Its remedy is given as a set of inner acts, each replacing the fretting:**
  - "Trust in the Lord, and do good" (37:3)
  - "Commit your way to the Lord" (37:5)
  - "**Be still** before the Lord and **wait patiently** for him" (37:7)
  - "Refrain from anger, and forsake wrath!" (37:8)

  **Heat is to be replaced by stillness and waiting.**
- **What it leads to.** **A reason is given in the next verse, each time:** the wicked "will soon fade like the grass" (37:2); "the evildoers shall be cut off, but those who wait for the Lord shall inherit the land" (37:9); "the evil man has no future; the lamp of the wicked will be put out" (Pro 24:20). **The fretting is answered by a longer view of time**, which links to §3's anger that has a course.
  - **Pro 24:17–18, just before:** "Do not rejoice when your enemy falls, and let not your heart be glad when he stumbles, lest the Lord see it and be displeased". **Neither heating oneself at the wicked's success nor rejoicing at their fall is allowed.**
- **Seat.** None in-verse.
  - **Outside the verse:** Pro 24:17 "let not your heart be glad". **Genuine. The bearer's own, as a watched heart** (A v3 §4.4; F v4 §8.3; G v3 §9.3). **The seat is named for the opposite temptation, gladness at a fall, not for the fretting itself.**

## 25. Anger in the lists: put off with the old self (VL) — Rom 1:29; 13:13; 2Cor 12:20; Gal 5:20; Eph 4:31; Col 3:8

- **What it is.** Anger, wrath, strife and quarrelling **as items in a list** of works or states, **with the list's frame giving the inner place** (pilot §1.1).
- **What it does.** It stands among its companions:
  - "envy, murder, strife, deceit" (Rom 1:29)
  - "quarreling and jealousy" (Rom 13:13)
  - "quarreling, jealousy, anger, hostility, slander, gossip, conceit, and disorder" (2Cor 12:20)
  - "enmity, strife, jealousy, fits of anger, rivalries, dissensions, divisions, envy" (Gal 5:20–21)
  - "bitterness and wrath and anger and clamor and slander … malice" (Eph 4:31)
  - "anger, wrath, malice, slander, and obscene talk from your mouth" (Col 3:8)

  **Anger's constant neighbours are envy and jealousy (4 of the 6 lists: all but Eph 4:31 and Col 3:8) and speech-sins (slander, clamor, obscene talk).**
- **Where it comes from, by the frame:**
  - **"the works of the flesh"** (Gal 5:19)
  - **"a debased mind"** given up to by God (Rom 1:28)
  - **"make no provision for the flesh"** (Rom 13:14)
  - **the old self**: "you have put off the old self with its practices" (Col 3:9)
- **How it co-exists.** **Each list faces an opposite:**
  - "the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, patience …" (Gal 5:22)
  - "Be kind to one another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another, as God in Christ forgave you" (Eph 4:32)
  - "put on the new self, which is being renewed in knowledge" (Col 3:10)
  - "put on the Lord Jesus Christ" (Rom 13:14)
  - **In Eph 4:30–31 the list comes straight after "do not grieve the Holy Spirit of God".** Anger is set as something that grieves the Spirit.
- **What it leads to.** "those who do such things will not inherit the kingdom of God" (Gal 5:21). Paul fears having to "mourn over many" (2Cor 12:21).
- **Seat.** None in-verse.
  - **Outside the verse:**
    - Gal 5:19, 22 "flesh … Spirit" (D v2 §8.5; C v3 §3.4; A v3 §4.5)
    - Eph 4:30 "Holy Spirit" (A v3 §3.4, "the divine Spirit and grief")
    - Rom 13:14 "flesh"

    **The frame's words are the seat's substitutes** (pilot §3 item 7), and nothing here changes that. **New:** Eph 4:30–31 puts the **Spirit's grief** directly before the anger list. So the other party affected by human anger is named as the Holy Spirit.

## 26. Indignation at the good Jesus does (IJ) — Mat 21:15; 26:8; Mar 14:4; Luk 13:14; Joh 7:23; Act 4:2

- **What it is.** Indignation that **defends a norm** (the Sabbath, the law, good stewardship, the priests' authority) **against a good act** by Jesus or done to him, or by his apostles.
- **What it does.** **It speaks for the norm.**
  - "Why this waste? For this could have been sold for a large sum and given to the poor" (Mat 26:8–9)
  - "said to themselves indignantly, 'Why was the ointment wasted like that?' … And they scolded her" (Mar 14:4–5)
  - "There are six days in which work ought to be done. Come on those days and be healed, and not on the Sabbath day" (Luk 13:14), **addressed to the crowd, not to Jesus**
  - "Do you hear what these are saying?" (Mat 21:16)
- **Where it comes from.** **Seeing the good done:**
  - "when the chief priests and the scribes **saw** the wonderful things that he did, and the children crying out … 'Hosanna'" (Mat 21:15)
  - "when the disciples **saw** it" (Mat 26:8)
  - "because Jesus had healed on the Sabbath" (Luk 13:14)
  - "because on the Sabbath I made a man's whole body well" (Joh 7:23)
  - "because they were teaching the people and proclaiming in Jesus the resurrection" (Act 4:2)
- **How it co-exists.** **It is answered in each case by a reframing of the norm:**
  - "She has done a beautiful thing to me" (Mat 26:10; Mar 14:6)
  - "Does not each of you on the Sabbath untie his ox …? And ought not this woman, a daughter of Abraham … be loosed from this bond on the Sabbath day?" (Luk 13:15–16)
  - "If on the Sabbath a man receives circumcision … are you angry with me because … I made a man's whole body well? Do not judge by appearances" (Joh 7:23–24)
  - "Out of the mouth of infants and nursing babies you have prepared praise" (Mat 21:16)

  **This is §9's and §6's reframing move, used by Jesus on indignation.**
  - **The indignation can be hidden:** "said to themselves" (Mar 14:4, pilot §1.2, the reflexive in place of the seat), and yet "Jesus, aware of this" (Mat 26:10).
- **What it leads to.** Arrest (Act 4:3), the reframing (above), and in Joh 7:25, the people's question, "Is not this the man whom they seek to kill?". **The indignation borders on the plan to kill.** Compare Mar 3:6 (§32).
- **Seat.** None in-verse or ±2. **What stands in for the seat is the reflexive "to themselves" (Mar 14:4), and Jesus's awareness** (Mat 26:10).

## 27. Rage at the word and at God's anointed (RW) — Isa 45:24; Luk 4:28; Act 4:25; 5:33; 7:54; 19:28; Rev 11:18

- **What it is.** **Collective** rage (a synagogue, a council, a city, the nations) **at a word spoken** or **against God and his anointed**.
- **What it does.** It **fills, cuts, grinds and cries out**:
  - "all in the synagogue were **filled with wrath**" (Luk 4:28)
  - "they were **enraged** [literally *sawn through*] and wanted to kill them" (Act 5:33)
  - "they were **enraged** [*cut to the heart*], and **they ground their teeth at him**" (Act 7:54)
  - "they were enraged and were **crying out, 'Great is Artemis of the Ephesians!'**" (Act 19:28)
  - "Why did the Gentiles **rage**, and the peoples plot in vain?" (Act 4:25, quoting Psa 2)
- **Where it comes from.** **Hearing:**
  - "When they **heard** these things" (Luk 4:28; Act 5:33; 7:54; 19:28)
  - the grace to the widow of Zarephath and to Naaman **rather than to Israel** (Luk 4:26–27)
  - "the Holy Spirit, whom God has given to those who obey him" (Act 5:32)
  - "you who received the law … and did not keep it" (Act 7:53)
  - a threat to trade and to a goddess's standing (Act 19:26–27)

  **The word heard is a word that lowers the hearers' standing or exposes their guilt.** In Act 19 the trade motive is stated openly.
- **How it co-exists.**
  - **With stopping the ears:** "they cried out with a loud voice and **stopped their ears** and rushed together at him" (Act 7:57). **The rage refuses to go on hearing.**
  - **With a calm figure beside it:** "But he, **full of the Holy Spirit**, gazed into heaven and saw the glory of God" (Act 7:55). And "passing through their midst, he went away" (Luk 4:30). **The seat is again named in the contrast figure** (M20 v3 §20 point 4; §17 here).
  - **With counsel that restrains it:** Gamaliel, "if it is of God, you will not be able to overthrow them. You might even be found opposing God!" (Act 5:39). They "took his advice", and yet "beat them" (5:40). **Restrained from killing, but not from beating.**
- **What it leads to.**
  - Trying to throw him off a cliff (Luk 4:29), stoning (Act 7:58), a riot (Act 19:29).
  - **The end God sets:** "all who were incensed against him … shall come and be ashamed" (Isa 45:24); "The nations raged, but your wrath came" (Rev 11:18, B2). **The collective rage meets the divine wrath at the end.** This is Psa 2 as the apostles read it: "against the Lord and against his Anointed … for truly in this city there were gathered together against your holy servant Jesus" (Act 4:26–27).
- **Seat.**
  - **In the verse:**
    - Act 7:54: the M47 tag's surface is "enraged". The Greek reads *dieprionto tais kardiais autōn*, "they were sawn through **in their hearts**" [lexical, not in DB]. The ESV folds "hearts" into "enraged". **Genuine. The enraged ones' own heart, as where the word cut.** Not in any batch: **new**.
      - Compare pilot §1.3 (*cholaō* built on bile) and §1.1 (*thumos*). Here the seat is a separate word, **hidden by the translation, not by the Greek.**
      - Act 5:33 uses the same verb **without** "hearts" [lexical].
    - Act 4:25 "Holy Spirit". **Divine, speaking** (the Spirit speaking through David's psalm). **Spurious for the ragers.**
  - **Outside the verse:** Act 7:55 "full of the Holy Spirit" (E v2 §3.3); Act 5:32 "the Holy Spirit … given to those who obey". **Divine, as received by the other party.**

## 28. The enemy's anger, as the one attacked bears it (EA) — Psa 7:6; 55:3; 58:4; 124:3; 138:7; 140:3; Isa 41:11; 2Cor 7:5

- **What it is.** Anger **seen from the side of its target**: the psalmist, Israel, or Paul in Macedonia.
- **What it does to the one attacked.**
  - It **swallows**: "they would have swallowed us up alive, when their anger was kindled against us; then the flood would have swept us away" (Psa 124:3–4).
  - It **bears a grudge and drops trouble**: "they drop trouble upon me, and in anger they bear a grudge against me" (Psa 55:3).
  - It **poisons through speech**: "They make their tongue sharp as a serpent's, and under their lips is the venom of asps" (140:3); "They have venom like the venom of a serpent, like the deaf adder that stops its ear" (58:4). **Venom is the image of the enemy's anger in speech.** Compare §16's Deu 32:33.
  - **In Paul's case it is summed up without detail:** "our bodies had no rest, but we were afflicted at every turn — **fighting without and fear within**" (2Cor 7:5). **Outer conflict and inner fear are named as a pair.**
- **Where it comes from.** **The enemy's heart:**
  - "in your hearts you devise wrongs" (Psa 58:2)
  - "who plan evil things in their heart and stir up wars continually" (140:2)
  - they are "estranged from the womb" (58:3)
- **How it co-exists.**
  - **With the attacked one's inner state named next:** "My heart is in anguish within me; the terrors of death have fallen upon me. Fear and trembling come upon me" (Psa 55:4–5). **The enemy's anger (55:3) is felt as anguish of heart (55:4).**
  - **With an appeal to God's anger against it:** "Arise, O Lord, in your anger; lift yourself up against the fury of my enemies" (Psa 7:6, B2). **The human's answer to another's anger is to call on God's.** Compare §24, where the answer to the wicked's success is waiting, not heating oneself.
- **What it leads to.** **Deliverance, looked back on or asked for:**
  - "If it had not been the Lord who was on our side" (Psa 124:1–2)
  - "you stretch out your hand against the wrath of my enemies, and your right hand delivers me" (138:7)
  - "all who are incensed against you shall be put to shame" (Isa 41:11), after "fear not, for I am with you" (41:10)
  - "God, who comforts the downcast, comforted us by the coming of Titus" (2Cor 7:6)
- **Seat.**
  - **In the verse:** 2Cor 7:5 "bodies" (*sarx*, flesh). **The bearer's own, bodily: "no rest"**. Not in any batch: **new**. **The inner state is carried by "fear within", not by the seat word.**
  - **Outside the verse:**
    - Psa 55:4 "My heart is in anguish within me". **Genuine. The attacked one's own** (A v3 §1; G v3 §16.3).
    - Psa 58:2 and 140:2 "hearts … heart". **Genuine. The enemy's own**, where the wrong is devised (F v4 §12.2; D v2 §2.2; C v3 §2.1).
    - Psa 7:5 "let the enemy pursue my soul". **Genuine / life** (F v4 §8.4; E v2 §3.2; G v3 §12.4).
    - 2Cor 7:3 "you are in our hearts". **Genuine. The bearer's own, holding others.** Not in any batch.
    - **So the seat appears on both sides: the enemy's heart that devises, and the attacked one's heart that is in anguish.**

## 29. Fear of another's anger, and freedom from it (FA) — Isa 7:4; 51:13; Dan 1:10; Heb 11:27

- **What it is.** **Fear** as the response to someone else's anger, and the **freedom** from it that faith gives.
- **What it does.**
  - It **shakes the heart**: "the heart of Ahaz and the heart of his people shook as the trees of the forest shake before the wind" (Isa 7:2)
  - it **goes on all day**: "you fear continually all the day because of the wrath of the oppressor" (Isa 51:13)
  - it **makes a subordinate refuse**: "I fear my lord the king … So you would endanger my head with the king" (Dan 1:10)
- **Where it comes from.** From the other's anger, and **from forgetting God**: "who are you that you are afraid of man who dies … **and have forgotten the Lord, your Maker**" (Isa 51:12–13).
- **How it co-exists.** **The freedom is given in the same verses as the fear:**
  - "Be careful, be quiet, do not fear, and **do not let your heart be faint** because of these two smoldering stumps of firebrands, at the fierce anger of Rezin" (Isa 7:4). **The anger is shrunk to smoking stubs.** "If you are not firm in faith, you will not be firm at all" (7:9).
  - "And where is the wrath of the oppressor?" (Isa 51:13). **The anger is questioned out of existence.**
  - "By faith he left Egypt, **not being afraid of the anger of the king**, for he endured as seeing him who is invisible" (Heb 11:27). **Seeing the invisible makes the king's anger nothing to fear.**
  - Daniel's answer to the eunuch's fear is a **test**: "Test your servants for ten days" (Dan 1:12). This follows "Daniel resolved [*set on his heart*] that he would not defile himself" (1:8).
- **What it leads to.** Release: "He who is bowed down shall speedily be released" (Isa 51:14). Endurance (Heb 11:27), and a test passed (Dan 1:12).
- **Seat.**
  - **In the verse:** Isa 7:4 "heart" (*levav*). **The bearer's own, as what must not faint. Genuine** (A v3 §1; F v4 §6).
  - **Outside the verse:**
    - Isa 7:2 "the heart of Ahaz … shook". **Genuine** (F v4 §6).
    - Dan 1:8 "resolved" [*set on his heart*]. **Genuine. The contrast figure's own** (C v3 §2.2; D v2 §8.3; F v4 §7; H v1 §7). **The one afraid of the king (the eunuch) has no seat named. The one who is not afraid (Daniel) has a heart that resolved.**

## 30. Overflowing pride: insolence (PR) — Pro 21:24; Isa 16:6; Jer 48:30; Hos 7:16

- **What it is.** *'Ebrah* in its sense of **overflowing** (the "overflowings of your anger" of Job 40:11, §14): **arrogance that spills out**, rendered "insolence" or "pride".
- **What it does.** It **boasts falsely**:
  - "in his idle boasting he is not right" (Isa 16:6)
  - "his boasts are false, his deeds are false" (Jer 48:30)
  - "the insolence of their tongue" (Hos 7:16)
  - "'Scoffer' is the name of the arrogant, haughty man who acts with arrogant pride [*overflowing of pride*]" (Pro 21:24)
- **Where it comes from.** **The heart lifted up:** "We have heard of the pride of Moab … of his loftiness, his pride, and his arrogance, and **the haughtiness of his heart**" (Jer 48:29). Moab's pride is heaped up in word after word (Isa 16:6; Jer 48:29).
- **How it co-exists.** **With the tongue**, which carries it (Hos 7:16; Isa 16:6 "boasting"). **With the prophet's own wailing:** "Therefore I wail for Moab … More than for Jazer I weep for you" (Jer 48:31–32); "let Moab wail for Moab" (Isa 16:7). **God knows the insolence ("I know his insolence", Jer 48:30), and the prophet weeps over its end.**
- **What it leads to.** Falling "by the sword because of the insolence of their tongue" (Hos 7:16), and lament. The same word links to the rage of §5's oppressor ("the insolent fury ceased", Isa 14:4).
- **Seat.**
  - **In the verse:** none.
  - **Outside the verse:** Jer 48:29 "haughtiness of his heart". **Genuine. The bearer's own** (D v2 §9.1). This is the same lifted heart as 2Ch 26:16 (§4) and D v2 §9.1.
  - Hos 7:14 "They do not cry to me from the heart, but they wail upon their beds". **Genuine** (A v3 §3.3; E v2 §1.1; C v3 §8.2). **The insolent tongue (7:16) comes from a heart that does not cry to God (7:14).**

## 31. The parable master's anger (PM) — Mat 18:34; 22:7; Luk 14:21 (B9: figures standing for God)

- **What it is.** The anger of a master or king **inside a parable**, where the figure stands for God.
- **What it does.** **It acts at once, with no interval** (pilot §1.3):
  - "in anger his master delivered him to the jailers" (Mat 18:34)
  - "The king was angry, and he sent his troops and destroyed those murderers and burned their city" (22:7)
  - "the master of the house became angry and said to his servant, 'Go out quickly … and bring in the poor and crippled and blind and lame'" (Luk 14:21)
- **Where it comes from.** **Mercy refused or invitation refused:**
  - "should not you have had mercy on your fellow servant, as I had mercy on you?" (Mat 18:33)
  - "they paid no attention … the rest seized his servants … and killed them" (22:5–6)
  - the excuses of oxen and a wife (Luk 14:19–20)
- **How it co-exists.** **In Luk 14:21 anger turns into a wider invitation, not punishment.** The anger that invitations were refused fills the house with the poor: "compel people to come in, that my house may be filled" (14:23). **Of the three parable angers, one ends in grace to outsiders.** Mat 22:8–9 does the same after the destruction: "invite to the wedding feast as many as you find".
- **What it leads to.** The applied lesson turns to the **human heart**: "So also my heavenly Father will do to every one of you, **if you do not forgive your brother from your heart**" (Mat 18:35).
- **Seat.**
  - **In the verse:** none.
  - **Outside the verse:** Mat 18:35 "from your heart". **Genuine. The hearer's own, as the place where forgiveness must come from** (E v2 §9.1; D v2 R2). **The parable master's anger is shown in order to place the requirement in the hearer's heart.** This is unit 2's territory (divine anger borne by the human), noted here because the ruling put B9 in unit 1.

## 32. Jesus's anger and indignation (JA) — Mar 3:5; 10:14 (B4: own item, per ruling)

- **What it is.** Anger in Jesus, **described in human terms**.
- **What it does.**
  - "he **looked around at them with anger, grieved at their hardness of heart**, and said to the man, 'Stretch out your hand'" (Mar 3:5). **The anger looks, then acts to heal.**
  - "when Jesus saw it, he was indignant and said to them, 'Let the children come to me; do not hinder them'" (Mar 10:14). **The indignation is on behalf of the children**, like §7's indignation for another.
- **Where it comes from.**
  - **Silence in the face of a plain question:** "Is it lawful on the Sabbath to do good or to do harm, to save life or to kill?" But they were silent (Mar 3:4). **The anger is at hardness of heart shown as silence.**
  - **The disciples rebuking** those who brought children (Mar 10:13).
- **How it co-exists.** **With grief, in the same clause**: "with anger, grieved" (A v3 §2.5). This is the one M02 verse where **anger and grief are held together, at the same object, by the same person**. Part 1 had Jonathan's anger with grief (1Sa 20:34), but that grief was for David, not at the one he was angry with. **Jesus grieves over those he is angry with.** With blessing: "he took them in his arms and blessed them" (Mar 10:16).
- **What it leads to.**
  - **The healing, and at once the plot to kill:** "The Pharisees went out and immediately held counsel with the Herodians against him, how to destroy him" (Mar 3:6). **His anger heals. Their silence becomes a plot.**
  - Teaching about receiving the kingdom "like a child" (10:15).
- **Seat.**
  - **In the verse:** Mar 3:5 "heart" (*kardia*). **The other party's hardened heart, as the object of both the anger and the grief. Genuine** (A v3 §2.5; C v3 §9.3). **Jesus's own seat is not named; his opponents' hardened heart is.** This is the §1 and §38 point 3 pattern again.
  - **Outside the verse:** Mar 3:4 "to save life" (*psychē*). **Life-sense, the life at stake** (D v2 §2.3; E v2 §11.3).

## 33. Neither cold nor hot (HC) — Rev 3:15, 3:16

- **What it is.** The inner state of a church given as **temperature** (pilot §1.6): "you are neither cold nor hot".
- **What it does.** **Lukewarmness makes the church rejected:** "Would that you were either cold or hot! So, because you are lukewarm … I will spit you out of my mouth" (3:15–16). **Either extreme is preferred to the middle.**
- **Where it comes from.** **Self-sufficiency that does not see itself:** "you say, 'I am rich, I have prospered, and I need nothing,' **not realizing** that you are wretched, pitiable, poor, blind, and naked" (3:17). **The lukewarm state is linked to a failed self-perception.** Compare M20's exposure phenomena (M20 v3 §6).
- **How it co-exists.** **With counsel to buy** gold, white garments and eye-salve, "so that you may see" (3:18).
- **What it leads to.** Rejection, or, through the counsel, sight.
- **Seat.** None in-verse or ±2 (Rev 3:13 "Spirit" is divine, speaking). *Zestos* "hot" is the M02 tag. **Whether "hot" here is anger at all is doubtful. It is heat as zeal.** Recorded as M02-tagged; its sense is zeal, not wrath.

## 34. Anger and passion as a drink given to others (PI) — Hab 2:15; Rev 14:8; 18:3

- **What it is.** Human wrath or passion **given to others to drink**, so that it intoxicates them.
- **What it does.**
  - "Woe to him who makes his neighbors drink — **you pour out your wrath and make them drunk**, in order to gaze at their nakedness!" (Hab 2:15). **The anger (or venom, *chemah*) is poured out to shame another.**
  - Babylon "made all nations drink the wine of the passion [*thumos*] of her sexual immorality" (Rev 14:8). "All nations have drunk the wine of the passion of her sexual immorality" (18:3).
- **Where it comes from.** A will to see another's nakedness (Hab 2:15). For Babylon, luxury and trade ("the merchants of the earth have grown rich", 18:3).
- **How it co-exists.** **The drink comes back to the one who gave it**, as God's cup:
  - "You will have your fill of shame instead of glory. Drink, yourself … **The cup in the Lord's right hand will come around to you**" (Hab 2:16)
  - "he also will drink the wine of God's wrath, poured full strength into the cup of his anger" (Rev 14:10, divine, read as context)

  **Human passion given as a drink is answered by divine wrath given as a drink.** Pilot §1.1 noted "the drinking image is shared".
- **What it leads to.** Shame (Hab 2:16), and Babylon's fall ("Fallen, fallen", Rev 14:8; 18:2). The call: "Come out of her, my people" (18:4).
- **Seat.** None in-verse. Rev 18:2 "every unclean spirit". **Not human** (D v2 §8.1).

## 35. Provoking and indignation turned to good (PG) — 2Cor 7:11; 9:2; Heb 10:24

- **What it is.** The **same words**, provoking and indignation, **used of good** (pilot §1.5, "one word, opposite value").
- **What it does.**
  - "let us consider how to **stir up** [*paroxusmos*, the word of Act 15:39's 'sharp disagreement'] one another to love and good works" (Heb 10:24)
  - "your zeal has **stirred up** most of them" (2Cor 9:2)
  - "what earnestness this godly grief has produced in you … what **indignation**, what fear, what longing, what zeal, what punishment!" (2Cor 7:11)
- **Where it comes from.**
  - **Godly grief:** "godly grief produces a repentance that leads to salvation without regret, whereas worldly grief produces death" (2Cor 7:10). **Indignation is one of the fruits of grief that turns toward God.**
  - **Readiness and zeal** (2Cor 9:2).
  - **Drawing near "with a true heart"** (Heb 10:22).
- **How it co-exists.** **In 2Cor 7:11, indignation is one of a sequence of inner operations valued as good:** earnestness, eagerness, indignation, fear, longing, zeal, punishment. **Indignation here is aimed at the wrong in their own midst.**
- **What it leads to.** "At every point you have proved yourselves innocent in the matter" (2Cor 7:11). **The comfort of the other**: "his spirit has been refreshed by you all" (7:13). Meeting together and encouraging (Heb 10:25).
- **Seat.** None in-verse.
  - **Outside the verse:**
    - Heb 10:22 "a true heart … hearts sprinkled clean from an evil conscience". **Genuine. The provokers' own, as the ground from which the good provoking comes** (D v2 §1.4; E v2 §5.1).
    - 2Cor 7:13 "his spirit has been refreshed". **Genuine. Titus's**, the other party's. Not in any batch.

    **The good provoking is set after a heart made clean (Heb 10:22), just as the bad strife of §19 is set after passions within (Jam 4:1).**

**What the map shows (v2, all 246 verses).**
1. **Genre decides where the angry person's own seat appears.**
   - In **narrative** the angry person's own seat is rare: 2Ki 6:11, Est 5:9 (before the anger), Act 7:54 (hidden by the translation), Act 17:16, and the prophet's Eze 3:14.
   - In **wisdom and teaching** it is frequent: Job 36:13; Pro 6:14; 14:29; 16:32; 19:3; Ecc 7:9; and Qoheleth's vexed heart (Ecc 2:23; 7:3; 11:10).
   - **Narrative shows anger by its act. Wisdom locates it in the heart or spirit, so that it can be governed or judged.** This refines v1's point 1: Job 36:13 is not a lone exception, but the head of the wisdom pattern.
2. **Where anger is governed, the seat is the *ruach*.** "Rules his spirit" (Pro 16:32), "short of spirit" (14:29), "quick in your spirit" (Ecc 7:9), and in context "a cool spirit" (Pro 17:27) and "patient in spirit" (Ecc 7:8). **Where anger is kept or devised, the seat is the *lev*:** "cherish" (Job 36:13), "lodges" (Ecc 7:9, bosom), "devises … discord" (Pro 6:14), "rages against the Lord" (Pro 19:3). ~~**Spirit for the rising and ruling, heart for the keeping.** A v3 §2.1 read Ecc 7:9 as "two stages, two seats". The wider set extends that.~~ **Withdrawn 2026-10-01 by researcher ruling** (imputed meaning, not supported by the verses; see §X). The verse list above stands as a list.
3. **In the narratives, the seat is still most often named by someone else** (v1 point 2 stands), and part 2 adds **Jesus's anger, where the seat named is the opponents' hardened heart** (Mar 3:5). **The one who is angry in the right way has no seat named; the one who provokes it does.**
4. **The seat of the contrast figure** (v1 point 3) gains:
   - "His heart is steady" (Psa 112:8) against the wicked's melting anger
   - Stephen "full of the Holy Spirit" (Act 7:55) against the council's rage
   - Daniel "resolved" [*set on his heart*] (Dan 1:8) against the eunuch's fear of the king's anger
5. **A lifted or set heart comes before anger** (v1 point 4) gains:
   - "the haughtiness of his heart" (Jer 48:29) before Moab's insolence
   - "his heart shall be set against the holy covenant" (Dan 11:28) before the rage of 11:30
6. **The *nephesh* stands where the angry one's company or anger puts the self at risk:**
   - Gen 49:6 (the soul refuses the company)
   - Pro 22:25 (the self snared by learning an angry man's ways)
   - Pro 20:2 and Est 7:7 (the provoker's life forfeit)
   - Jon 4:3, 8 (the angry one asks for his own life to end)
