## 1. The fallen face: denied, the body withdraws (FF) — Gen 4:5, 4:6; 1Ki 20:43; 21:4

- **What it is.** Anger that does not go out at the one who caused it, but **sinks into the body**: the face falls, the person lies down, turns the face away, and will not eat.
- **What it does.**
  - "Cain was very angry, and his face fell" (Gen 4:5).
  - Ahab went home "vexed and sullen … lay down on his bed and turned away his face and would eat no food" (1Ki 21:4).
  - In 1Ki 20:43 the same pair, "vexed and sullen", takes him home to Samaria.
  - The movement is **inward and downward**: face, bed, refusing food.
- **Where it comes from.** From **a denial the person cannot reverse**:
  - no regard from God for Cain's offering while Abel's is regarded (Gen 4:4–5)
  - the prophet's verdict, "your life shall be for his life, and your people for his people" (1Ki 20:42)
  - Naboth's refusal, "I will not give you the inheritance of my fathers" (1Ki 21:3–4)

  In each case the denier is out of the angry person's reach: God, a prophet speaking for God, and a man protected by the law of inheritance.
- **How it co-exists.**
  - **Someone else reads it off the body and names it.** God asks Cain, "Why are you angry, and why has your face fallen?" (Gen 4:6). Jezebel asks Ahab, "Why is your spirit so vexed that you eat no food?" (1Ki 21:5). **The angry person names nothing. The inner state is put into words by the questioner.**
  - **The questioner then offers a way forward, and the two ways are opposites.**
    - God: "If you do well, will you not be accepted? … sin is crouching at the door … you must rule over it" (Gen 4:7). This is **mastery of the anger from within**.
    - Jezebel: "Arise and eat bread and let your heart be cheerful; I will give you the vineyard" (1Ki 21:7). This is **removing the denial by a crime**.
- **What it leads to.** In both passages, **killing**.
  - "Cain rose up against his brother Abel and killed him" (Gen 4:8).
  - Naboth is falsely charged and "stoned to death" (1Ki 21:13). "As soon as Ahab heard that Naboth was dead, Ahab arose to go down to the vineyard … to take possession of it" (21:16). **Ahab rises from the bed he lay down on in 21:4.**

  The anger that sank inward comes out as a death: **Cain's by his own hand, Ahab's by another's.**
- **Seat.**
  - **In the verse:** none of the four.
  - **Whose seat:** in context, **named by the other party.** 1Ki 21:5 "spirit" is Ahab's own, but Jezebel names it, not the narrator (C v3 §9.7: "spirit state stops appetite"; pilot §1.4). **Genuine.**
  - **Outside the verse:**
    - **Before:** 1Ki 20:42 *nephesh* "your life shall be for his life". **Life-sense only.** It is the verdict on Ahab's life that sends him home sullen (H v1 §2.2, "the price of a life, again").
    - **After:** 1Ki 21:7 "let your heart be cheerful". **Genuine.** The heart is offered as the place where the anger will be undone, and the price is Naboth (C v3 §1.6).
    - **Gen 4:** no seat word in Gen 4:2–9. God's question and the image of sin "crouching at the door" do the seat's work: **the inner struggle is given as a scene at a door, not as an organ.** This agrees with pilot §1.4 and does not change it.

## 2. Strife among kin, refused or fought over (KS) — Gen 13:8; Deu 2:9, 2:19; 2Sa 19:42

- **What it is.** Contention between parties who are **kin**, where kinship is named as the reason to stop it, or as the ground of the quarrel.
- **What it does.**
  - Abram **refuses** it: "Let there be no strife between you and me … for we are kinsmen" (Gen 13:8).
  - God **forbids** it: "Do not harass Moab … because I have given Ar to the people of Lot" (Deu 2:9). Likewise Ammon, "the sons of Lot" (Deu 2:19).
  - The men of Judah and Israel **fight over the king** with kinship as the weapon: "Because the king is our close relative. Why then are you angry over this matter?" (2Sa 19:42).
- **Where it comes from.**
  - **Scarcity:** "the land could not support both of them" (Gen 13:6), and "strife between the herdsmen" (13:7).
  - **Rival claims on a person:** "Why have our brothers the men of Judah stolen you away?" (2Sa 19:41).
- **How it co-exists.**
  - In Gen 13 strife is **ended by giving the other the choice**: "Is not the whole land before you? … If you take the left hand, then I will go to the right" (13:9). **The refusal of strife is carried out as a yielding.** Lot then "lifted up his eyes and saw" (13:10) and chose.
  - In 2Sa 19 kinship **feeds** the quarrel. Each side counts its share: "We have ten shares in the king" (19:43), and it ends with "the words of the men of Judah were fiercer than the words of the men of Israel" (19:43). **The same ground, kinship, stops strife in Gen 13 and inflames it in 2Sa 19.**
- **What it leads to.** Separation in peace (Gen 13:11), a boundary kept (Deu 2), or **words growing fiercer** (2Sa 19:43).
- **Seat.** None in the verses or their ±3 context. Kinship does the seat's work of grounding the movement. Compare E v2 R7, kinship and conflict words in M44.

## 3. Anger's own course: turning away, cherished, fatal (AL) — Gen 27:44, 27:45; Job 5:2; 36:13; 36:18

- **What it is.** Anger treated as **something with a life of its own**. It lasts, turns away, is cherished, entices, and kills the one who carries it.
- **What it does.**
  - It **runs a course and passes**, with time and forgetting: "until your brother's fury turns away … until your brother's anger turns away from you, and he forgets what you have done to him" (Gen 27:44–45).
  - It is **held and fed**: "The godless in heart cherish anger" (Job 36:13).
  - It **draws the bearer on**: "Beware lest wrath entice you into scoffing" (Job 36:18).
  - It **kills its bearer**: "Surely vexation kills the fool, and jealousy slays the simple" (Job 5:2).
- **Where it comes from.**
  - For Esau, from the stolen blessing: "Esau hated Jacob because of the blessing" (Gen 27:41).
  - Job 36:13 gives its source in the heart: "the godless **in heart**".
- **How it co-exists.**
  - **With a murder plan spoken inwardly:** "Esau said to himself, '… then I will kill my brother Jacob'" (Gen 27:41). And Rebekah's reading of it: "your brother Esau **comforts himself** about you by planning to kill you" (27:42). **The anger is kept going by a plan that soothes it.**
  - **With the refusal to cry out:** "they do not cry for help when he binds them" (Job 36:13). Cherished anger shuts off the cry.
  - **Rebekah's movement is the counter-weight:** removing the object (flight) and waiting for the anger's course to end. Her own inner state follows at once: "Why should I be bereft of you both in one day?" (27:45), and "I loathe my life" (27:46).
- **What it leads to.**
  - For Esau: the anger turns away over years, and there is no killing. When they meet, "Esau ran to meet him and embraced him … and they wept" (Gen 33:4). **Rebekah's "until he forgets" comes true, beyond embrace.**
  - For the godless: "They die in youth" (Job 36:14).
  - For the fool: death by his own vexation (Job 5:2).
  - Eliphaz sets this beside "man is born to trouble as the sparks fly upward" (Job 5:7). **Trouble is universal, but dying of vexation is the fool's.**
- **Seat.**
  - **In the verse:** Job 36:13 "heart" (*lev*). **The bearer's own. Genuine.** A v3 §2.1 read it as human anger in the inner seat. G v3 §16.5 read it with 36:14 as death as accountability. Both stand. The phenomenon adds only that **the heart here is where anger is kept, not where it flares.**
  - **Outside the verse:** Gen 27:41 "said to himself" (Hebrew *in his heart*). **Genuine, the bearer's own** (F v4 §8.2, "hatred spoken in the heart, and leaking out"). The seat arrives **three verses before** the anger word, as the place where the plan is spoken.
  - Job 36:18: no seat. Whose wrath it is was settled in §19.

## 4. Anger when one's will is blocked, exposed or refused (TH) — 11 verses

Verses: Gen 30:2; Num 22:27; 24:10; 2Ki 5:11, 5:12; 6:11; 2Ch 16:10; 25:10; 26:19; Est 1:12, 1:18.

- **What it is.** Anger in someone whose **purpose meets resistance**. Four strands emerged, and they work differently.
- **(a) A demand the angry one cannot meet.** Gen 30:2: "Jacob's anger was kindled against Rachel, and he said, 'Am I in the place of God, who has withheld from you the fruit of the womb?'"
  - It comes from Rachel's "Give me children, or I shall die!" (30:1), which itself came from envy: "she envied her sister" (30:1).
  - **The anger answers an impossible demand by naming who alone could meet it.** Rachel then turns to a workaround: "Here is my servant Bilhah" (30:3).
- **(b) Thwarted and made a fool of.**
  - Balaam: "Balaam's anger was kindled, and he struck the donkey with his staff" (Num 22:27). When the donkey speaks, he gives his reason: "Because you have made a fool of me. I wish I had a sword in my hand, for then I would kill you" (22:29). **The anger is about being shamed, and it wishes to kill.**
  - Balak: "Balak's anger was kindled against Balaam, and he struck his hands together … 'I called you to curse my enemies, and behold, you have blessed them these three times'" (Num 24:10). It ends in dismissal and the withholding of honour: "the Lord has held you back from honor" (24:11).
  - Naaman: "Naaman was angry and went away, saying, 'Behold, I thought that he would surely come out to me …'" (2Ki 5:11), and "he turned and went away in a rage" (5:12). **What the anger releases is a disappointed expectation of honour**, quoted as his own thought. Pilot §1.4 read the whole movement: rage → persuasion → obedience → restoration.
  - The Ephraimite army, discharged, "became very angry with Judah and returned home in fierce anger" (2Ch 25:10), and later "raided the cities of Judah" (25:13). **Anger at being sent away returns as violence against those who sent it.**
- **(c) Rebuked by a holy person.**
  - Asa, told by the seer that he "relied on the king of Syria", "was angry with the seer and put him in the stocks … for he was in a rage with him" (2Ch 16:10). The rage spreads: "Asa inflicted cruelties upon some of the people at the same time" (16:10).
  - Uzziah, withstood by the priests at the altar: "Uzziah was angry … and when he became angry with the priests, leprosy broke out on his forehead" (2Ch 26:19).
  - **In both, the anger is against the messenger of a boundary, and the story ends with the angry king's own body struck:** Asa "diseased in his feet … Yet even in his disease he did not seek the Lord" (16:12); Uzziah "a leper to the day of his death" (26:21).
- **(d) Refused by a subordinate, or exposed.**
  - Vashti's refusal: "the king became enraged, and his anger burned within him" (Est 1:12). The counsellor at once generalises the fear to every household: "there will be contempt and wrath in plenty" (1:18). **One refusal is read as a threat to the whole order of husbands**, and answered by decree (1:19–20).
  - The king of Syria, whose plans Elisha keeps disclosing: "the mind of the king of Syria was greatly troubled because of this thing" (2Ki 6:11). He suspects a traitor: "Will you not show me who of us is for the king of Israel?" (6:11). The servant's answer shows how completely he is exposed: Elisha "tells the king of Israel the words that you speak in your bedroom" (6:12).
- **Where it comes from, across the strands.** In every case the resistance comes from **someone who stands under God or for God**: God himself (Rachel's closed womb), the angel (Balaam), God's word (Balak), the prophet (Naaman, the king of Syria, Asa), or the priests (Uzziah). Two exceptions: the discharged army (strand b) and Vashti (strand d).
- **How it co-exists.**
  - **With a prior high state:** Uzziah "when he was strong, he grew proud" (2Ch 26:16), and the king's "heart … merry with wine" (Est 1:10). **Anger comes where a lifted state is checked.** F v4 §7 already read Est 1:10 as "the merry heart as the threshold"; this adds Uzziah.
  - **With striking** (Num 22:27; 24:10, hands; 2Ch 16:10), and with going away (2Ki 5:12; 2Ch 25:10).
- **What it leads to.**
  - Violence against the obstacle: the donkey, the seer in stocks, cities raided.
  - Or **being turned round by the lowly.** The donkey speaks (Num 22:28). Naaman's servants say "My father … will you not do it?" (2Ki 5:13), and he is restored.
  - Or the angry one's own **body struck** (Uzziah, Asa).
- **Seat.**
  - **In the verse:** 2Ki 6:11 "mind" (*lev*). **The bearer's own. Genuine.** A v3 §2.1 read it as "the mind [heart] of the king … troubled [*sāʿar*, storm]". H v1 §1.1 read it as "the most powerful heart is the least its own". Both stand. **New here:** in this phenomenon the heart's storm is **the anger of exposure**. What troubles it is that its bedroom words are known (6:12).
  - **Outside the verse:**
    - **Before:**
      - 2Ch 16:9 "those whose heart is blameless toward him". **Genuine.** It is the seer's diagnosis just before Asa's rage (G v3 §3.4; C v3 §5.1). **The rage is at being told where his heart failed.**
      - 2Ch 26:16 "grew proud" (*gāvah lēv*, the heart high). **Genuine. The bearer's own**, and it comes before the anger (G v3 §9.1; D v2 §9.1).
      - Est 1:10 "heart … merry". **Genuine** (F v4 §7).
    - **Beside:** Num 24:13, Balaam: "to do either good or bad of my own will [*heart*]". **Genuine**, but **the other party's**. Balak's anger meets a heart **subordinated to the word** (D v2 §2.6).
    - 2Ki 5:10, 14 "flesh" is Naaman's skin restored. **Spurious** as a seat (D v2 R3).

## 5. The anger of the one with power, and those beneath it (PW) — 13 verses

Verses: Gen 31:35; 39:19; 40:2; 41:10; 44:18; Judg 9:30; 1Sa 20:7; 29:4; 2Sa 11:20; Est 2:1; 2:21; 7:7; 7:10.

- **What it is.** The anger of a master, king or commander, which **decides other people's fate**, and the **inner work of those beneath it**: reading it, pleading against it, anticipating it, waiting it out.
- **What it does to the one who holds it.** It turns at once into custody or death:
  - Potiphar, hearing his wife's story, "his anger was kindled", and "Joseph's master took him and put him into the prison" (Gen 39:19–20)
  - "Pharaoh was angry with his two officers … and he put them in custody" (Gen 40:2–3; recalled at 41:10)
  - the king "arose in his wrath" and says "Hang him on that" (Est 7:7, 10)

  The pilot's §1.3 point, "no interval in which a seat would be named", holds here.
- **What it does to those beneath it:**
  - **Pleading against it before it rises:**
    - Judah: "let not your anger burn against your servant, for you are like Pharaoh himself" (Gen 44:18). The plea is a long retelling of the father's love (44:19–21 onward).
    - Rachel: "Let not my lord be angry that I cannot rise before you, for the way of women is upon me" (Gen 31:35). **The plea for no anger covers a concealment:** she is sitting on the stolen gods (31:34).
  - **Reading it as a sign:** "If he says, 'Good!' it will be well with your servant, but if he is angry, then know that harm is determined by him" (1Sa 20:7). **Saul's anger is to be read as the evidence of Saul's intent.** Jonathan's knowledge follows the same path: after the spear, "Jonathan knew that his father was determined to put David to death" (20:33).
  - **Anticipating it and scripting a reply:** Joab to the messenger: "if the king's anger rises, and if he says to you, 'Why did you go so near the city?' … then you shall say, 'Your servant Uriah the Hittite is dead also'" (2Sa 11:20–21). **The anger is foreseen, and so is the one sentence that will turn it.** That sentence works because the king had wanted Uriah dead. The line manages the king's anger by exposing the king's own secret.
  - **Plotting against it from below:** "Bigthan and Teresh … became angry and sought to lay hands on King Ahasuerus" (Est 2:21). This is anger rising **from** the servants, discovered through Mordecai (2:22).
  - **Anger acting covertly:** Zebul, "when [he] heard the words of Gaal … his anger was kindled. And he sent messengers to Abimelech secretly" (Judg 9:30–31). **Anger turned into an ambush plan**, "set an ambush in the field" (9:32).
  - **A group overrules its king:** "the commanders of the Philistines were angry with him … 'Send the man back … how could this fellow reconcile himself to his lord?'" (1Sa 29:4). The king Achish yields: "Nevertheless, the lords do not approve of you" (29:6). **The anger comes from suspicion**: they fear a loyalty they cannot see.
- **Where it comes from.** A report heard (Gen 39:19; Judg 9:30), an offence by servants (Gen 40:1–2), or suspicion (1Sa 29:4). **In Gen 39 the anger is kindled by a lie**, and the text lets the reader see that the anger is misdirected.
- **How it co-exists.** **With abating and remembering:** "when the anger of King Ahasuerus had abated, he remembered Vashti and what she had done and what had been decreed against her" (Est 2:1), and "Then the wrath of the king abated" (7:10).
  - **Anger's time-course (§3) shows in the powerful as abating after the act, not before.**
  - **Memory returns once the anger is gone**, and the decree cannot be undone.
- **What it leads to.** Imprisonment (Joseph, the officers), hanging (the baker, Haman, the eunuchs), a friend sent away (David from the Philistines).
  - **In Joseph's story the anger that imprisons also positions.** "The Lord was with Joseph" in the prison (Gen 39:21), and the officers imprisoned by Pharaoh's anger bring about Joseph's release (41:9–13).
- **Seat.**
  - **In the verse:** Est 7:7 "life" (*nephesh*): "Haman stayed to beg for his life". **The other party's. Life-sense only.** C v3 §7.5 read it under "the soul-life hunted". In this phenomenon the seat word names **what the powerful one's anger puts at stake in the one beneath**, as M20 §2's Seat found for the hunted.
  - **Outside the verse:**
    - Est 7:5 "dared" (literally "whose **heart has filled him** to do this"). **The offender's heart**, as the king asks about it. **Genuine. New here** (no batch cites it). The king's wrath two verses later is aimed at the heart that dared.
    - 1Sa 20:4 "Whatever you say [*your soul says*], I will do for you". **Genuine. The other party's** (David's), pledged to by Jonathan. **New here.** It stands three verses before 1Sa 20:7: **Jonathan binds himself to David's soul just before they plan how to read Saul's anger.** This adds to F v4 §1.1 (Jonathan and David) without changing it.
    - Gen 41:8 "his spirit was troubled". **Genuine. The bearer's own** (Pharaoh's), though in a different scene from his anger (A v3 §3.2; B v3 §7). **The same king appears once with an angry act and once with a troubled spirit.** The seat word comes with the trouble, not with the anger.

## 6. The held grievance released (GH) — Gen 31:36; 2Sa 3:8; Job 32:2, 32:3, 32:5

- **What it is.** Anger that **breaks out after a long restraint** and releases a store of grievance in speech.
- **What it does.** It **speaks at length**, and the speech counts years or services:
  - Jacob: "Jacob became angry and berated Laban … 'What is my offense? … These twenty years I have been with you … I bore the loss of it myself'" (Gen 31:36–39).
  - Abner: "Am I a dog's head of Judah? **To this day I keep showing steadfast love** to the house of Saul … And yet you charge me today with a fault concerning a woman" (2Sa 3:8).
  - Elihu burns with anger three times (Job 32:2, 3, 5). Then comes a long speech (32:6 onward).
- **Where it comes from.**
  - **A final provocation** on top of the store: Laban's search (31:33–35), Ish-bosheth's accusation (3:7).
  - For Elihu, **an argument that has failed**: at Job "because he justified himself rather than God", at the friends "because they had found no answer, although they had declared Job to be in the wrong" (32:2–3).
- **How it co-exists.**
  - **With enforced silence first.** "Elihu had waited to speak to Job because they were older than he" (32:4), and "I was timid and afraid to declare my opinion" (32:6). **Fear and deference hold the anger in, until the others fall silent.**
  - **Elihu describes the pressure in bodily terms:** "I am full of words; the spirit within me constrains me. Behold, my belly is like wine that has no vent; like new wineskins ready to burst. I must speak, that I may find relief" (32:18–20). **The held anger is felt as pressure in the belly, and speech is its release.**
- **What it leads to.**
  - Abner: a sworn switch of allegiance, "God do so to Abner … if I do not accomplish for David what the Lord has sworn" (2Sa 3:9). The one who charged him is silenced: "Ish-bosheth could not answer Abner another word, because he feared him" (3:11).
  - Jacob: a demand for judgment, "that they may decide between us two" (Gen 31:37).
  - Elihu: six chapters of speech.
- **Seat.**
  - **In the verse:** Job 32:2 "himself" (*nephesh*): Job "justified himself rather than God". **Genuine, as the reflexive self.** But it is **the other party's** (Job's), not Elihu's. **The anger is at a self that has made itself right** (H v1 §6.2, "the heart as judge of the self").
  - **Outside the verse:**
    - **After**, Job 32:8: "it is the spirit in man, the breath of the Almighty, that makes him understand". **Genuine** (B v3 §1.6). Elihu justifies speaking despite his youth.
    - **Further on**, Job 32:18: "the spirit within me constrains me". **Genuine, the bearer's own. New here** (outside the ±3 window; read in this session). **The seat of Elihu's released anger arrives as a spirit under pressure, with the belly as its bodily image.**

## 7. Indignation at an outrage done to another (OU) — Gen 34:7; Exo 11:8; 1Sa 20:34; 2Sa 12:5; 13:21; Neh 5:6

- **What it is.** Anger **on behalf of someone else** who has been wronged: a sister, a friend, the poor, a people.
- **What it does.**
  - "The men were indignant and very angry, because he had done an outrageous thing in Israel … for such a thing must not be done" (Gen 34:7). **The anger states a norm.**
  - "Jonathan rose from the table in fierce anger and ate no food … **for he was grieved for David**, because his father had disgraced him" (1Sa 20:34).
  - "David's anger was greatly kindled against the man … 'the man who has done this deserves to die'" (2Sa 12:5).
  - "I was very angry when I heard their outcry and these words" (Neh 5:6).
  - "He went out from Pharaoh in hot anger" (Exo 11:8).
- **Where it comes from.** **Hearing or seeing the wrong:**
  - "as soon as they heard of it" (Gen 34:7)
  - "when I heard their outcry" (Neh 5:6)
  - "When King David heard of all these things" (2Sa 13:21)
  - Jonathan seeing the spear thrown at himself (1Sa 20:33)
  - David hearing a parable (2Sa 12:1–4)
- **How it co-exists.**
  - **With grief:** Jonathan's anger is joined to being "grieved for David" (1Sa 20:34). Pilot §1.4 noted "anger, grief and fasting together".
  - **With deliberation:** Neh 5:6 → 5:7, "I took counsel with myself" (pilot §2.1).
  - **With a hidden self-accusation:** David's verdict on the rich man is turned on himself: "You are the man!" (2Sa 12:7). **Indignation at another's outrage becomes the instrument of the angry man's own conviction.**
  - **With inaction:** 2Sa 13:21 says only "he was very angry". Nothing follows from David, and Absalom acts instead. "Absalom hated Amnon" (13:22). "After two full years" (13:23) he orders, "Mark when Amnon's heart is merry with wine … then kill him" (13:28). **The verse sets the king's anger that does nothing beside the brother's hatred that waits.** Read further on (2Sa 13:39): "the spirit of the king longed to go out to Absalom, because he was comforted about Amnon, since he was dead." **The king's anger passes into longing for the killer.**
- **What it leads to.**
  - Public action: Nehemiah's "great assembly" and charge (Neh 5:7–9).
  - Vengeance with deceit: the brothers "answered Shechem … deceitfully" (Gen 34:13), and "Simeon and Levi … took their swords and came against the city while it felt secure and killed all the males" (34:25). This is the deed Jacob's curse names in §10.
  - Self-conviction: David.
  - Nothing: David at 13:21.
  - Fasting: Jonathan.
- **Seat.**
  - **In the verse:** none of the six.
  - **Outside the verse:**
    - Neh 5:7 "I took counsel with myself [*my heart*]". **Genuine. The bearer's own. It arrives in the next verse, with the deliberation** (pilot §2.1; B v3 §7; E v2 §7.4; C v3 §5.4).
    - Gen 34:8 "The soul of my son Shechem longs for your daughter". **Genuine, but the offender's** (C v3 §1.1). **The indignation of the brothers and the desire of the offender's soul stand side by side in consecutive verses.**
    - Exo 11:10 "the Lord hardened Pharaoh's heart". **Genuine. The offender's heart, hardened. New here** (no batch cites it). Moses's hot anger (11:8) is set between God's announcement and the heart that will not yield.
    - 2Sa 13:20 "do not take this to heart" (Absalom to Tamar). **Genuine. The victim's, told to suppress** (B v3 §0; E v2 R7). 2Sa 13:39 "spirit of the king". **Genuine. The bearer's own**, the angry king's spirit after the killing, turned to longing (G v3 §16.6; H v1 §1.3, "the Absalom arc"). **New here is only the link back to the anger at 13:21.**
    - 2Sa 12:3, 8 "arms" (*chêq*, bosom): the lamb "lie in his arms", and "your master's wives into your arms". **Relational and bodily, not inner** (D v2 R6; E v2 R8). The parable's bosom and David's bosom are the same word: **that is how the parable catches him.** This is noted as a link, not as a seat.
    - Neh 5:5 "our flesh is as the flesh of our brothers" is **kinship-sense.** **Spurious** as a seat (H v1 §1.10 fringe).

## 8. Vexation suffered: the provoked one (VX) — Gen 40:6; 1Sa 1:6, 1:7, 1:16; Job 6:2; 17:7

- **What it is.** Here the M02 word names **what is done to a person, or what a person bears**, not the anger they show. It is *ka'as* as the sufferer's vexation, and *za'aph* as a downcast state.
- **What it does.**
  - It **wears down over time**: "her rival used to provoke her grievously to irritate her … So it went on year by year" (1Sa 1:6–7).
  - It **stops eating and brings weeping**: "Therefore Hannah wept and would not eat" (1:7).
  - It **dims the eye and wastes the body**: "My eye has grown dim from vexation, and all my members are like a shadow" (Job 17:7).
  - It **cannot be weighed**: "Oh that my vexation were weighed … it would be heavier than the sand of the sea; therefore my words have been rash" (Job 6:2–3).
  - It **shows in the face**: Joseph "saw that they were troubled", and asks "Why are your faces downcast today?" (Gen 40:6–7).
- **Where it comes from.**
  - A rival's provocation, "because the Lord had closed her womb" (1Sa 1:6)
  - calamity and God's "arrows" (Job 6:2, 4)
  - being made "a byword of the peoples" (Job 17:6)
  - dreams with "no one to interpret them" (Gen 40:8)
- **How it co-exists.**
  - **With the sufferer's own speech that explains it.** Hannah: "I am a woman troubled in spirit … I have been pouring out my soul before the Lord … I have been speaking out of my great anxiety and vexation" (1Sa 1:15–16). Job: "therefore my words have been rash" (Job 6:3). **The vexation is given as the reason the sufferer's speech looks the way it does**: drunk to Eli, rash to the friends.
  - **With misreading by the onlooker:** Eli takes Hannah "to be a drunken woman" (1:13–14).
- **What it leads to.**
  - Resolution through a word given: Eli's "Go in peace", and then "the woman went her way and ate, and her face was no longer sad" (1Sa 1:17–18). **The body's signs reverse**: she eats, and her face changes.
  - Or a plea to be allowed to die: "that it would please God to crush me, that he would let loose his hand and cut me off!" (Job 6:9).
  - Or interpretation: Joseph's "Please tell them to me" (Gen 40:8).
- **Seat.**
  - **In the verse:** none of the six.
  - **Outside the verse**, dense in 1Sa 1:
    - 1Sa 1:8 "why is your heart sad?". **Genuine. The bearer's own, named by the husband. New here** (no batch cites it).
    - 1Sa 1:10 "deeply distressed [*bitter of soul*]" (A v3 §3.1; E v2 §1.1).
    - 1Sa 1:13 "speaking in her heart" (B v3 §3.4; E v2 §1.1; G v3 §8.2).
    - 1Sa 1:15 "troubled in spirit … pouring out my soul" (pilot §2.1, §3).

    **All genuine. All the bearer's own.** The sequence runs: **named by another (1:8) → the narrator (1:10) → the heart as the place of voiceless prayer (1:13) → named by herself (1:15).** The M02 word comes last (1:16), in her own summary.
  - Job 6:4 "my spirit drinks their poison". **Genuine. The bearer's own, as receiver** (A v3 §1; pilot §2). The vexation of 6:2 is located in the next-but-one verse as a spirit poisoned by God's arrows. That verse is unit 2's (divine), and is cited here only as the seat of Job's vexation.
  - Job 6:11 "that I should be patient" (literally *lengthen my nephesh*). **Genuine. The bearer's own** (G v3 §3.5; C v3 §5.2). **New here is only the pairing with vexation: vexation shortens the soul.** Compare "slow to anger" = "long of nostrils" (pilot §2.2).
  - Job 17:4 "you have closed their hearts to understanding". **Genuine, but the friends'** (B v3 §1.3).

## 9. Anger turned on oneself (SS) — Gen 45:5; Job 18:4

- **What it is.** Anger whose object is **the self**.
- **What it does.**
  - Joseph to his brothers: "do not be distressed or angry **with yourselves** because you sold me here" (Gen 45:5). **The brothers' self-directed anger is expected**, and Joseph forbids it.
  - Bildad to Job: "You who **tear yourself** in your anger" (Job 18:4). **Anger that wounds its bearer.**
- **Where it comes from.**
  - Joseph's brothers: guilt, now uncovered. "They were dismayed at his presence" (45:3), and "I am your brother, Joseph, whom you sold" (45:4).
  - Job: his protest, as Bildad hears it.
- **How it co-exists.** **With a reframing that removes its ground.** "For God sent me before you to preserve life … So it was not you who sent me here, but God" (45:5, 8).
  - Joseph does not deny the act ("you sold me"). He **re-assigns its meaning**, and the anger at oneself is released by the new meaning.
  - Bildad offers the opposite of a reframing: the world will not change for Job, "shall the earth be forsaken for you, or the rock be removed?" (18:4).
- **What it leads to.** Joseph: reconciliation, and a summons to the father (45:9). Job: Bildad goes on to the fate of the wicked (18:5–7).
- **Seat.**
  - **In the verse:** Job 18:4 "yourself" (*nephesh*). **The bearer's own. Genuine, as the self that anger harms** (A v3 §2.1; pilot §2).
  - Gen 45:5 "with yourselves" is **literally "in your eyes"** [lexical, not in DB]. It is not tagged as a seat in the DB. [verify] if the researcher wants the Hebrew checked.
  - **Outside the verse:** no seat in Gen 45:2–9 or Job 18:1–7.

## 10. Anger cursed, its company refused (CC) — Gen 49:6, 49:7

- **What it is.** A **judgment on anger by a third party**: Jacob on his sons Simeon and Levi.
- **What it does.**
  - **It separates the speaker's self from the angry:** "Let my soul come not into their council; O my glory, be not joined to their company" (49:6).
  - **It curses the anger, not the men:** "Cursed be their anger, for it is fierce, and their wrath, for it is cruel!" (49:7).
- **Where it comes from.** The deed: "in their anger they killed men, and in their willfulness they hamstrung oxen" (49:6). The reader carries Gen 34 (§7) here. That link is the reader's, not the verse's.
- **How it co-exists.** **With violence named as the brothers' very identity:** "weapons of violence are their swords" (49:5).
- **What it leads to.** Scattering: "I will divide them in Jacob and scatter them in Israel" (49:7).
- **Seat.**
  - **In the verse:** Gen 49:6 "soul" (*nephesh*), and "glory" (*kāvôd*) in parallel. **Genuine. The speaker's own**, not the angry ones'. **The seat is the self that withdraws from the anger** (A v3 §2.1, "Soul withdrawing from others' anger"; E v2 §3.2, *kāvôd* = the self; E v2 §7.3, fellowship).
  - **Outside the verse:** none in 49:3–10.

## 11. The leader's anger at those he leads (LD) — Exo 16:20; 32:19; 32:22; Lev 10:16; Num 16:15; 31:14; 2Ki 13:19

- **What it is.** Anger in the one responsible for a people, a priesthood or a king, **at their failure to do what was commanded**.
- **What it does.**
  - It **flares at the sight**: "as soon as he came near the camp and saw the calf and the dancing, Moses' anger burned hot" (Exo 32:19).
  - It **breaks what was given**: "he threw the tablets … and broke them" (32:19).
  - It **destroys the object**: the calf "burned … ground it to powder … made the people of Israel drink it" (32:20).
  - It **rebukes by question**: "Why have you not eaten the sin offering …?" (Lev 10:17); "Have you let all the women live?" (Num 31:15).
  - It **states the limit that was missed**: "You should have struck five or six times … now you will strike down Syria only three times" (2Ki 13:19).
- **Where it comes from.** Disobedience to a given word:
  - "they did not listen to Moses" (Exo 16:20)
  - the calf (32:19)
  - the sin offering burned instead of eaten (Lev 10:16)
  - the women of Midian spared "on Balaam's advice" (Num 31:14–16)
  - the king striking only three times (2Ki 13:18)
  - rebels refusing to come: "We will not come up" (Num 16:12–14)
- **How it co-exists.**
  - **With appeal to God** against those who resist: "Moses was very angry and said to the Lord, 'Do not respect their offering. I have not taken one donkey from them'" (Num 16:15). **The anger is carried straight into a plea of innocence before God.**
  - **With the one beneath deflecting it:** Aaron, "Let not the anger of my lord burn hot. You know the people, that they are set on evil … I threw it into the fire, and out came this calf" (Exo 32:22–24). **Deflection by blaming the people and making the act passive.**
  - **With being answered:** Aaron's reply to the Lev 10 rebuke ("such things as these have happened to me! If I had eaten the sin offering today, would the Lord have approved?", 10:19) meets "And when Moses heard that, he approved" (10:20). **The leader's anger yields to a reason.**
- **What it leads to.** Destruction of the object (Exo 32:20), purge and execution (Num 31:17), a lesser victory (2Ki 13:19), or **approval once the explanation is heard** (Lev 10:20).
- **Seat.** None in the seven verses or their ±3 context. **The leader's anger is carried by the act and the question.** Pilot §3 items 1 and 3 read this as act and speech.

## 12. Quarrelling with God: Meribah (MB) — Exo 17:7; Num 20:13, 20:24; 27:14; Deu 32:51

- **What it is.** A **quarrel of the people with God**, stored in a place name. In the later verses it is also **the leaders' failure at the same place**.
- **What it does.**
  - It **tests God with a question**: "they tested the Lord by saying, 'Is the Lord among us or not?'" (Exo 17:7).
  - It **nearly turns on the leader**: "They are almost ready to stone me" (17:4).
  - In the Numbers and Deuteronomy verses, Meribah is where the leaders themselves "rebelled against my command" (Num 20:24), "failing to uphold me as holy … before their eyes" (27:14), and "broke faith with me" (Deu 32:51).
- **Where it comes from.** Thirst, and doubt of God's presence (17:1–7). For Moses and Aaron, "Because you did not believe in me" (Num 20:12). **The people's quarrel is answered in the leaders by unbelief**, and the leaders' act at the rock (20:10–11: "Hear now, you rebels", striking twice) takes the quarrel into themselves.
- **How it co-exists.**
  - **With naming:** the place becomes a memory. "He called the name of the place Massah and Meribah" (Exo 17:7), and "These are the waters of Meribah" (Num 20:13).
  - **With death sentences** on the leaders, repeated at every mention: Aaron "shall not enter the land" (20:24), Moses "gathered to your people" (27:13), "die on the mountain" (Deu 32:50).
- **What it leads to.** The leaders' exclusion from the land, and **a request for a successor**: "Let the Lord, the God of the spirits of all flesh, appoint a man over the congregation … that the congregation of the Lord may not be as sheep that have no shepherd" (Num 27:16–17).
- **Seat.**
  - **In the verse:** none of the five.
  - **Outside the verse:**
    - Num 27:16 "the God of the spirits of all flesh". **Genuine, a human spirit, as known to God. New here.** At the point where Meribah costs Moses the land, he appeals to God as **the one who knows every human spirit** and can choose the next leader.
    - Num 27:11 "kinsman" (*she'er*, flesh). **Kinship-sense. Spurious.**
  - **Cross-reference:** M20 v3 §13 (grumbling) shares the setting, the wilderness complaint. Meribah is **grumbling turned into contention with God.** This is a pointer only.

## 13. Anger at what God has done (AG) — Num 11:10; 1Sa 15:11; 2Sa 6:8; 1Ch 13:11

- **What it is.** A human's anger or displeasure **whose occasion is an act or decision of God**.
- **What it does.**
  - "David was angry **because the Lord had broken out against Uzzah**" (2Sa 6:8 = 1Ch 13:11).
  - "the anger of the Lord blazed hotly, and Moses was displeased" (Num 11:10).
  - "'I regret that I have made Saul king' … And Samuel was angry, and he cried to the Lord all night" (1Sa 15:11).
- **Where it comes from.** From God's anger or regret **falling on something the human was carrying**: the ark procession, the people Moses must bear, the king Samuel anointed.
- **How it co-exists.** **With fear, straight after:** "David was afraid of the Lord that day, and he said, 'How can the ark of the Lord come to me?'" (2Sa 6:9; 1Ch 13:12). **Anger at God and fear of God follow one another in consecutive verses.** A new pairing, not read in the batches.
  - **With complaint to God:** Moses, "Why have you dealt ill with your servant? … Did I conceive all this people? … 'Carry them in your bosom'" (Num 11:11–12).
  - **With all-night crying:** Samuel (1Sa 15:11). Later, Samuel "grieved over Saul" (15:35). **Anger turns to crying out, and then to grief.**
- **What it leads to.**
  - **Withdrawal from what God struck:** David will not take the ark home (2Sa 6:10). **The anger ends in avoidance**, and the ark's blessing goes to Obed-edom instead (6:11).
  - **A burden shared:** Moses (H v1 §3.3, "Moses' burden, then the Spirit shared", Num 11:10–17).
  - **Obedience with grief:** Samuel goes to meet Saul the next morning (15:12).
- **Seat.**
  - **In the verse:** none.
  - **Outside the verse:** Num 11:12 "Carry them in your bosom, as a nurse carries a nursing child". **The bearer's own, relational and bodily.** Moses's displeasure (11:10) comes out as a protest about **the bosom he is asked to carry the people in** (E v2 data note). Num 11:13 "meat" is **spurious**.
  - **The unit-2 side** of these four verses is God's own anger (Num 11:10; 2Sa 6:7; 1Ch 13:10), for unit 2.

## 14. Who may pronounce anger (JV) — Num 23:7, 23:8; Job 19:29; 40:11

- **What it is.** Anger as a **verdict or curse**, and the question of **who has the right to pronounce it**.
- **What it does.**
  - Balaam **cannot** pronounce it: "How can I curse whom God has not cursed? How can I denounce whom the Lord has not denounced?" (Num 23:8). **Human denunciation is shown to be derivative: it cannot go beyond God's.**
  - God **offers it ironically to Job**: "Pour out the overflowings of your anger, and look on everyone who is proud and abase him" (Job 40:11). This comes inside the challenge, "Have you an arm like God …?" (40:9), and "Then will I also acknowledge to you that your own right hand can save you" (40:14). **Anger that abases the proud belongs to the one who can save by his own right hand.**
  - Job warns his friends that **their own wrath is answerable**: "be afraid of the sword, for wrath brings the punishment of the sword, that you may know there is a judgment" (Job 19:29). This comes after "If you say, 'How we will pursue him!'" (19:28).
- **Where it comes from.**
  - For Balaam, **a hired commission**: "Come, curse Jacob for me, and come, denounce Israel!" (Num 23:7).
  - For the friends, **pursuit of a sufferer.**
- **How it co-exists.**
  - **With a turned wish:** the hired curser ends by wishing for the end of those he was hired to curse, "Let me die the death of the upright" (Num 23:10; G v3 §16.2; H v1 §6.5).
  - **With Job's own hope, just before:** "yet in my flesh I shall see God … My heart faints within me!" (Job 19:26–27).
- **What it leads to.** Blessing instead of curse (Num 23:11). The judgment turned back on the pursuers (Job 19:29).
- **Seat.**
  - **In the verse:** none.
  - **Outside the verse:**
    - Num 23:10 "Let me [*my nephesh*] die the death of the upright". **Genuine. The would-be pronouncer's own** (G v3 §16.2).
    - Job 19:27 "My heart [*kidneys*] faints within me". **Genuine. The bearer's own**, Job's, just before his warning. **New here.** Job 19:26 "in my flesh" is **bodily**, the flesh in which he will see God.

## 15. Anger that comes with God's Spirit or zeal, and anger measured against God's (SP) — Num 25:11; Judg 14:19; 1Sa 11:6; 2Ch 28:9

- **What it is.** Human anger that **the text relates to God's own**: given by the Spirit, shared as God's jealousy, or found to exceed God's commission.
- **What it does.**
  - **Given by the Spirit:** "the Spirit of God rushed upon Saul when he heard these words, and his anger was greatly kindled" (1Sa 11:6); "the Spirit of the Lord rushed upon him … In hot anger he went back to his father's house" (Judg 14:19).
  - **Shared as God's jealousy:** Phinehas "has turned back my wrath from the people of Israel, **in that he was jealous with my jealousy among them**" (Num 25:11). **A human's zeal is counted as God's own, and it stops God's wrath.**
  - **Exceeding God's commission:** "because the Lord … was angry with Judah, he gave them into your hand, **but you have killed them in a rage that has reached up to heaven**" (2Ch 28:9). **Human rage goes beyond the divine anger it served, and becomes its own guilt:** "Have you not sins of your own …?" (28:10); "the fierce wrath of the Lord is upon you" (28:11).
- **Where it comes from.**
  - Hearing (1Sa 11:5–6: "they told him the news of the men of Jabesh").
  - Betrayal (Judg 14:16–18: the riddle given away through his wife).
  - Seeing: an Israelite brought a Midianite woman "in the sight of Moses and … the whole congregation … while they were weeping" (Num 25:6). "When Phinehas … saw it, he rose … and took a spear" (25:7), and killed them both (25:8).
  - Victory in battle (2Ch 28:6–8).
- **How it co-exists.**
  - **Directions of flow:** Batch A §2.4 read 1Sa 11:5–7 as "hearing → Spirit → anger → act → dread spreading to the people", in contrast with 1Sa 18:8–10: "jealous anger → a harmful spirit → a spear". Both stand. **New here:** Judg 14:19 runs **Spirit → killing → hot anger afterwards**. The anger is **the aftermath of a Spirit-driven act**, not its cause, and it comes out as going home, not as more violence.
  - **Saul's anger ends in mercy that same day:** "Not a man shall be put to death this day, for today the Lord has worked salvation" (1Sa 11:13).
- **What it leads to.**
  - A people united (1Sa 11:7).
  - A covenant of peace for Phinehas (Num 25:12–13).
  - **Captives released and cared for**, after the rebuke of rage: "they clothed them, gave them sandals, provided them with food and drink, and anointed them … brought them to their kinsfolk" (2Ch 28:15). **Rage beyond commission is answered by a reversal toward kin**, which links to §2.
  - Samson's withdrawal to his father's house, and the loss of his wife (Judg 14:20).
- **Seat.**
  - **In the verse:**
    - Judg 14:19 and 1Sa 11:6 "Spirit" (*ruach*). **Divine, the other party's, received by the human.** The human anger follows in the same verse (A v3 §2.4 [D1]; B v3 §3.3 for "what is heard moves the inner seat"; G v3 R-V).
    - Num 25:11 and 2Ch 28:9 have no seat.
  - **The seat-sense question these verses raise:** where the Spirit is the seat word, **the seat is God's, and the anger is the human's.** This matches M20 v3 §20's Eze 39:29 row: "God's, received by Israel: the human side is the receiving".

## 16. Provocation among Israel, God and the nations (SM) — Deu 32:21, 32:27, 32:33

- **What it is.** In the Song of Moses, **provocation passes in a circle**: Israel provokes God, God provokes Israel through the nations, and the enemy's provocation and venom are described.
- **What it does.**
  - Measure for measure: "They have made me jealous with what is no god; they have provoked me to anger with their idols. **So I will make them jealous with those who are no people; I will provoke them to anger with a foolish nation**" (Deu 32:21). **Human anger is induced by God as the mirror of the anger they induced in him.**
  - The enemy's taunt: "had I not feared provocation by the enemy, lest their adversaries should misunderstand, lest they should say, 'Our hand is triumphant, it was not the Lord who did all this'" (32:27). The enemy's *ka'as* is **a misreading of events**, claiming God's work as their own.
  - The enemy's nature: "their wine is the poison of serpents and the cruel venom of asps" (32:33).
- **Where it comes from.** Idolatry (32:16–21), and the enemy's lack of understanding: "a nation void of counsel, and there is no understanding in them" (32:28).
- **How it co-exists.** **With understanding withheld:** "If they were wise, they would understand this; they would discern their latter end!" (32:29).
- **What it leads to.** God's restraint because of the enemy's likely boast (32:26–27), and vengeance kept "in store" (32:34–35).
- **Seat.** None in the verses or ±3. The human inner operations here (being made jealous and angry, misunderstanding) are carried by the verbs and by "understanding" (32:28–29).
  - **Pointer:** Rom 10:19 quotes Deu 32:21 and belongs to part 2.

## 17. Anger at a rival's rise (RV) — 1Sa 17:28; 18:8; 20:30; Neh 4:1; 4:7; Est 3:5; 5:9

- **What it is.** Anger at **another person's gain in standing**, or at their refusal to give honour.
- **What it does.**
  - It **reads the rival's heart as evil**: Eliab, "I know your presumption and the evil of your heart, for you have come down to see the battle" (1Sa 17:28).
  - It **reckons the rival's future**: Saul, "They have ascribed to David ten thousands, and to me they have ascribed thousands, and what more can he have but the kingdom?" (18:8).
  - It **attacks one's own son as the rival's ally**: "You son of a perverse, rebellious woman … as long as the son of Jesse lives … neither you nor your kingdom shall be established" (20:30–31).
  - It **jeers**: Sanballat "was angry and greatly enraged, and he jeered at the Jews … 'What are these feeble Jews doing?'" (Neh 4:1–2).
  - It **enlarges its target**: Haman "disdained to lay hands on Mordecai alone … sought to destroy all the Jews" (Est 3:6).
- **Where it comes from.** **Seeing or hearing the rival's rise or refusal:**
  - the women's song (1Sa 18:7)
  - David's question in the camp (17:26–28)
  - "when Sanballat heard that we were building" (Neh 4:1), and "that the breaches were beginning to be closed" (4:7)
  - "when Haman saw that Mordecai did not bow down" (Est 3:5), and "that he neither rose nor trembled before him" (5:9)
- **How it co-exists.**
  - **With surveillance:** "Saul eyed David from that day on" (1Sa 18:9).
  - **With joy that flips.** Haman "went out that day joyful and glad of heart. But when Haman saw Mordecai … he was filled with wrath" (Est 5:9). Then he goes home and recounts "the splendor of his riches" (5:11). A v3 §4.4 read this ("Haman's swing"), and H v1 §2.1 read it as "wealth does not satisfy a heart set on a grievance". Both stand.
  - **With restraint that is only postponement:** "Haman restrained himself and went home" (5:10).
  - **With plotting:** "they all plotted together to come and fight" (Neh 4:8).
- **What it leads to.**
  - The spear, twice (1Sa 18:11; 20:33).
  - A plan of genocide (Est 3:6–8).
  - Armed plotting (Neh 4:8).
  - On the other side, **prayer and a guard** (Neh 4:9), and building on regardless: "the people had a mind to work" (4:6).
- **Seat.**
  - **In the verse:**
    - 1Sa 17:28 "heart" (*levav*). **Genuine, but the rival's heart as the angry man reads it, and misread** (D v2 §2.8, §9.5: "a misreading of another's heart"). The angry man's own seat is not named.
    - Est 5:9 "heart" (*lev*). **Genuine. The bearer's own, in the state just before the anger** (A v3 §4.4; H v1 §2.1; C v3 §1.6; pilot §2.1).
  - **Outside the verse:**
    - 1Sa 18:10 "a harmful spirit from God rushed upon Saul". **The other party's (God's) sending, received by Saul, the day after the anger** (A v3 §2.4 contrast; F v4 §14).

The heart of the rival party**, the builders. **New here.** **Between the enemy's two outbursts (4:1, 4:7) stands the builders' heart set on the work.** The angry party has no seat named, and the ones they are angry at do. **What the map shows** (for these 93 verses only):
1. **Only four of the ten in-verse seats belong to the angry person,** and in two of those the seat is **before** the anger (Est 5:9) or its **object** (Job 18:4). **The only verse where the angry person's own heart holds the anger is Job 36:13**, cherished anger, and it is a **wisdom** saying, not a narrative.
2. **In the narratives, the seat of human anger is almost always found by context, and usually in someone else's words.**
   - God's question (Gen 4:6)
   - Jezebel's (1Ki 21:5)
   - Elkanah's (1Sa 1:8)
   - the seer's diagnosis (2Ch 16:9)
   - the king's question about who "dared" (Est 7:5)

   **Where the angry person names his own seat, it comes after the anger, as deliberation or pressure.** Nehemiah's "I took counsel with my heart" (Neh 5:7), Elihu's "the spirit within me constrains me" (Job 32:18), Hannah's "troubled in spirit" (1Sa 1:15).
3. **The seat of the other party is named at the point of contrast** (compare M20 v3 §20 point 4):
   - the builders' "mind to work" between Sanballat's two outbursts (Neh 4:6)
   - Balaam's heart "subordinated to the word" against Balak's anger (Num 24:13)
   - Jacob's soul withdrawing from his sons' anger (Gen 49:6)
4. **A lifted state of heart often comes just before a check turns into anger:**
   - the heart merry with wine (Est 1:10)
   - the heart high in strength (2Ch 26:16)
   - glad of heart (Est 5:9)
