## 1. Asking once more, aware of the anger (ON) — Gen 18:30, 18:32; Judg 6:39

- **What it is.** A person who has already asked of God asks **again**, and prefaces the new request with a plea that God not be angry at the asking.
- **What it does.**
  - Abraham: "Oh let not the Lord be angry, and I will speak. Suppose thirty are found there" (Gen 18:30), and at the last: "Oh let not the Lord be angry, and I will speak again but this once. Suppose ten" (18:32).
  - Gideon, after the fleece was wet: "Let not your anger burn against me; let me speak just once more. Please let me test just once more with the fleece" (Judg 6:39).
  - **The same form appears in two different kinds of asking.** Abraham asks for a city he does not belong to. Gideon asks for assurance for himself.
- **Where it comes from.** From **knowing the distance** and asking anyway. Abraham says twice, "I have undertaken to speak to the Lord, I who am but dust and ashes" (Gen 18:27; 18:31). Gideon has already had his sign (Judg 6:38). **The asker knows that the request presses on God's patience.**
- **How it co-exists.**
  - **Boldness and restraint in one sentence.** The plea not to anger does not stop the asking. It is what **allows** the asking.
  - **Every request is granted, and no anger comes.** "I will not do it, if I find thirty there" (Gen 18:30); "God did so that night" (Judg 6:40). **The anger feared never appears in either passage.**
- **What it leads to.** The asker stops. Abraham stops at ten and "returned to his place" (Gen 18:33). **The asker, not God, sets the limit** ("but this once").
- **Seat.** None in-verse or in ±3. The self-description "dust and ashes" (Gen 18:27) does the seat's work: **the asker places himself before God as creature**. Job repents "in dust and ashes" (Job 42:6), read in §25 of this document. **Pointer only:** the phrase links the bold asker and the humbled sufferer.

## 2. God's anger at Moses (MS, B6) — Exo 4:14; Deu 1:37; 3:26; 4:21

- **What it is.** God's anger falling on **the one closest to him**, and how that man carries it.
- **What it does.** Two different occasions.
  - **At the call:** "Oh, my Lord, please send someone else." "Then the anger of the Lord was kindled against Moses" (Exo 4:13–14). **The anger is answered at once by provision:** Aaron "is coming out to meet you, and when he sees you, he will be glad in his heart" (4:14); "I will be with your mouth and with his mouth" (4:15).
  - **At the end:** "Even with me the Lord was angry on your account and said, 'You also shall not go in there'" (Deu 1:37); "the Lord was angry with me because of you and would not listen to me … 'Enough from you; do not speak to me of this matter again'" (3:26); "I must die in this land; I must not go over the Jordan" (4:21–22).
- **Where it comes from.**
  - Exo 4: **the servant's refusal of the call**.
  - Deuteronomy: **"on your account" / "because of you"**, three times (Deu 1:37; 3:26; 4:21). Moses traces the anger at himself to the people. (The narrative cause, Meribah, is U1 §12.) **The leader bears God's anger as the people's leader, and says so.**
- **How it co-exists.**
  - **The plea refused, and something else given instead.** Moses "pleaded with the Lord" (Deu 3:23–25). The answer was "Enough", then "lift up your eyes … and look at it with your eyes" (3:27). **The land is given to the eye, not the foot.**
  - **The anger is followed each time by a charge to hand on:** "Encourage him" (Deu 1:38); "charge Joshua, and encourage and strengthen him" (3:28). In Exo 4 a partner is given, and in Deuteronomy a successor. **The anger does not end the man's service. It reshapes it.**
- **What it leads to.** Moses **speaks of his exclusion to the people as a warning to them**. Deu 4:21–22 is followed at once by "Take care, lest you forget the covenant" (4:23) and "the Lord your God is a consuming fire" (4:24). **His own bearing of the anger becomes his teaching.**
- **Seat.**
  - **In the verse:** Exo 4:14 "glad in his heart". **The other party's (Aaron's). Genuine.** Already read in G v3 §13.2 ("the heart goes out to meet"). **Read from the M02 side, it shows that the anger at Moses is met with a glad heart coming towards him.**
  - **Outside the verse:** none in Deu 1:34–40; 3:23–29; 4:18–25.

## 3. God's anger on the side of the one who cries or obeys (DL) — Exo 15:7; 22:24; 23:22; 2Sa 22:8

- **What it is.** God's anger **directed at the enemy or oppressor** of a human, seen from the side of the one it protects.
- **What it does.**
  - **Sung by the rescued:** "you send out your fury; it consumes them like stubble" (Exo 15:7).
  - **Promised to the obedient:** "if you carefully obey his voice … then I will be an enemy to your enemies and an adversary to your adversaries" (Exo 23:22).
  - **Released by the cry of the weak:** "If you do mistreat them, and they cry out to me, I will surely hear their cry, and my wrath will burn" (Exo 22:23–24).
  - **Released by the psalmist's call:** "In my distress I called upon the Lord … my cry came to his ears. Then the earth reeled … because he was angry" (2Sa 22:7–8).
- **Where it comes from.** From **the human cry**: the widow's and orphan's (Exo 22:23), the poor debtor's (22:27: "if he cries to me, I will hear, for I am compassionate"), David's "in my distress" (2Sa 22:7). **In two of the four verses, the human cry is what precedes the anger.**
- **How it co-exists.**
  - **The enemy's own appetite.** In the song, the enemy boasts, "my desire [*nephesh*] shall have its fill of them" (Exo 15:9), and is answered by the wind of God (15:10). **The enemy's appetite and God's fury meet over the same people.** Already read in F v4 §8.4 ("the enemy's soul and the pursued soul").
  - **The same anger threatens the one it protects, if roles reverse.** Exo 22:24 is said to Israel about **their own** widows: "I will kill you with the sword, and your wives shall become widows". **God's anger is for the weak, whoever the oppressor is.**
- **What it leads to.** Praise (Exo 15), or deliverance (2Sa 22:17 onward, not read here).
- **Seat.** None in-verse. **Outside:** Exo 15:9 *nephesh* (the enemy's, appetite-sense) and 15:8, 10 "blast", "wind", "heart of the sea" (not inner-being). F v4 §8.4 covers 15:9. **Nothing new.**

## 4. Intercession: standing before the anger for others (IN) — Exo 32:10, 32:11, 32:12; Num 16:22; Deu 9:18, 9:19, 9:20

- **What it is.** A human who **places himself between God's anger and the people it threatens**, and argues with God to turn it.
- **What it does.**
  - **God himself leaves the door open:** "Now therefore let me alone, that my wrath may burn hot against them" (Exo 32:10). **The anger waits on whether Moses will let God alone.**
  - Moses argues. He argues from **ownership**: "why does your wrath burn hot against your people, whom you have brought out" (32:11). He argues from **God's reputation**: "Why should the Egyptians say, 'With evil intent did he bring them out'?" (32:12). He argues from **promise**: "Remember Abraham, Isaac, and Israel" (32:13).
  - He argues from **justice between the one and the many**: "O God, the God of the spirits of all flesh, shall one man sin, and will you be angry with all the congregation?" (Num 16:22).
  - **With the body:** "I lay prostrate before the Lord … forty days and forty nights. I neither ate bread nor drank water" (Deu 9:18).
  - **For the particular man:** "the Lord was so angry with Aaron that he was ready to destroy him. And I prayed for Aaron also" (Deu 9:20).
- **Where it comes from.** From **fear**. "For I was afraid of the anger and hot displeasure that the Lord bore against you" (Deu 9:19). **The intercessor's own fear of God's anger drives the plea for others.** It does not stop it.
- **How it co-exists.**
  - **Intercession sits alongside the intercessor's own anger.** The same chapter has Moses' own anger breaking the tablets (Exo 32:19; U1 §11 LD; Deu 9:17). **Moses is angry at the people and pleads for them against God's anger, within one episode.**
  - **It is answered:** "the Lord relented" (Exo 32:14); "the Lord listened to me that time also" (Deu 9:19).
- **What it leads to.** The people are spared. In Deuteronomy, the memory of the intercession **is itself turned into instruction** (Deu 9:25–29, "So I lay prostrate …"), set against the people's claim to righteousness (§8).
- **Seat.**
  - **In the verse:** Num 16:22 "spirits of all flesh". **God's relation to every human spirit, given as the ground of the plea. Genuine** (D v2 §3.4: "God's relation to every spirit is the ground of the plea against collective punishment"). **Nothing new.**
  - **Outside:** none in Exo 32:7–15; Deu 9:15–22.
  - **Pointer:** the fasting body (Deu 9:18) **does the seat's work** here, as the face and bed do in U1 §1.

## 5. "Slow to anger": the name proclaimed, pleaded back, recited (SA) — Exo 34:6; Num 14:18; Neh 9:17

- **What it is.** God's self-description, "slow to anger" (*erek appayim*, "long of nostrils", pilot §2.2), as **received by humans and used by them**.
- **What it does.** Three moments:
  1. **Proclaimed and received:** "The Lord, the Lord, a God merciful and gracious, slow to anger" (Exo 34:6). Moses "quickly bowed his head toward the earth and worshiped" (34:8).
  2. **Pleaded back:** Moses quotes it to God word for word as his argument: "'The Lord is slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love …' Please pardon the iniquity of this people" (Num 14:18–19). **It is answered:** "I have pardoned, according to your word" (14:20).
  3. **Recited in confession, as history:** "they stiffened their neck and appointed a leader to return to their slavery in Egypt. But you are a God ready to forgive, gracious and merciful, slow to anger" (Neh 9:17). **The name is now told as what God has shown himself to be through Israel's whole story.**
- **Where it comes from.** From **God's own mouth first** (Exo 34:6). Every human use goes back to that proclamation.
- **How it co-exists.**
  - **The name is used at once, in the next breath, to ask:** "pardon our iniquity and our sin" (Exo 34:9), with "for it is a stiff-necked people". **The name and the people's stiff neck are put side by side by Moses himself.** The same pairing returns in Neh 9:16–17.
  - **The name includes its limit:** "but who will by no means clear the guilty" (Exo 34:7; Num 14:18). Moses quotes the limit too, and still pleads.
- **What it leads to.** Worship (Exo 34:8), pardon (Num 14:20), and confession (Neh 9).
- **Seat.** None in-verse. Neh 9:20 "your good Spirit to instruct them" is **God's, given** (divine; C v3 §1.6). **Pointer:** U1 §13 has Jonah using the same creed **against** God (Jon 4:2). With Moses and the Levites of Neh 9, **one creed is used three ways: worship, plea, and complaint.** Pilot §2.2 already notes the idiom shared between God and the wise (Pro 14:29 and others), and U1 §22 reads the human side of it.

## 6. Near the holy: wrath guarded against, and breaking out (HO) — Lev 10:6; Num 1:53; 18:5; 2Sa 6:7; 1Ch 13:10

- **What it is.** Human life **arranged around the holy so that wrath does not break out**, and what happens when that arrangement is broken.
- **What it does.**
  - **The guard:** "the Levites shall camp around the tabernacle of the testimony, so that there may be no wrath on the congregation" (Num 1:53); "keep guard over the sanctuary and over the altar, that there may never again be wrath" (Num 18:5). **The guard is a people placed in a ring**, and "any outsider who comes near shall be put to death" (Num 1:51; 18:7).
  - **Grief held back:** after Nadab and Abihu die, Aaron and his sons may not mourn: "Do not let the hair of your heads hang loose, and do not tear your clothes, lest you die, and wrath come upon all the congregation" (Lev 10:6). "And Aaron held his peace" (10:3). **The priest's own grief is withheld because of the holy.** The rest of Israel "may bewail the burning" (10:6).
  - **The breaking out:** "Uzzah put out his hand to the ark of God and took hold of it, for the oxen stumbled. And the anger of the Lord was kindled against Uzzah" (2Sa 6:6–7; 1Ch 13:9–10). **The act was a reflex to steady the ark.**
- **Where it comes from.** From **the nearness itself.** None of these verses names a sin of heart. The danger is **approach**.
- **How it co-exists.**
  - **The breaking out stops a celebration.** "David and all the house of Israel were celebrating before the Lord" (2Sa 6:5; 1Ch 13:8).
  - **It produces anger at God, then fear of God.** "David was angry … David was afraid of the Lord that day … 'How can the ark of the Lord come to me?'" (2Sa 6:8–9; 1Ch 13:11–12). This is U1 §13 and §37.
  - **(New here, from context.)** **The fear is resolved by seeing blessing on someone else.** "The Lord blessed Obed-edom and all his household … So David went and brought up the ark … with rejoicing" (2Sa 6:11–12). **David does not reason his way past the fear. He watches another household live with the ark.**
- **What it leads to.** Order (the Levites), silence (Aaron), avoidance, and then return with rejoicing (David).
- **Seat.** None in-verse or in ±3. **Aaron "held his peace" (Lev 10:3) does the seat's work.** The grief is present, and it is not voiced.

## 7. Provoking God: the turn to other gods, and its named roots (PV) — Lev 26:28; Deu 4:25; 6:15; 7:4; 11:17; 29:20; 31:29; 32:16; 32:19; 32:22; Jos 23:16; Judg 2:12; 1Ki 11:9; 2Ki 17:11; 17:17; 17:18; 2Ch 28:25

- **What it is.** The human act that **provokes** God's anger (*ka'as*, "provoke to anger", and "the anger of the Lord was kindled against") by **turning to other gods**, and **the inner roots the texts give for that turn**.
- **What it does.** It is warned of in advance (Lev 26; Deu 4–32; Jos 23) and narrated afterwards (Judg 2; 1Ki 11; 2Ki 17; 2Ch 28). **The same act appears as prediction and as history.**
- **Where it comes from.** **This is where the texts give most.** The roots named in the context of these verses:
  - **Forgetting.** "take care lest you forget the Lord" (Deu 6:12); "You were unmindful of the Rock that bore you, and you forgot the God who gave you birth" (Deu 32:18).
  - **Fullness.** "Jeshurun grew fat, and kicked; you grew fat, stout, and sleek; then he forsook God who made him" (Deu 32:15); "when … they have eaten and are full and grown fat, they will turn to other gods" (Deu 31:20). **Plenty comes before the turn.**
  - **A deceived or turning heart.** "Take care lest your heart be deceived, and you turn aside" (Deu 11:16); "a man or woman … whose heart is turning away today" (Deu 29:18); Solomon's "heart had turned away" (1Ki 11:9), because "his wives turned away his heart" (1Ki 11:4).
  - **False security in the heart.** The man who "blesses himself in his heart, saying, 'I shall be safe, though I walk in the stubbornness of my heart'" (Deu 29:19). **Here the anger is said to refuse forgiveness to one man, singled out:** "The Lord will not be willing to forgive him … the anger of the Lord and his jealousy will smoke against that man" (29:20).
  - **Intermarriage.** "they would turn away your sons from following me" (Deu 7:4).
  - **Distress.** "In the time of his distress he became yet more faithless to the Lord — this same King Ahaz. For he sacrificed to the gods of Damascus that had defeated him" (2Ch 28:22–23). **Distress drives Ahaz further from God, not back.** Contrast §16, where distress leads to crying out.
  - **Stubbornness, and not believing.** "they would not listen, but were stubborn, as their fathers had been, who did not believe in the Lord their God" (2Ki 17:14); "I know how rebellious and stubborn you are" (Deu 31:27).
- **How it co-exists.**
  - **You become what you follow.** "They went after false idols and became false" (2Ki 17:15). In exile "you will serve gods of wood and stone … that neither see, nor hear, nor eat, nor smell" (Deu 4:28).
  - **Walking contrary meets walking contrary.** "if … you will not listen to me, but walk contrary to me, then I will walk contrary to you in fury" (Lev 26:27–28). **The anger answers in the form of the provocation.** The next verses are the famine ("eat the flesh of your sons", 26:29) and "my soul will abhor you" (26:30). F v4 §4 reads this as mutual abhorrence.
  - **What is known in the heart.** Joshua ties the threat to what the people already know: "you know in your hearts and souls, all of you, that not one word has failed of all the good things … so the Lord will bring upon you all the evil things" (Jos 23:14–15). **The heart's own knowledge of God's faithfulness is made the ground for believing the warning.**
  - **The generation that did not know.** "there arose another generation after them who did not know the Lord or the work that he had done" (Judg 2:10), and they "provoked the Lord to anger" (2:12).
- **What it leads to.** Scattering, drought ("he will shut up the heavens", Deu 11:17), being given to plunderers, removal "out of his sight" (2Ki 17:18). Also **the hidden face** (Deu 32:20; 31:17–18; see §12).
- **Seat.**
  - **In the verse:** 1Ki 11:9 "his heart had turned away". **Solomon's own. Genuine, the cause.** Already read in A v3 §2.2 and pilot §2 ("the heart that provokes the anger").
  - **Outside the verse (all genuine unless noted):**
    - Deu 11:16 "lest your heart be deceived" (before) and 11:18 "lay up these words of mine in your heart and in your soul" (after). **The deceived heart is the cause; the heart filled with the words is the guard.** B v3 §4.1; C v3 §8.2; E v2 §4.1; G v3 §10; H v1 §5.1.
    - Deu 29:18–19 "whose heart is turning away" / "blesses himself in his heart … stubbornness of my heart". E v2 §4.1, §12; C v3 §9.1; F v4 §9.6. Pilot §1.6 reads 29:20 with it.
    - 1Ki 11:4 "his wives turned away his heart … his heart was not wholly true" (C v3 §5.1, §8.2).
    - Jos 23:14 "you know in your hearts and souls". B v3 §1.1 ("the heart as the knower").
    - Lev 26:29–30 "flesh" (bodily) and "my soul will abhor you" (God's; F v4 §4).
  - **Nothing new in the seat itself.** What part A adds is **how regularly the heart is named in the verses around the anger verse, and not in the anger verse itself.** Pilot §2 had said that the "anger was kindled" series carries "the human inner cause by narrative". **Part A confirms this, and finds that the seat is named nearby, in warnings and explanations.**

## 8. The wilderness provocation, and remembering it (WP) — Num 11:1; 11:33; 32:10; 32:13; 32:14; Deu 1:34; 9:7; 9:8; 9:22

- **What it is.** The provocations of the wilderness generation, **and the later command to remember them**.
- **What it does.**
  - **Complaint:** "the people complained in the hearing of the Lord about their misfortunes, and when the Lord heard it, his anger was kindled" (Num 11:1).
  - **Craving:** "While the meat was yet between their teeth … the anger of the Lord was kindled" (Num 11:33). The place is named after it: "there they buried the people who had the craving" (11:34). Already read in A v3 §2.2 ("craving and anger").
  - **Unbelief:** "Yet in spite of this word you did not believe the Lord your God … And the Lord heard your words and was angered" (Deu 1:32–34).
  - **Discouraging the heart:** the spies "discouraged the heart of the people of Israel from going into the land" (Num 32:9), and "the Lord's anger was kindled on that day" (32:10).
- **Where it comes from.**
  - **From ingratitude after being carried:** "the Lord your God carried you, as a man carries his son … Yet … you did not believe" (Deu 1:31–32).
  - **From not following wholly:** "because they have not wholly followed me, none except Caleb … and Joshua … for they have wholly followed the Lord" (Num 32:11–12).
- **How it co-exists.**
  - **The provocation is inherited.** "you have risen in your fathers' place, a brood of sinful men, to increase still more the fierce anger of the Lord" (Num 32:14). **The sons are warned that they are repeating the fathers.** They answer by offering to go armed before their brothers (32:16–17).
  - **Remembering is commanded against self-righteousness.** "Do not say in your heart … 'It is because of my righteousness'" (Deu 9:4); "Remember and do not forget how you provoked the Lord your God to wrath in the wilderness" (9:7). **Memory of provoking God is the cure for the heart's claim to righteousness.**
  - **Moses caught between.** In Num 11:10–15 (U1 §13 pointer; pilot §2.1) Moses hears the weeping, is "displeased", and says: "Why have you dealt ill with your servant? … kill me at once … that I may not see my wretchedness" (11:11, 15). **The leader between God's anger and the people's complaint asks to die.**
- **What it leads to.** Fire, plague, forty years of wandering "until all the generation that had done evil … was gone" (Num 32:13), and **a list of place names that keeps the memory**: "At Taberah also, and at Massah and at Kibroth-hattaavah you provoked the Lord to wrath" (Deu 9:22).
- **Seat.**
  - **In the verse:** Num 11:33 "meat" (*basar*). **Spurious.** Pilot §0 lists it among the links that do not count.
  - **Outside:** Num 32:7, 9 "discourage the heart of the people". **The people's own heart, as what the spies weakened. Genuine.** Deu 9:4–5 "Do not say in your heart … the uprightness of your heart". **Genuine** (D v2 §1.1). **Num 32:7, 9 is not cited in any batch.** **New as an M02 context seat:** discouraging the heart of others is what provokes the anger.

## 9. The messenger spurned (RB) — Num 12:9; 2Ch 24:18; 25:15

- **What it is.** God's anger when **the one he sends is spoken against, silenced or killed**.
- **What it does.**
  - **Spoken against:** "Why then were you not afraid to speak against my servant Moses?" And the anger of the Lord was kindled against them (Num 12:8–9). **The charge is a lack of fear.**
  - **Silenced:** Amaziah to the prophet: "Have we made you a royal counselor? Stop! Why should you be struck down?" (2Ch 25:16). The prophet stops, but says: "I know that God has determined to destroy you, because you have done this and have not listened to my counsel."
  - **Killed:** after Jehoiada died, "the king listened to them [the princes]"; prophets "testified against them, but they would not pay attention"; Zechariah, whom "the Spirit of God clothed", was stoned "in the court of the house of the Lord" (2Ch 24:17–21).
- **Where it comes from.**
  - From **rivalry** (Miriam and Aaron, Num 12:1–2, not read here).
  - From **loss of the restraining figure**: "after the death of Jehoiada the princes … paid homage to the king. Then the king listened to them" (2Ch 24:17).
  - From **pride after victory**: Amaziah worships "the gods of a people who did not deliver their own people from your hand" (2Ch 25:15).
- **How it co-exists.** **Aaron confesses at once:** "Oh, my lord, do not punish us because we have done foolishly and have sinned" (Num 12:11). Amaziah does not.
- **What it leads to.** Leprosy and intercession (Num 12:10–13), stoning (2Ch 24:21), and a determined end (2Ch 25:16).
- **Seat.** None in-verse. 2Ch 24:20 "Spirit of God clothed Zechariah". **God's, given. Divine** (D v2 §3.4; C v3 §9.2). Num 12:12 "flesh" is **bodily** (G v3 §16.7).

## 10. Acts that stop the anger: atonement and purging (TW) — Num 16:46; 25:3; 25:4; Deu 13:17

- **What it is.** A human act, **not a prayer**, that stops God's anger.
- **What it does.**
  - **Atonement carried into the plague:** "Take your censer … carry it quickly to the congregation and make atonement for them, for wrath has gone out from the Lord" (Num 16:46). Aaron "ran into the midst … And he stood between the dead and the living, and the plague was stopped" (16:47–48). **The priest's body stands on the line.**
  - **Execution of the leaders:** "hang them in the sun before the Lord, that the fierce anger of the Lord may turn away from Israel" (Num 25:4). Then Phinehas's spear (25:7), read in U1 §15 (SP, 25:11).
  - **Nothing of the devoted thing kept:** "None of the devoted things shall stick to your hand, that the Lord may turn from the fierceness of his anger and show you mercy and have compassion on you" (Deu 13:17).
- **Where it comes from.** From **the command** (Num 25:4; Deu 13) or **the leader's urgency** (Num 16:46: "carry it quickly").
- **How it co-exists.** **Weeping and zeal in one scene.** The people are "weeping in the entrance of the tent of meeting" (Num 25:6) while the offender walks past with a Midianite woman, and Phinehas acts.
- **What it leads to.** The plague stops; the anger turns; **mercy and compassion are promised in the same verse as the purging** (Deu 13:17).
- **Seat.** None in-verse or in ±3.

## 11. Not seeing the anger that stands in the way (BL) — Num 22:22

- **What it is.** A man **going on his way while God's anger stands in front of him, unseen by him and seen by his animal**.
- **What it does.** "God's anger was kindled because he went, and the angel of the Lord took his stand in the way as his adversary" (Num 22:22). "The donkey saw the angel … And Balaam struck the donkey" (22:23, 25).
- **Where it comes from.** From **going** after a permission hedged with "only do what I tell you" (22:20).
- **How it co-exists.** **Balaam's own anger** at the donkey (Num 22:27, in pilot §2.1 "anger with an act") falls on the creature that saved him. **Balaam is angry at the donkey while God is angry at Balaam, and only the donkey sees it.**
- **What it leads to.** Not read past 22:25 here. U1 has Balaam at Num 23:7–8 (§14).
- **Seat.** None in-verse or in ±3.

## 12. Reading the ruin: "why?" and the answer (RR) — Deu 29:23, 29:24, 29:27, 29:28; 31:17; 2Ch 29:8; Ezr 5:12

- **What it is.** People who **see a ruin, ask why, and name God's anger as its cause**.
- **What it does.**
  - **Outsiders ask:** "the next generation … and the foreigner who comes from a far land, will say … 'Why has the Lord done thus to this land? What caused the heat of this great anger?'" (Deu 29:22, 24).
  - **An answer is given:** "It is because they abandoned the covenant of the Lord" (29:25), "Therefore the anger of the Lord was kindled against this land" (29:27), "and the Lord uprooted them from their land in anger and fury and great wrath … as they are this day" (29:28).
  - **The sufferers ask:** "they will say in that day, 'Have not these evils come upon us because our God is not among us?'" (Deu 31:17). **The people read their trouble as God's absence.**
  - **A king reads it to his people:** "the wrath of the Lord came on Judah and Jerusalem … as you see with your own eyes" (2Ch 29:8).
  - **Exiles explain it to foreign officials:** "because our fathers had angered the God of heaven, he gave them into the hand of Nebuchadnezzar" (Ezr 5:12).
- **Where it comes from.** From **seeing**. "as you see with your own eyes" (2Ch 29:8); "when they see the afflictions of that land" (Deu 29:22).
- **How it co-exists.**
  - **A limit on reading.** "The secret things belong to the Lord our God, but the things that are revealed belong to us and to our children forever, that we may do all the words of this law" (Deu 29:29). **Read straight after the explanation of the ruin, this sets the reading of God's anger inside what is revealed, and turns it to doing.**
  - **A song as witness.** "write this song and teach it … that this song may be a witness for me against the people of Israel" (Deu 31:19). **The explanation is given before the ruin, so that the ruin can be read when it comes.**
- **What it leads to.** Reform (Hezekiah, §22) or rebuilding (Ezr 5:13). **In 2Ch 29 and Ezr 5, reading the ruin rightly is the first step back.**
- **Seat.** None in-verse or in ±3.

## 13. Meribah from God's side: Levi tested (MB, revisits U1 §12) — Deu 33:8

- **What it is.** The Meribah contention (U1 §12) **seen as God's test of Levi**, from Moses' blessing.
- **What it does.** "Give to Levi your Thummim … whom you tested at Massah, with whom you quarreled at the waters of Meribah" (Deu 33:8).
- **Where it comes from.** From the same event as U1 §12. **Here, God is the one who quarrels, and Levi is the one tested.**
- **How it co-exists.** **The test is passed by putting God above kin:** Levi "said of his father and mother, 'I regard them not'; he disowned his brothers and ignored his children. For they observed your word" (33:9). Contrast U1 §2 (KS), where kinship grounds the refusal of strife.
- **What it leads to.** **Teaching and priesthood:** "They shall teach Jacob your rules … they shall put incense before you" (33:10).
- **Seat.** None in-verse or in ±3.
- **(Revisit of U1 §12.)** U1 §12 reads Meribah as **human contention with God that costs the leaders the land**. **Deu 33:8 adds a second reading: at the same place, one party was "godly" and was found faithful by testing.**

## 14. One sins, all bear (OS) — Jos 7:1; 7:26; 22:18; 22:20

- **What it is.** God's anger falling on the **whole people for the act of one**, and the people's **fear** of this.
- **What it does.**
  - "the people of Israel broke faith in regard to the devoted things, for Achan … took some … And the anger of the Lord burned against the people of Israel" (Jos 7:1). **One man took, and the text says the people broke faith.**
  - **The fear drives a confrontation.** The tribes fear the eastern altar: "if you too rebel against the Lord today then tomorrow he will be angry with the whole congregation of Israel" (Jos 22:18). "Did not Achan … break faith … and wrath fell upon all the congregation of Israel? And he did not perish alone for his iniquity" (22:20).
- **Where it comes from.** From **memory of shared punishment**: "Have we not had enough of the sin at Peor from which even yet we have not cleansed ourselves" (Jos 22:17).
- **How it co-exists.**
  - **Accusation and generosity together.** The same speech that threatens also offers land: "pass over into the Lord's land … and take for yourselves a possession among us" (Jos 22:19).
  - **The accused answer by appealing to God's knowledge:** "The Mighty One, God, the Lord! He knows; and let Israel itself know! If it was in rebellion … do not spare us today" (Jos 22:22).
  - **The reverse plea.** Moses argues against collective anger ("shall one man sin, and will you be angry with all?", Num 16:22, §4). David, after the census, asks to bear it alone: "I have sinned … But these sheep, what have they done? Please let your hand be against me and against my father's house" (2Sa 24:17, context of §18). **The one who sinned asks that the anger fall on himself only.**
- **What it leads to.** The offender removed ("Why did you bring trouble on us? The Lord brings trouble on you today", Jos 7:25), a heap of stones, "Then the Lord turned from his burning anger" (7:26). The misunderstanding over the altar is resolved (22:21–23 onward).
- **Seat.** None in-verse or in ±3.

## 15. Doing the required thing, lest wrath come (GW) — Jos 9:20; 2Ch 19:10; Ezr 7:23

- **What it is.** A decision made **so that wrath does not come**, where the thing required is **a word to be kept**.
- **What it does.**
  - **An oath kept, though obtained by deceit:** "We have sworn to them by the Lord … now we may not touch them. This we will do to them: let them live, lest wrath be upon us, because of the oath" (Jos 9:19–20). "Then all the congregation murmured against the leaders" (9:18).
  - **A brother warned:** judges are to "warn them, that they may not incur guilt before the Lord and wrath may not come upon you and your brothers" (2Ch 19:10). **Warning another guards both of them.**
  - **A foreign king's decree:** "Whatever is decreed by the God of heaven, let it be done in full … lest his wrath be against the realm of the king and his sons" (Ezr 7:23). **Fear of God's wrath appears in a Persian king.**
- **Where it comes from.** From **fear of the Lord.** "let the fear of the Lord be upon you" (2Ch 19:7); "Thus you shall do in the fear of the Lord, in faithfulness, and with your whole heart" (19:9).
- **How it co-exists.** **Against the crowd:** in Jos 9 the leaders keep the oath while "all the congregation murmured".
- **What it leads to.** The Gibeonites live as servants (Jos 9:21–23); justice is ordered (2Ch 19); the temple is provided for (Ezr 7).
- **Seat.** None in-verse. **Outside:** 2Ch 19:9 "with your whole heart". **The judges' own. Genuine** (C v3 §5.1; E v2 §5.1). **Nothing new.**

## 16. Sold, distressed, crying out, relapsing (CY) — Judg 2:14; 2:20; 3:8; 10:7; 2Ki 13:3

- **What it is.** The human course **under God's anger** in the Judges cycle and its echo in Kings: given over, distressed, crying out, delivered, and then relapsing.
- **What it does.**
  - **Given over:** "he gave them over to plunderers … so that they could no longer withstand their enemies … And they were in terrible distress" (Judg 2:14–15).
  - **Crying out:** "when the people of Israel cried out to the Lord, the Lord raised up a deliverer" (Judg 3:9); "We have sinned against you, because we have forsaken our God" (10:10); "do to us whatever seems good to you. Only please deliver us this day" (10:15).
  - **Seeking favour:** "Jehoahaz sought the favor of the Lord, and the Lord listened to him, for he saw the oppression of Israel" (2Ki 13:4).
  - **Relapsing:** "whenever the judge died, they turned back and were more corrupt than their fathers … They did not drop any of their practices or their stubborn ways" (Judg 2:19); "Nevertheless, they did not depart from the sins of the house of Jeroboam" (2Ki 13:6).
- **Where it comes from.** From **distress**, which here produces the cry. **Contrast Ahaz** (§7), in whom distress produced more faithlessness.
- **How it co-exists.**
  - **God's pity answers the groaning, not a repentance:** "the Lord was moved to pity by their groaning because of those who afflicted and oppressed them" (Judg 2:18). In Judg 10:16 they put away the foreign gods, and "he became impatient over the misery of Israel" (the Hebrew has God's *nephesh*; God's, not the object here).
  - **The cry is answered while the relapse is foreseen.** Nations are left "to test Israel by them" (Judg 2:22).
- **What it leads to.** Rest "forty years" (Judg 3:11), then again.
- **Seat.** None in-verse. **Outside:** Judg 3:10 "The Spirit of the Lord was upon him" (Othniel). **God's, given. Divine.**

## 17. The agent who did not carry out the wrath (AW, B3) — 1Sa 28:18

- **What it is.** A human **commissioned to carry out God's wrath who did not**, and what it cost him.
- **What it does.** "Because you did not obey the voice of the Lord and did not carry out his fierce wrath against Amalek, therefore the Lord has done this thing to you this day" (1Sa 28:18).
- **Where it comes from.** From **disobedience in the commission** (1Sa 15, not read here).
- **How it co-exists.** **Abandonment, fear and bodily collapse.**
  - "God has turned away from me and answers me no more" (1Sa 28:15).
  - "Saul fell at once full length on the ground, filled with fear … And there was no strength in him, for he had eaten nothing all day and all night" (28:20).
  - The medium: "I have taken my life in my hand" (28:21).
- **What it leads to.** Death the next day (28:19).
- **Seat.** None in-verse. **Outside:** 1Sa 28:21 *nephesh*. **The medium's. Life-sense only.**
- **Pointer.** Part A has only one B3 verse. The other ten B3 verses (a human agent carrying out God's wrath) are in parts B and C.

## 18. Anger that incites: the census (IC) — 2Sa 24:1; 1Ch 27:24

- **What it is.** God's anger that **moves a human to act**, and the act afterwards judged.
- **What it does.** "Again the anger of the Lord was kindled against Israel, and he incited David against them, saying, 'Go, number Israel and Judah'" (2Sa 24:1). Joab objects: "why does my lord the king delight in this thing?" (24:3). **The king's word prevailed** (24:4). Chronicles records that "Joab … began to count, but did not finish. Yet wrath came upon Israel for this" (1Ch 27:24).
- **Where it comes from.** 2Sa 24:1 names **God's anger** as the source of the incitement. **The verse gives no inner cause in David.** Joab's question names a **delight** in the king.
- **How it co-exists.**
  - **Conscience afterwards:** "David's heart struck him after he had numbered the people … 'I have sinned greatly … I have done very foolishly'" (2Sa 24:10).
  - **Choosing whose hand:** "Let us fall into the hand of the Lord, for his mercy is great; but let me not fall into the hand of man" (24:14). **The one under God's anger chooses God's hand because of God's mercy.**
  - **Asking to bear it alone** (24:17; §14).
- **What it leads to.** Plague, David's confession, and the offering (24:18 onward, not read).
- **Seat.** None in-verse. **Outside:** 2Sa 24:10 "David's heart struck him". **The bearer's own. Genuine** (D v2 §3.3, §9.5, §11: "the heart that strikes"). **Read from the M02 side, the struck heart comes after an act that God's anger incited.** Pointer for D v2 §3.3.

## 19. Humbled under anger: the heart that turns (RH) — 1Ki 8:46; 2Ki 22:13; 2Ch 6:36; 12:7; 12:12; 32:25; 32:26; 34:21; Ezr 9:14

- **What it is.** Humbling oneself, and **the heart turning**, in the face of God's anger. The anger is then turned, reduced or deferred.
- **What it does.**
  - **Foreseen in Solomon's prayer:** "If they sin against you — for there is no one who does not sin — and you are angry with them … yet if they turn their heart … and repent … with all their heart and with all their soul … then hear" (1Ki 8:46–49; 2Ch 6:36–39).
  - **Josiah hears the book:** "he tore his clothes … 'great is the wrath of the Lord that is kindled against us, because our fathers have not obeyed'" (2Ki 22:11, 13; 2Ch 34:19, 21). **The answer names the heart:** "because your heart was penitent [*tender*], and you humbled yourself … and have torn your clothes and wept before me, I also have heard you" (2Ki 22:19; 2Ch 34:27).
  - **Rehoboam and the princes:** "humbled themselves and said, 'The Lord is righteous.' When the Lord saw that they humbled themselves … I will not destroy them" (2Ch 12:6–7); "when he humbled himself the wrath of the Lord turned from him" (12:12).
  - **Hezekiah's pride and humbling:** "Hezekiah did not make return according to the benefit done to him, for his heart was proud. Therefore wrath came … But Hezekiah humbled himself for the pride of his heart, both he and the inhabitants of Jerusalem, so that the wrath of the Lord did not come upon them in the days of Hezekiah" (2Ch 32:25–26).
  - **Ezra confesses:** "you, our God, have punished us less than our iniquities deserved … shall we break your commandments again …? Would you not be angry with us until you consumed us …? … you are just … none can stand before you" (Ezr 9:13–15).
  - **Manasseh (context of §20):** "when he was in distress, he entreated the favor of the Lord his God and humbled himself greatly … Then Manasseh knew that the Lord was God" (2Ch 33:12–13).
- **Where it comes from.**
  - From **hearing the word** (Josiah).
  - From **the prophet's announcement** (Rehoboam, 2Ch 12:5).
  - From **distress** (Manasseh; the captives of 1Ki 8).
  - From **recognising that one's own heart was proud** (Hezekiah). **This is the only case in part A where the provocation and the humbling are named in the same heart, in consecutive verses.**
- **How it co-exists.**
  - **Humbling with a confession of God's justice:** "The Lord is righteous" (2Ch 12:6); "you are just" (Ezr 9:15).
  - **Humbling with the body:** torn clothes and weeping (Josiah).
  - **Humbling that is partial.** Rehoboam is spared destruction but made to serve Shishak, "that they may know my service and the service of the kingdoms" (2Ch 12:8). Bronze shields replace gold (12:10). And "he did evil, for he did not set his heart to seek the Lord" (12:14). **The humbling turned the wrath, and the heart was still not set.**
- **What it leads to.**
  - The anger is turned (Rehoboam), deferred past the humbled one's lifetime (Hezekiah; Josiah, "your eyes shall not see all the disaster", 2Ki 22:20), or feared again (Ezra).
  - **Josiah's humbling is heard for Josiah, and the wrath on the place stands** (§21).
- **Seat.**
  - **In the verse:** 2Ch 32:25 "his heart was proud" and 32:26 "the pride of his heart". **Hezekiah's own. Genuine.** A v3 §2.2; D v2 §9.1, §10.1, §11; pilot §2.
  - **Outside (all genuine, the bearer's own):**
    - 1Ki 8:47–48 / 2Ch 6:37–38 "turn their heart … with all their heart and with all their soul" (B v3 §5; C v3 §2.3, §8.1; D v2 §5; E v2 §13).
    - 2Ki 22:19 / 2Ch 34:27 "your heart was penitent / tender" (A v3 §3.3; B v3 §3.4; D v2 §10.1; F v4 §6).
    - 2Ch 12:14 "did not set his heart to seek the Lord" (C v3 §7.1).
  - **Nothing new in any single seat.** **What part A adds is a third kind of seat for pilot §2.** The pilot has the heart that provokes (the cause) and the spirit that receives (the effect). **Part A adds the heart that turns, and so turns the anger away.** In these verses, and in §22, it is the human seat most often named in the context of God's anger. For the researcher: whether pilot §2 takes this as a third row (see §R-V).

## 20. "Made Israel to sin": provocation handed on (MI) — 1Ki 14:9; 14:15; 15:30; 16:2; 16:7; 16:13; 16:26; 16:33; 21:22; 22:53; 2Ki 21:6; 21:15; 2Ch 33:6

- **What it is.** A leader's provocation of God that **passes to the people and to the next ruler**, in a repeated formula.
- **What it does.**
  - The formula: "the sins of Jeroboam, which he sinned and which he made Israel to sin" (1Ki 14:16; 15:30; 16:13, 26).
  - **Each king is measured by the one before:** Baasha "walked in the way of Jeroboam" (1Ki 16:2); Omri "did more evil than all who were before him" (16:25); Ahab "did more to provoke the Lord … than all the kings of Israel who were before him" (16:33); Ahaziah walked "in the way of his father and in the way of his mother and in the way of Jeroboam" (22:52–53); Manasseh "led them astray to do more evil than the nations" (2Ki 21:9; 2Ch 33:9).
- **Where it comes from.**
  - **Contempt:** "you … have cast me behind your back" (1Ki 14:9).
  - **Ingratitude for elevation:** "Because I exalted you from among the people" (1Ki 14:7); "Since I exalted you out of the dust" (16:2).
  - **Self-sale:** "you have sold yourself to do what is evil" (1Ki 21:20). "There was none who sold himself … like Ahab, whom Jezebel his wife incited" (21:25).
  - **Lightness:** "as if it had been a light thing for him to walk in the sins of Jeroboam" (1Ki 16:31).
  - **The measure given is David's heart:** "you have not been like my servant David, who … followed me with all his heart" (1Ki 14:8).
- **How it co-exists.**
  - **The executor is also judged.** Baasha is condemned "both because of all the evil that he did … in being like the house of Jeroboam, and also because he destroyed it" (1Ki 16:7). **Carrying out the prophesied judgment did not make the executor righteous.**
  - **One found pleasing in a condemned house.** Jeroboam's child "shall come to the grave, because in him there is found something pleasing to the Lord" (1Ki 14:13).
  - **Recognition without turning.** "Have you found me, O my enemy?" (1Ki 21:20). **Ahab recognises the prophet as his enemy.**
- **What it leads to.** Whole houses cut off, "as a man burns up dung" (1Ki 14:10). Israel scattered "because of the sins of Jeroboam" (14:15–16). **Manasseh's provocation outlives him**, and Josiah's reform does not undo it (§21).
- **Seat.** None in-verse. **Outside:** 1Ki 14:8 "followed me with all his heart". **David's own, as the standard. Genuine.** Not cited in the batches in this form. **Pointer only:** the heart appears in these chapters as the measure the kings fail, not as their own seat.

## 21. Past remedy: anger not quenched (NR) — 2Ki 22:17; 23:26; 24:20; 2Ch 34:25; 36:16

- **What it is.** God's anger that has **reached a point where human turning no longer reverses it for the people**.
- **What it does.**
  - "my wrath will be kindled against this place, and it will not be quenched" (2Ki 22:17; 2Ch 34:25).
  - **Even after the fullest turning:** "Before him there was no king like him, who turned to the Lord with all his heart and with all his soul and with all his might … Still the Lord did not turn from the burning of his great wrath … because of all the provocations with which Manasseh had provoked him" (2Ki 23:25–26).
  - "it came to the point in Jerusalem and Judah that he cast them out from his presence" (2Ki 24:20).
  - "they kept mocking the messengers of God, despising his words and scoffing at his prophets, until the wrath of the Lord rose against his people, until there was no remedy" (2Ch 36:16).
- **Where it comes from.**
  - **From accumulated provocation:** "since the day their fathers came out of Egypt, even to this day" (2Ki 21:15).
  - **From the king's heart hardened against turning:** Zedekiah "stiffened his neck and hardened his heart against turning to the Lord" (2Ch 36:13).
- **How it co-exists.**
  - **God's compassion is named in the same passage as "no remedy":** "The Lord … sent persistently to them by his messengers, because he had compassion on his people" (2Ch 36:15). **The persistence of the compassion is what the mocking used up.**
  - **The one who turns is still heard personally** (Josiah, §19), **while the people's end stands.** **Individual turning and national judgment are held apart here.** Contrast Hezekiah (§19), where "he and the inhabitants of Jerusalem" humbled themselves together.
- **What it leads to.** Josiah's death at Megiddo (2Ki 23:29), exile, and the burning of the temple (2Ch 36:17–19).
- **Seat.** None in-verse. **Outside:**
  - 2Ki 23:25 "all his heart and … all his soul" (Josiah's own). **Genuine.**
  - 2Ch 36:13 "hardened his heart against turning" (Zedekiah's own). **Genuine** (C v3 §9.3; G v3 §3.3).
  - 2Ki 22:19 / 2Ch 34:27 (§19).
  - **For the researcher:** 2Ki 23:25 was not found in the batch files. **New as an M02 context seat**: the fullest turning of heart and soul named next to wrath that did not turn.

## 22. Turning back to turn the anger away: reform, release, covenant (RF) — 2Ki 23:19; 2Ch 19:2; 28:11; 28:13; 29:10; 30:8; Ezr 10:14; Neh 13:18

- **What it is.** A leader or group acting to **turn God's anger away**: by removing what provoked it, releasing what was wrongly taken, making a covenant, or calling others back.
- **What it does.**
  - **Removal:** Josiah "removed all the shrines … which kings of Israel had made, provoking the Lord to anger" (2Ki 23:19).
  - **Covenant formed in the heart:** "Now it is in my heart to make a covenant with the Lord … in order that his fierce anger may turn away from us. My sons, do not now be negligent" (2Ch 29:10–11).
  - **A call to return:** "Do not now be stiff-necked as your fathers were, but yield yourselves to the Lord … that his fierce anger may turn away from you. For if you return … your brothers and your children will find compassion" (2Ch 30:8–9).
  - **Separation:** "Let our officials stand for the whole assembly … until the fierce wrath of our God over this matter is turned away from us" (Ezr 10:14).
  - **Sabbath restored:** "Now you are bringing more wrath on Israel by profaning the Sabbath" (Neh 13:18), then the gates are shut (13:19).
  - **Captives released:**
    - Oded: "you have killed them in a rage that has reached up to heaven … Have you not sins of your own against the Lord your God? … send back the captives … for the fierce wrath of the Lord is upon you" (2Ch 28:9–11).
    - The chiefs: "you propose to bring upon us guilt against the Lord in addition to our present sins … there is fierce wrath against Israel" (28:13).
    - **Then:** "with the spoil they clothed all who were naked among them … provided them with food and drink, and anointed them, and carrying all the feeble among them on donkeys, they brought them to their kinsfolk" (28:15). **Fear of adding to the wrath turns into care for enemies who are also kin.**
  - **Rebuke acted on:** "Should you help the wicked and love those who hate the Lord? Because of this, wrath has gone out against you … Nevertheless, some good is found in you … you … have set your heart to seek God" (2Ch 19:2–3). Jehoshaphat "went out again among the people … and brought them back to the Lord" (19:4).
- **Where it comes from.**
  - From **reading the ruin** (§12: 2Ch 29:8–9).
  - From **a prophet's word** (2Ch 19:2; 28:9).
  - From **awareness of accumulated guilt** ("our guilt is already great", 2Ch 28:13; "we have greatly transgressed", Ezr 10:13).
  - From **the fathers' example as a warning** ("Did not your fathers act in this way …?", Neh 13:18; "Do not be like your fathers", 2Ch 30:7).
- **How it co-exists.**
  - **Mockery.** The couriers were "laughed … to scorn and mocked" (2Ch 30:10). "However, some men … humbled themselves and came" (30:11).
  - **Opposition within.** "Only Jonathan … and Jahzeiah … opposed this" (Ezr 10:15).
  - **Practical limits.** "the people are many, and it is a time of heavy rain; we cannot stand in the open" (Ezr 10:13).
- **What it leads to.** Passover (2Ki 23:21–22; 2Ch 30), the captives returned (2Ch 28:15), and the Sabbath kept (Neh 13:21). **Josiah's reform does not turn the anger for Judah** (§21).
- **Seat.**
  - **In the verse:** 2Ch 29:10 "it is in my heart to make a covenant". **Hezekiah's own. Genuine.** A v3 §2.2; E v2 §7.1.
  - **Outside:** 2Ch 19:3 "set your heart to seek God" (**genuine**; C v3 §7.1, §11).
  - **The seat of reform is where the intent to turn is formed.** This joins §19 (see there).

## 23. Trust staked on a spoken confession (TS) — Ezr 8:22

- **What it is.** Having **told an outsider that God's anger is against those who forsake him**, the speaker must now **live by that confession**.
- **What it does.** "For I was ashamed to ask the king for a band of soldiers … since we had told the king, 'The hand of our God is for good on all who seek him, and the power of his wrath is against all who forsake him'" (Ezr 8:22).
- **Where it comes from.** From **shame** at the thought of contradicting his own words.
- **How it co-exists.** **Fasting and humbling:** "I proclaimed a fast … that we might humble ourselves before our God, to seek from him a safe journey" (8:21). "we fasted and implored our God for this, and he listened" (8:23).
- **What it leads to.** A safe journey is sought from God, and "he listened to our entreaty" (8:23).
- **Seat.** None in-verse or in ±3. **Shame does the seat's work.**

## 24. Calling the anger down on those who despise (IM) — Neh 4:5

- **What it is.** A prayer asking God **to keep his anger on the mockers**, because they provoked him.
- **What it does.** "Hear, O our God, for we are despised. Turn back their taunt on their own heads … Do not cover their guilt … for they have provoked you to anger in the presence of the builders" (Neh 4:4–5).
- **Where it comes from.** From **being despised**. "What are these feeble Jews doing?" (4:2); "if a fox goes up on it he will break down their stone wall!" (4:3).
- **How it co-exists.** **The prayer is followed at once by work, not by waiting:** "So we built the wall … for the people had a mind to work" (4:6). **The enemies' anger then rises again** (4:7; U1 §17).
- **What it leads to.** The wall "joined together to half its height" (4:6).
- **Seat.** None in-verse. **Outside:** Neh 4:6 "a mind [heart] to work". **Genuine**, already read in U1 §17 and §39. **What unit 2 adds:** in U1 §17 the builders' heart stands **between the enemy's two outbursts**. **It also stands straight after the builders' prayer for God's anger on the mockers. The heart to work follows the handing-over of the enemies to God.**
- **Pointer.** Part A has one imprecation. Parts B and C (the psalms and prophets) are expected to have more. That is a pointer to read for, not a claim.

## 25. Explaining suffering by God's anger, and being answered for it (EX) — Job 4:9; 20:23; 20:28; 21:17; 21:20; 21:30; 35:15; 42:7

- **What it is.** Human speakers who **use God's anger to explain who suffers and why**, the sufferer's contest of their explanation, and **God's anger turned on the explainers**.
- **What it does.**
  - **Eliphaz:** "who that was innocent ever perished? … those who plow iniquity and sow trouble reap the same. By the breath of God they perish, and by the blast of his anger they are consumed" (Job 4:7–9).
  - **Zophar:** "To fill his belly to the full, God will send his burning anger against him and rain it upon him into his body" (20:23); "dragged off in the day of God's wrath. This is the wicked man's portion" (20:28–29).
  - **Job contests it from what he has seen:** "How often is it that the lamp of the wicked is put out? … That God distributes pains in his anger?" (21:17); "Let their own eyes see their destruction, and let them drink of the wrath" (21:20). He also cites travellers' testimony "that the evil man is spared in the day of calamity, that he is rescued in the day of wrath" (21:29–30).
  - **Elihu:** "because his anger does not punish … Job opens his mouth in empty talk" (35:15–16).
  - **God:** "My anger burns against you and against your two friends, for you have not spoken of me what is right, as my servant Job has" (42:7).
- **Where it comes from.** From **a rule held with confidence**. Eliphaz: "As I have seen" (4:8). His opening is "Is not your fear of God your confidence?" (4:6). **The explainers' certainty rests on their own observation, as Job's contest does.**
- **How it co-exists.**
  - **Job's contest wants God's anger visible on the wicked.** "Let him pay it out to them, that they may know it" (21:19). **Job does not deny God's anger; he denies that it is seen where the friends say.**
  - **The wicked in Job 21 reject God outright:** "Depart from us! We do not desire the knowledge of your ways" (21:14).
- **What it leads to.**
  - **The explainers must go to the sufferer, and he prays for them.** "go to my servant Job and offer up a burnt offering for yourselves. And my servant Job shall pray for you, for I will accept his prayer" (42:8). "the Lord restored the fortunes of Job, when he had prayed for his friends" (42:10). **The one they explained by God's anger stands between them and God's anger** (compare §4).
  - **Job himself has just said:** "I had heard of you by the hearing of the ear, but now my eye sees you; therefore I despise myself, and repent in dust and ashes" (42:5–6). **God's anger then turns on the friends, not on Job.**
- **Seat.**
  - **In the verse:** Job 4:9 "blast" (*ruach*). **God's breath. Divine; not a human seat.** A v3 §2.3 ("anger, breath and nose").
  - **Outside:** Job 21:18 "wind" (**not inner-being**).

## 26. The sufferer who bears God's anger without knowing why (JB) — Job 6:4; 9:5; 9:13; 10:17; 14:13; 16:9; 19:11

- **What it is.** A man who **experiences God's anger as directed at him, from within and without, with no cause given to him**.
- **What it does.**
  - **Within:** "the arrows of the Almighty are in me; my spirit drinks their poison; the terrors of God are arrayed against me" (Job 6:4).
  - **As an enemy:** "He has torn me in his wrath and hated me; he has gnashed his teeth at me" (16:9); "He has kindled his wrath against me and counts me as his adversary" (19:11).
  - **As a trial with fresh witnesses:** "You renew your witnesses against me and increase your vexation toward me" (10:17).
  - **As irresistible and unanswerable:** "God will not turn back his anger … How then can I answer him …? Though I am in the right, I cannot answer him; I must appeal for mercy to my accuser" (9:13–15). He "removes mountains, and they know it not, when he overturns them in his anger" (9:5).
- **Where it comes from.** **The texts give Job no cause.** "If I am guilty, woe to me! If I am in the right, I cannot lift up my head" (10:15). "I was at ease, and he broke me apart" (16:12). **The reader knows from 42:7 that he spoke rightly; Job, inside the anger, does not.**
- **How it co-exists.**
  - **Isolation.** "He has put my brothers far from me … My relatives have failed me, my close friends have forgotten me" (19:13–14). "Men have gaped at me with their mouth" (16:10).
  - **Not being able to see God.** "Behold, he passes by me, and I see him not" (9:11). "If I summoned him and he answered me, I would not believe that he was listening to my voice" (9:16).
  - **A wish not to have been born.** "Why did you bring me out from the womb?" (10:18). "leave me alone, that I may find a little cheer" (10:20).
  - **Hope beyond the anger, in the same breath.** "Oh that you would hide me in Sheol, that you would conceal me until your wrath be past, that you would appoint me a set time, and remember me! … You would call, and I would answer you; you would long for the work of your hands" (14:13, 15). **Job asks for a hiding place from God's anger in God's own keeping, and expects God to long for him afterwards.**
- **What it leads to.** Within part A: the sight of God (42:5), repentance "in dust and ashes" (42:6), and God's verdict for him (42:7; §25).
- **Seat.**
  - **In the verse:** Job 6:4 "my spirit drinks their poison". **Job's own, as receiver. Genuine.** A v3 §1; pilot §2 ("the spirit that receives the anger"); U1 §8 and §39.
  - **Outside:** Job 6:7 "My appetite refuses to touch them" (*nephesh*). **Appetite-sense, the bearer's own** (C v3 §1.1, §9.4). Job 9:4 "wise in heart" is **God's**. Job 14:13 is the verse G v3 §16 left for M20 or unit 2 (U1 §R-V). **It is read here: its movement is hiding until the wrath passes, followed by being remembered. It is not a death-wish.**
  - **Nothing new in the seat.** Part A has Job 6:4 as its single verse where **God's anger is located inside the human seat by the bearer himself**. Parts B and C are expected to add the laments (pilot §2, second row).

**What the sequences show, read together (for the researcher's review, not a synthesis):**
1. **What the human does in response to God's anger comes in a small set of ways, and they recur across books:**
   - plead (§1, §4, §5)
   - humble oneself / turn the heart (§19)
   - act to remove the cause (§10, §22)
   - read the ruin (§12)
   - cry out from distress (§16)
   - refuse the warning (§9, §21)

   Two of the sequences above contain several of these in order (2Ki 22–23; Judg 2).
2. **Distress goes both ways.** It produces crying out (§16; Manasseh, §19) and more faithlessness (Ahaz, §7). **The texts do not say why; they set the two side by side in the same books.**
3. **The heart appears in these passages in three roles:** the cause (§7), the one that turns (§19, §22), and the hardened one "against turning" (§21). **In part A, the receiving spirit (§26) appears only in Job.**
4. **The leader's bearing of God's anger is distinct from the people's.** Moses bears it "because of you" (§2). Aaron stands between the dead and the living (§10). Josiah is heard for himself while the place is not (§21). David asks to bear it alone (§14, §18).
