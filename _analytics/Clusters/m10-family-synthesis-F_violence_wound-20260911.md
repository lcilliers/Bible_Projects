## Finding: the same crushing-verb is used destructively of enemies and redemptively of the self, with no lexical marker distinguishing the two

The most striking pattern here is not a split between families of meaning but a **split within a
single word's own usage**. H1792/H1794 (dakha/dakhah, "to crush") are applied, in the same handful
of verses, to two opposite situations:

- **Destructively**, of enemies and oppressors being crushed in judgment: "you crushed Rahab like a
  carcass" (Ps 89:10), "crush the oppressor" (Ps 72:4), the wicked who "crush your people" (Ps
  94:5).
- **Redemptively, of the self**, as the state God specifically desires and does not despise: "The
  sacrifices of God are a broken spirit; a broken and contrite heart, O God, you will not despise"
  (Ps 51:17), "him who is of a contrite and lowly spirit" (Isa 57:15) — here "crushed" names the
  humbled disposition that draws God near, the functional opposite of a condemned state.

Nothing in the lexical form distinguishes these — the same root, same stem pattern in places (the
Niphal/Pual senses of H1792 explicitly include both "to be crushed" and "to be contrite (fig.)" in
one entry) — only the referent (an enemy vs. one's own heart) tells you which reading applies.

## H2555 (chamas, violence) is the family's real anchor — structural and social, not incidental

At 43 verses, chamas defines violence as a *filled-world* condition rather than isolated acts: the
paradigm case is the pre-Flood indictment itself — "the earth was filled with violence" (Gen 6:11,
6:13) — and the recurring pattern afterward is social and structural: false witness (Ps 27:12,
Exod 23:1), dishonest commerce and oppression of the poor (Ps 12:5, Amos 3:10), and a "garment" of
violence worn by the wicked as a visible attribute (Ps 73:6, Ezek 7:23). **Isaiah 53:9 — "he had
done no violence" — uses this same word (chamas)** as the Servant's negative self-defence,
converging with Isaiah 53:10's crushing verb (dakha, this same family) in the same Song. This is
the *fifth* family in this exercise to independently converge on Isaiah 53 (after guilt,
transgression, iniquity, and the bearing verb nasa) — worth flagging as a standalone pattern, not
buried inside any one family's write-up.
