## Isaiah 7:15–16 — "when he knows how to refuse the evil and choose the good"

The setting is a king’s heart in fear. Syria and Ephraim are marching on Jerusalem, and *"the heart of Ahaz and the heart of his people shook as the trees of the forest shake before the wind"* (v2). The Lord sends Isaiah with a word for that heart:
- *"Be careful, be quiet, do not fear, and do not let your heart be faint"* (v4)
- the threat *"shall not stand"* (v7)
- then the condition: *"If you are not firm in faith, you will not be firm at all"* (v9). Both "firm" words are the one verb (H0539), so standing firm and being kept firm are the same word.

Then the Lord makes Ahaz an offer: *"Ask a sign of the Lord your God; let it be deep as Sheol or high as heaven"* (v11). Ahaz refuses, in pious words: *"I will not ask, and I will not put the Lord to the test"* (v12). The answer shows what that refusal was: *"Is it too little for you to weary men, that you weary my God also?"* (v13). Ahaz’s refusal was a choice. He chose not to take hold of what the Lord held out.

The sign is given anyway, and at its centre is a child who has what Ahaz lacked: *"He shall eat curds and honey when he knows how to refuse the evil and choose the good"* (v15). The act is one act with two sides:
- refusing the evil (H3988A)
- choosing the good (H0977)

Refusing is not a step before the choice. It is the choice seen from the other side. Both rest on a knowing: *"when he knows how"*. The verse marks the moment a person can tell evil from good and turn from the one to the other.

The verse ties the eating to that knowing: he eats *"when he knows how to refuse the evil and choose the good"*. The oracle itself says what curds and honey are. *"In that day … because of the abundance of milk that they give, he will eat curds, for everyone who is left in the land will eat curds and honey"* (v21–22). It is the food of those left in the land after the Lord has swept it with *"the king of Assyria"*: vineyards turned to *"briers and thorns"*, hills *"where cattle are let loose"* (v23–25). The child who refuses the evil and chooses the good eats with those who are left, and eats from abundance, in a land stripped of what it had.

The next verse sets a second consequence against the same knowing: *"before the boy knows how to refuse the evil and choose the good, the land whose two kings you dread will be deserted"* (v16). What Ahaz feared will be gone. Then comes what Ahaz’s own choice brings on him: *"The Lord will bring upon you and upon your people and upon your father’s house such days as have not come … the king of Assyria!"* (v17).

The oracle uses the same verb for refusing once more. Isaiah 8:6 has *"Because this people has refused the waters of Shiloah that flow gently"*, and it is the same word as *"refuse the evil"* (H3988A). Its consequence is the opposite of a gentle stream: *"the waters of the River, mighty and many, the king of Assyria"* overflowing *"even to the neck"*, and filling *"the breadth of your land, O Immanuel"* (8:7–8).

So in one oracle refusing runs both ways:
- the child refuses the evil and chooses the good, and eats among those who are left
- Ahaz refuses the sign, and the people refuse the gentle waters, and the flood comes

I read the passage as setting the right refusing against the wrong refusing, each with its consequence. The text does not join them in one sentence; it places them in one oracle.

**What this verse shows of choosing:**
- Refusing and choosing are one act with two sides; refusing is itself a choice.
- The act rests on knowing how to tell evil from good.
- It is set in a heart under fear, where faith is what makes a person firm.
- The consequence of the right refusing and choosing is to eat among those who are left.
- The consequence of refusing what God holds out, whether the sign or the gentle waters, is the flood.

## Isaiah 1:29 — "the oaks that you desired … the gardens that you have chosen"

The oracle opens as a lawsuit: *"Hear, O heavens, and give ear, O earth"* (v2). The charge goes to the inner being:
- *"Children have I reared and brought up, but they have rebelled against me"* (v2)
- *"The ox knows its owner … but Israel does not know, my people do not understand"* (v3)
- *"The whole head is sick, and the whole heart faint"* (v5)

Their worship goes on, and the Lord refuses it: *"I do not delight in the blood of bulls"* (v11); *"your appointed feasts my soul hates"* (v14); *"even though you make many prayers, I will not listen"* (v15).

Then the choice is laid before them, in the same two-sided shape as Isa 7:15:
- *"cease to do evil, learn to do good"* (v16–17)
- *"Come now, let us reason together"*, with the offer that sins *"like scarlet"* shall be *"as white as snow"* (v18)
- the two outcomes: *"If you are willing and obedient, you shall eat the good of the land"*; *"but if you refuse and rebel, you shall be eaten by the sword"* (v19–20)

Being willing, and refusing (here H3985, a different word from Isa 7), are the two sides of the choice. Eating, and being eaten, are its two outcomes.

The oracle ends with those who did not take the offer. v29–31 is figurative speech, and the figure is the point:
- *"they shall be ashamed of the oaks that you desired; and you shall blush for the gardens that you have chosen"*
- *"you shall be like an oak whose leaf withers, and like a garden without water"*
- *"the strong shall become tinder, and his work a spark, and both of them shall burn together"*

The things they desired and chose become the picture of what they themselves become. They chose oaks and gardens, and they become a withering oak and a garden with no water. The choice comes back as an image of the chooser. The last line goes further: the strong man’s own *"work"* is the spark that sets him alight. What he made burns him, *"with none to quench them"*.

Shame comes first in the verse: *"ashamed"*, *"blush"* (H0954, H2659). The chooser is ashamed of the very thing that was desired and chosen. What drew the desire becomes the cause of the shame.

The oracle also holds out the other side to those who turn: *"Zion shall be redeemed by justice, and those in her who repent, by righteousness"* (v27). The Lord will *"smelt away your dross"* and *"restore"* (v25–26). The choice is not closed off at the first wrong choosing. The offer of v18 stands in the middle of the charge.

Elsewhere in Isaiah, gardens are places of the worship the Lord rejects: *"sacrificing in gardens"* (65:3), *"those who … go into the gardens … eating pig’s flesh"* (66:17). It is the same word (H1593). That fills in what the figure draws on; the figure in 1:29 does not need it to be understood.

**What this verse shows of choosing:**
- Choosing goes together with desiring: *"desired"* and *"chosen"* are paired.
- It is set inside an offer the Lord makes, *"let us reason together"*, with the two sides laid out: willing or refusing, eat or be eaten.
- The wrong choice ends in shame at the thing chosen.
- What was chosen becomes the image of the chooser, and the chooser’s own work becomes the fire.
- Repentance stays open within the same oracle.

## Isaiah 66:3–4 — "These have chosen their own ways, and their soul delights in their abominations"

The oracle opens with the Lord’s greatness and with the person he looks to:
- *"Heaven is my throne, and the earth is my footstool; what is the house that you would build for me"* (v1)
- *"But this is the one to whom I will look: he who is humble and contrite in spirit and trembles at my word"* (v2)

Before any choosing is described, the oracle has set out the inner being the Lord looks for: humbled, contrite, trembling at his word.

Against that stands worship that looks right and is not: *"He who slaughters an ox is like one who kills a man; he who sacrifices a lamb, like one who breaks a dog’s neck"* … *"like one who blesses an idol"* (v3). The outward act is the same as before; what has gone wrong is inside. The verse says where: *"These have chosen their own ways, and their soul delights in their abominations."* Choosing and the soul’s delight stand side by side. They chose their own ways, and their soul (H5315) takes pleasure in what the Lord calls abominations.

The word for delight is the same on both sides:
- their soul *"delights"* in abominations (v3)
- they *"chose that in which I did not delight"* (v4)

Both are H2654A. So the choosing comes down to whose delight governs it: the soul’s own, or the Lord's.

The Lord’s answer is a choice of his own, in the same verb: *"I also will choose harsh treatment for them and bring their fears upon them"* (v4). What he brings is *their fears* (H4035): what they themselves dreaded. Set against v2 and v5, the contrast is in the inner being. The one who *"trembles at my word"* is looked on. Those who chose their own ways are given what they feared.

The reason is given in the same words as 65:12: *"because when I called, no one answered, when I spoke, they did not listen; but they did what was evil in my eyes and chose that in which I did not delight"* (v4). Their choosing is the end of not answering and not listening.

The oracle carries on to those around the choosers:
- The ones who chose their own ways turn on those who tremble: *"Your brothers who hate you and cast you out for my name’s sake"*, and mock them with *"Let the Lord be glorified, that we may see your joy"*. The verdict is *"it is they who shall be put to shame"* (v5).
- The Lord sees beneath the choosing: *"For I know their works and their thoughts"* (v18).
- The tremblers are promised the opposite outcome: *"You shall see, and your heart shall rejoice"* (v14).

**What this verse shows of choosing:**
- Choosing one’s own ways goes together with the soul delighting in what it chose.
- The question is whose delight governs the choice: the soul’s own, or the Lord's.
- The Lord answers the choice with a choice of his own, and what comes is the chooser’s own fears.
- The counter-figure is the humble and contrite one who trembles at his word.
- Those who choose their own ways turn against those who tremble, and end in shame.
- The Lord knows the thoughts as well as the works.

## What batch 1 adds to the account of choosing

These are set beside the three test readings. Nothing in the test readings has to be withdrawn.

**New:**
- **Refusing is choosing (Isa 7:15–16).** The act is one act with two sides, and refusing can go either way. The child refuses the evil. Ahaz refuses the sign, and the people refuse the gentle waters (8:6), with the same verb. This sharpens the test reading of Isa 65:12, which said that not answering and not listening are already choosing: in Isa 8:6 the refusal is named outright.
- **Choosing rests on knowing how (Isa 7:15).** It is a knowing *how* to tell evil from good and turn. In Isa 1 the oracle opens with its absence: *"Israel does not know, my people do not understand"* (1:3).
- **Choosing goes with desire and delight.** It is paired with *"desired"* (1:29) and with the soul’s delight (66:3). In Isa 66 the delight word is the same on both sides, so the choice is a question of whose delight governs it.
- **The choice is laid out as an offer, with both outcomes (Isa 1:16–20).** *"cease to do evil, learn to do good"*; *"willing"* or *"refuse"*; eat, or be eaten. In Isa 7:15 the right choice also ends in eating.
- **What is chosen comes back on the chooser.** Each of these is stated in its verse:
  - *"Destiny"* and *"destine"* (65:11–12, from the test)
  - the chosen gardens and oaks become the image of the chooser withering (1:29–30)
  - the strong man’s own work becomes the spark (1:31)
  - *"I also will choose … their fears"* (66:4)
  - I read these as the same move in different figures. Each verse makes it on its own.
- **Shame at the thing chosen (1:29; 66:5).** The inner end of the wrong choice is being ashamed of what was desired and chosen.
- **The counter-figure (66:2, 5).** The humble and contrite one who trembles at his word is the one the Lord looks to. Those who chose their own ways turn against this person.

**Kept open:**
- The offer to turn stands inside the charge: *"let us reason together"* (1:18) and *"those in her who repent"* (1:27). A wrong choice does not close the matter.

**For the next batches:** whether choosing outside the oracles (the Psalms, the wisdom sayings, and the narratives of people choosing of their own accord) shows the same two-sidedness, and the same tie to knowing and delight. This is to be read from those verses, not assumed.
