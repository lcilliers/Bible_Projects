# Others around a choice, in the human inner being — the account from the §2c readings

**Date:** 2026-10-08 · **Escalation:** #1978 · **Status:** for the researcher's review. It is woven into 10.7 v12 in the same step, on instruction.

**Asked (researcher, verbatim):** *"write the 2c account and weave into 10.7"*.

**What it rests on:** 31 verses, each read in its whole passage in the ESV text in iba.db `verse`. Every quote below was checked against that text.

**Scope:** The account covers people who stand near a choice someone else made: at what was chosen, turned toward it, set against it, standing with the chosen, or knowing of the choice. In all but one of the readings the chooser is God. In 1Sa 20:30 it is Jonathan. God's own acts in reply (Num 17:5; Isa 45:4–5; 49:7; Jer 33:25–26; Rom 11:8; 1Pe 2:8) belong to 13.1 under rule 72. They are named here only where they answer what the others did. Readings beyond what a verse says are marked "I read".

**Companions:** [the account of choosing](m64-choosing-account-v1-20261007.md) (§2a, the chooser's side) and [the account of being chosen](m64-being-chosen-account-v1-20261008.md) (§2b, the chosen person's side).

**Researcher's rulings still open:** where Moses in the breach goes, and where the Levite of Deu 18:6 goes. The account places Moses with *Being chosen* (recommendation in batch 2) and the Levite here. Both are marked in the weave for the researcher to confirm.

The verses on choosing do not stop at the one who chooses and the one chosen. Others stand near: people who go to the place God chose, people who resent the man he chose, people who hear of a choice and argue from it, and people who suffer for the sake of the chosen. In these verses the others are seldom shown feeling anything about the choosing itself. They are shown in what they do at, toward, against, or for what was chosen, and the verses name what moves them.

## At the place God chose

When God chooses a place, the people's inner life at that place is set out in commands. They are not told to admire the place. They are told what to be there.

**Rejoicing.** *"And you shall rejoice before the Lord your God in all that you undertake"* (Deu 12:18). The joy is *"before the Lord"*, and its ground is given in the same chapter: *"in which the Lord your God has blessed you"* (12:7). At the Feast of Booths the joy is the purpose of the blessing: *"because the Lord your God will bless you … so that you will be altogether joyful"* (16:15). The place is where the joy is had. The blessing is what it is about.

The joy is never had alone. Each time the household, the servants and the Levite are named in it, and at the feasts *"the sojourner, the fatherless, and the widow"* too (16:11). The verse after the Feast of Weeks reminds the one rejoicing: *"You shall remember that you were a slave in Egypt"* (16:12).

**Learning to fear.** The tithe is eaten at the place *"that you may learn to fear the Lord your God always"* (14:23). The fear is learned at a meal. In the same passage the money is spent *"for whatever you desire … whatever your appetite craves. And you shall eat there before the Lord your God and rejoice"* (14:26). The passage does not set the fear, the appetite and the joy against each other.

**Remembering.** Passover at the place is eaten with *"the bread of affliction … that all the days of your life you may remember the day when you came out of the land of Egypt"* (16:3).

**Carefulness, against presumption.** When a case is *"too difficult for you"* (17:8), the decision is sought at the place. What is asked of the person then is *"you shall be careful to do according to all that they direct you"* (17:10), and not to turn *"to the right hand or to the left"* (17:11). Presumption is the opposite, and the man who acts presumptuously dies. The end is that *"all the people shall hear and fear and not act presumptuously again"* (17:13). I read this as a person's own judgement reaching its limit and being asked to yield. The passage gives the limit (*"too difficult for you"*) and the carefulness. It does not describe the inner struggle.

**Whose measure.** The chosen place stands against a measure the people already had: *"everyone doing whatever is right in his own eyes"* (12:8), and *"any place that you see"* (12:13).

## Desire, near and far

The soul's desire comes into these verses twice, and it is turned two ways.

- **Given room away from the place.** Ordinary meat need not be eaten at the chosen place. *"If the place that the Lord your God will choose to put his name there is too far from you, then you may kill … and you may eat within your towns whenever you desire"* (Deu 12:21). "Whenever you desire" is the soul's desire (*ʾawwāh*, desire, with *nepeš*, soul). It is spoken of plainly and allowed, with one limit: *"the blood is the life"* (12:23).
- **Drawing a man to the place.** A Levite in any town *"may come when he desires—to the place that the Lord will choose"* (18:6). The Hebrew is "with all the desire of his soul", the same words for desire and soul. He is of the tribe God chose *"to stand and minister"* (18:5), and he is given an equal share when he comes (18:8). His coming is left to that desire.

## Turned toward it from far off

For those far from the chosen city, the city is the direction of prayer. Solomon's prayer gives the hardest case, the people in captivity because they sinned: *"for there is no one who does not sin"* (1Ki 8:46). The inner turning comes in steps:
- *"yet if they turn their heart in the land to which they have been carried captive"* (8:47)
- *"and repent and plead with you … saying, 'We have sinned and have acted perversely and wickedly'"* (8:47)
- *"if they repent with all their heart and with all their soul in the land of their enemies … and pray to you toward their land, … the city that you have chosen"* (8:48)

"Turn" and "repent" are the one verb, *šûb*. The turning happens in the enemy's land. The whole heart and the whole soul are in it. The prayer is turned toward what God chose. Solomon prays it before the one who knows hearts: *"for you, you only, know the hearts of all the children of mankind"* (8:39). He also asks God to do the turning: *"that he may incline our hearts to him"* (8:58).

Nehemiah prays from exile in the same verb. He asks God to remember his word to Moses: *"if you return to me and keep my commandments and do them, … from there I will gather them and bring them to the place that I have chosen"* (Neh 1:9). Here the return is to God himself, and the place is where he brings those who return. Nehemiah's own part comes first: he *"sat down and wept and mourned for days"* (1:4), and confessed *"Even I and my father's house have sinned"* (1:6).

## Set against the chosen

Several verses show others setting themselves against someone God chose, or someone a person chose. Each passage names what moves them.

**Grumbling, and seeking more.** When God's choice of Aaron was challenged, Moses named what lay under it: *"would you seek the priesthood also?"* (Num 16:10). The grumbling at Aaron was against God: *"What is Aaron that you grumble against him?"* (16:11), and God calls it *"the grumblings … which they grumble against you"*, to be made to *"cease from me"* (17:5). Psalm 106 names the inner act: *"men in the camp were jealous of Moses and Aaron"* (Psa 106:16). God's reply was to make the choice plain, with the staff that sprouted. Then the grumbling became terror: *"Are we all to perish?"* (Num 17:13).

**Anger at what a choice costs.** Saul to Jonathan: *"do I not know that you have chosen the son of Jesse to your own shame"* (1Sa 20:30). Saul calls the choice shame and gives his reason: *"neither you nor your kingdom shall be established"* (20:31). His anger is for his house. It goes from insult to a death sentence to a spear thrown at his own son (20:33). Jonathan's choice was made in love, *"for he loved him as he loved his own soul"* (20:17). His own anger answers his father's, and it is grief for the one he chose: *"he was grieved for David, because his father had disgraced him"* (20:34). The choice holds, sealed by an oath *"in the name of the Lord"* (20:42).

**Contempt in the heart.** Michal *"saw King David leaping and dancing before the Lord, and she despised him in her heart"* (2Sa 6:16). The contempt is inward before it is spoken. When it comes out, its measure is honour before others: *"before the eyes of his servants' female servants"* (6:20). David answers by naming the choosing: *"It was before the Lord, who chose me above your father"* (6:21).

**Despising and abhorring.** The servant is addressed as *"one deeply despised, abhorred by the nation"* (Isa 49:7). "Despised" is the same verb as Michal's. The verse turns it round by God's faithfulness and choosing: *"Kings shall see and arise; princes, and they shall prostrate themselves; because of the Lord, who is faithful, … who has chosen you"*.

**A conclusion drawn from ruin.** *"'The Lord has rejected the two clans that he chose'? Thus they have despised my people so that they are no longer a nation in their sight"* (Jer 33:24). Others look at the state of the chosen, decide God has rejected them, and despise them on that ground. God's answer is that his choosing is as fixed as day and night (33:25–26).

**Hatred.** *"If you were of the world, the world would love you as its own; but because you are not of the world, but I chose you out of the world, therefore the world hates you"* (Joh 15:19). The verse gives the cause: the choosing. The world's love goes to *"its own"*. The passage says the hatred fell first on Jesus (15:18), and names under it *"because they do not know him who sent me"* (15:21).

**Rejection.** *"a living stone rejected by men but in the sight of God chosen and precious"* (1Pe 2:4). The word is to reject after testing: men weighed him and set him aside.

**Whose eyes.** In four of these the measure is named, and it is set against God's: *"in his own eyes"* against the place God chooses (Deu 12:8); *"before the eyes of his servants' female servants"* against *"before the Lord"* (2Sa 6:20–21); *"in their sight"* (Jer 33:24); *"rejected by men but in the sight of God chosen"* (1Pe 2:4).

## With the chosen, and for them

Others also stand with the chosen.

- **Asking to share their joy.** *"Remember me, O Lord, … that I may rejoice in the gladness of your nation, that I may glory with your inheritance"* (Psa 106:4–5). He asks God, not the chosen, and he asks it while confessing (106:6).
- **Enduring for them.** *"Therefore I endure everything for the sake of the elect, that they also may obtain the salvation"* (2Ti 2:10). Paul writes this in chains (2:9).
- **Loving them in truth.** *"to the elect lady and her children, whom I love in truth, … because of the truth that abides in us"* (2Jo 1–2). The love is grounded in the truth, not in her being chosen.
- **Believing in the chosen one.** *"a cornerstone chosen and precious, and whoever believes in him will not be put to shame"* (1Pe 2:6). Believing brings honour. For those who do not believe, the same stone is *"a stone of stumbling"* (2:7–8).

The chosen also stand for the others. *"Therefore he said he would destroy them—had not Moses, his chosen one, stood in the breach before him, to turn away his wrath"* (Psa 106:23). The psalm calls Moses chosen at the moment he stands for people who *"forgot God"* (106:21). The same psalm says those people were jealous of him (106:16), and that later *"they made his spirit bitter, and he spoke rashly with his lips"* (106:33). The psalm sets these side by side and does not comment.

## Knowing that a choice was made, and not knowing

**Knowing a choice, and arguing from it.** In the dispute at Jerusalem Peter appeals to what the assembly knows: *"you know that in the early days God made a choice among you, that by my mouth the Gentiles should hear the word of the gospel and believe"* (Act 15:7). The purpose of God's choosing one man lies in others: that they hear and believe. Their hearts were known by God and *"cleansed … by faith"* (15:8–9). The debate ends in silence and listening (15:12), and the assembly's own choosing follows (15:25).

**Knowing that others are chosen, from what was seen.** *"For we know, brothers loved by God, that he has chosen you, because our gospel came to you not only in word, but also in power and in the Holy Spirit and with full conviction"* (1Th 1:4–5). The knowing rests on what Paul saw: faith, love and hope at work (1:3), joy in affliction (1:6), and a turning *"to God from idols"* (1:9).

**Not knowing, and still used.** Cyrus is called for the sake of the chosen: *"For the sake of my servant Jacob, and Israel my chosen, I call you by your name, I name you, though you do not know me"* (Isa 45:4). And *"I equip you, though you do not know me"* (45:5). Knowing is held out as the purpose: *"that you may know that it is I"* (45:3).

**Seeking, and not obtaining.** *"Israel failed to obtain what it was seeking. The elect obtained it, but the rest were hardened"* (Rom 11:7). The rest were seeking. They were given *"eyes that would not see and ears that would not hear"* (11:8). Paul at once refuses the conclusion that this is the end: *"did they stumble in order that they might fall? By no means!"* (11:11). The remnant is chosen *"by grace"*, not works (11:5–6). The passage says how the elect were chosen. It does not say why the rest were not.

## An inner act weighed by what God chooses

In one passage the inner act of the others is itself set against God's choosing. The people fast and ask, *"Why have we humbled ourselves, and you take no knowledge of it?"* (Isa 58:3). They *"delight to know my ways"* and *"delight to draw near to God"* (58:2). Yet *"in the day of your fast you seek your own pleasure, and oppress all your workers"* (58:3). Then: *"Is such the fast that I choose, a day for a person to humble himself?"* (58:5). "Humble himself" is the humbling of the soul, the same words as their own complaint. What God does choose is turned outward: *"to let the oppressed go free"*, *"to share your bread with the hungry"*, *"not to hide yourself from your own flesh"* (58:6–7).

The self-humbling they offered was not wrong in itself. The passage says it stood beside oppression and quarrelling in the same people, and that it was not what he chooses.

## What changes the character (#1919)

- **Desire.**
  - The soul's desire for food is given room away from the chosen place (Deu 12:21).
  - The soul's whole desire draws a Levite to the chosen place (Deu 18:6).
  - The soul delights in its own abominations, beside a wrong choice (Isa 66:3, §2a).
  - *Why:* what the desire is turned toward. The same words for the soul's desire are used for meat in the towns and for the Levite's coming. The verses allow both. What the soul delights in decides the third.
- **Despising.**
  - Michal despises the chosen man *"in her heart"*, by the eyes of people (2Sa 6:16, 20).
  - The servant is *"deeply despised"* by others, and kings will bow (Isa 49:7). Same verb, *bāzâ*.
  - The chosen people themselves *"despised the pleasant land"* (Psa 106:24, §2b; *māʾas*).
  - Others *"despised my people"*, concluding that God had rejected them (Jer 33:24; *nāʾaṣ*).
  - *Why:* who despises what. It is aimed at the chosen by others, and by the chosen at what they were given. Each verse names its own ground: honour before people, the chosen people's ruin, no faith in the promise.
- **Fear.**
  - It is learned at a feast in the chosen place (Deu 14:23).
  - It falls on those who hear of presumption punished (Deu 17:13).
  - It comes as terror when God makes his choice plain to grumblers: *"Are we all to perish?"* (Num 17:13).
  - *Why:* the setting. The first is learned in plenty, the second comes from hearing, the third from facing a choice they resisted.
- **Knowing.**
  - Knowing that God chose is ground for settling a dispute (Act 15:7).
  - Another's being chosen is known from what is seen (1Th 1:4–5).
  - Not knowing God does not stop a man from being called for the sake of the chosen (Isa 45:4–5).
  - Not knowing the Father is named under the world's hatred (Joh 15:21).
  - *Why:* whose knowing, and of what. It is the knowing of a choice, or of the God who chose.
