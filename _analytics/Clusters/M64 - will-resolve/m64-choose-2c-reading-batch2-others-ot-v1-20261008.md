## Numbers 17:5: "Thus I will make to cease from me the grumblings of the people of Israel"

The verse closes a long dispute over who God has chosen. It begins in Numbers 16. Korah and 250 chiefs, *"chosen from the assembly, well-known men"* (16:2; *qārîʾ*, H7148, "called"), rise up: *"Why then do you exalt yourselves above the assembly of the Lord?"* (16:3). Moses answers with God's choosing: *"The one whom he chooses he will bring near to him"* (16:5), and *"the man whom the Lord chooses shall be the holy one"* (16:7).

Moses names what is underneath the complaint:
- *"is it too small a thing for you that the God of Israel has separated you … And would you seek the priesthood also?"* (16:9–10). What they already have is not enough. They seek more.
- *"Therefore it is against the Lord that you and all your company have gathered together. What is Aaron that you grumble against him?"* (16:11). The grumbling at Aaron is against the Lord.

Psalm 106 names the same event with an inner word: *"When men in the camp were jealous of Moses and Aaron, the holy one of the Lord, the earth opened and swallowed up Dathan, and covered the company of Abiram"* (Psa 106:16–17).

Moses himself is *"very angry"* (16:15). The rebels are judged (16:31–35). The next day *"all the congregation of the people of Israel grumbled against Moses and against Aaron, saying, 'You have killed the people of the Lord'"* (16:41). A plague begins, and Aaron *"stood between the dead and the living, and the plague was stopped"* (16:48).

Then God settles it with the staffs: *"And the staff of the man whom I choose shall sprout. Thus I will make to cease from me the grumblings of the people of Israel, which they grumble against you"* (17:5). The grumbling is against Moses and Aaron, and God calls it *"from me"*. In v10 it is *"their grumblings against me"*. The staff is kept *"as a sign for the rebels, that you may make an end of their grumblings against me, lest they die"* (17:10).

The grumbling turns to terror: *"Behold, we perish, we are undone, we are all undone. Everyone who comes near … shall die. Are we all to perish?"* (17:12–13).

"Grumble" is *lûn* (H3885B). The verse shows it as spoken complaint. I read the inner side from the passage: the seeking of the priesthood (16:10), and the jealousy that Psalm 106:16 names.

**What this verse shows of others around the choosing:**
- Grumbling against God's chosen man is counted as grumbling against God (16:11; 17:5, 10).
- Underneath it, by Moses' words, is seeking more than was given (16:10). Psalm 106:16 calls it jealousy.
- God answers by making his choice plain, so that the grumbling will cease.
- When the choice is made plain, the grumbling becomes fear: *"Are we all to perish?"*

## Judges 20:34: "but the Benjaminites did not know that disaster was close upon them"

The chosen men are 10,000 troops: *"there came against Gibeah 10,000 chosen men out of all Israel, and the battle was hard"*. The not-knowing belongs to the other side. The passage follows it through: Benjamin thinks *"They are routed before us, as at the first"* (v32); Israel draws them away because *"they trusted the men in ambush"* (v36); then the smoke rises, *"and the men of Benjamin were dismayed, for they saw that disaster was close upon them"* (v41).

The not-knowing is not bound to the choosing. "Chosen" names picked troops. The HIB/qualifier file has this as "none", and the reading agrees. Under rule 72 it was routed C.

**What this verse shows:**
- Nothing about choosing.
- For knowing (10.2) and fear (10.12), outside this strand: not knowing what is coming, then seeing it, then dismay (v34, v41). This is noted only. It is not placed.

## 1 Samuel 20:30: "do I not know that you have chosen the son of Jesse to your own shame"

The chapter is the covenant between Jonathan and David, tested at Saul's table. Before the meal it says what binds the two men:
- *"Whatever you say, I will do for you"* (v4)
- *"you have brought your servant into a covenant of the Lord with you"* (v8)
- *"And Jonathan made David swear again by his love for him, for he loved him as he loved his own soul"* (v17)

David knows Saul's mind before Jonathan does: *"Your father knows well that I have found favor in your eyes, and he thinks, 'Do not let Jonathan know this, lest he be grieved'"* (v3). Jonathan will not believe it: *"Far from it! … my father does nothing either great or small without disclosing it to me"* (v2). The test is set by Saul's anger: *"if he is angry, then know that harm is determined by him"* (v7).

At the table: *"Then Saul's anger was kindled against Jonathan, and he said to him, 'You son of a perverse, rebellious woman, do I not know that you have chosen the son of Jesse to your own shame, and to the shame of your mother's nakedness?'"* (v30).

Saul names the choice and calls it shame. He gives the reason in the next verse: *"For as long as the son of Jesse lives on the earth, neither you nor your kingdom shall be established. Therefore send and bring him to me, for he shall surely die"* (v31). What Saul fears for is the kingdom of his house.

When Jonathan asks *"Why should he be put to death? What has he done?"* (v32), Saul turns his spear on his own son (v33). Now Jonathan knows: *"So Jonathan knew that his father was determined to put David to death"* (v33).

Jonathan's own anger follows: *"And Jonathan rose from the table in fierce anger and ate no food the second day of the month, for he was grieved for David, because his father had disgraced him"* (v34). His anger is not for himself, though the spear was thrown at him. It is grief for David.

The chapter ends with the two men weeping together and with the oath: *"we have sworn both of us in the name of the Lord, saying, 'The Lord shall be between me and you, and between my offspring and your offspring, forever'"* (v42).

**For OT-62 (anger and will together):**
- Saul's anger is kindled at his son's choice. The chapter gives its reason: Jonathan's choice threatens Saul's kingdom (v31).
- The anger speaks first in shame and insult (v30), then becomes a death sentence (v31), then a spear at his own son (v33).
- Jonathan's anger answers it. It comes from grief for the one he chose, not from the attack on himself (v34).
- The choice holds. Jonathan keeps it through his father's anger, and seals it by an oath before the Lord (v42).

**What this verse shows of others around the choosing:**
- A choice made in love (v17) is named by another as shame (v30).
- The other's anger rests on what the choice costs him: his house and kingdom (v31).
- Knowing passes between the people in the chapter: Saul knows Jonathan's choice (v30), David knows Saul's mind (v3), and at last Jonathan knows it (v33).
- The one who chose suffers for it, and holds to it.

## 2 Samuel 6:16: "and she despised him in her heart"

This verse was read for §2b from David's side. Here it is read for Michal.

*"As the ark of the Lord came into the city of David, Michal the daughter of Saul looked out of the window and saw King David leaping and dancing before the Lord, and she despised him in her heart"* (v16).

"Despised" is *bāzâ* (*to despise*; H0959), and it is *"in her heart"*. The contempt is inward first. It does not come out until David comes home to bless his household: *"How the king of Israel honored himself today, uncovering himself today before the eyes of his servants' female servants, as one of the vulgar fellows shamelessly uncovers himself!"* (v20).

Her measure is honour before others: *"honored himself"*, *"before the eyes of his servants' female servants"*. The joy she despises is joy *"before the Lord"* (v16).

David's answer brings in the choosing: *"It was before the Lord, who chose me above your father and above all his house"* (v21). The chapter names Michal *"the daughter of Saul"* three times (v16, v20, v23). I read the naming as setting her contempt in the house that was passed over. The text does not say this was her reason.

The same word, *bāzâ*, is the word in Isaiah 49:7, *"one deeply despised"*, set against the Lord's choosing (below).

**What this verse shows of others around the choosing:**
- Another's contempt for the chosen begins in the heart, before any word.
- It is aimed at the chosen man's joy before the Lord.
- It measures by the eyes of people. The chosen man measures by the Lord.
- The chosen man answers it by naming the choosing (v21), and he is not moved by it (v22).

## Psalm 106:5: "that I may rejoice in the gladness of your nation, that I may glory with your inheritance"

This verse was read for §2b for the gladness of the chosen. Here it is read for the one who asks to share it.

*"Remember me, O Lord, when you show favor to your people; help me when you save them, that I may look upon the prosperity of your chosen ones, that I may rejoice in the gladness of your nation, that I may glory with your inheritance"* (v4–5).

The psalmist does not claim the gladness. He asks to be remembered so that he may share it. "Rejoice" is *śāmaḥ* (H8055). "Glory" is *hālal* (*to boast*; H1984H). His joy is to be joy *with* the chosen.

He asks it while confessing: *"Both we and our fathers have sinned; we have committed iniquity; we have done wickedness"* (v6). The rest of the psalm shows what the chosen people did (v7–43).

**What this verse shows of others around the choosing:**
- A person can ask to share the joy of the chosen.
- He asks God to remember him, not the chosen to receive him.
- He asks from within confession (v6).

## Psalm 106:23: "had not Moses, his chosen one, stood in the breach before him, to turn away his wrath"

The verse comes in the psalm's account of the calf: *"They made a calf in Horeb and worshiped a metal image. They exchanged the glory of God for the image of an ox that eats grass. They forgot God, their Savior"* (v19–21). *"Therefore he said he would destroy them—had not Moses, his chosen one, stood in the breach before him, to turn away his wrath from destroying them"* (v23).

"Chosen one" is *bāḥîr* (H0972). The psalm calls Moses chosen at the moment he stands between God's wrath and the people. The ones he stands for are those who *"forgot God"*.

Psalm 106 names Moses three times, and each time he is set beside the people:
- *"When men in the camp were jealous of Moses and Aaron, the holy one of the Lord"* (v16)
- *"Moses, his chosen one, stood in the breach before him"* (v23)
- *"They angered him at the waters of Meribah, and it went ill with Moses on their account, for they made his spirit bitter, and he spoke rashly with his lips"* (v32–33)

The people were jealous of him. He stood for them. They made his spirit bitter, and it cost him. The psalm sets the three side by side and does not comment on them.

The psalm gives one more man who stands: *"Then Phinehas stood up and intervened, and the plague was stayed"* (v30). Phinehas is not called chosen.

**For OT-62 (anger and will together):**
- The wrath in v23 is God's (*ḥēmāh*, H2534). Under rule 72 that part goes to Ch 13. The narrative already gives this event from Exodus 32 in 10.13 ("Moses stands between the anger and the people").
- What v23 adds to 10.13 is the word "chosen". The man who stands in the breach is named by his being chosen.
- Psalm 106:32–33, already cited in 10.13 ("Speech that must come out"), is the other side: the chosen man's own spirit embittered by the same people.

**Where it belongs (kept open from §2b).** §2b asked whether this goes with "Choosing together" or with §2c. Read in its psalm, the inner acts of others around Moses are the jealousy (v16) and the embittering (v32–33). In v23 itself the act is the chosen man's: he stands for others before God. I read it as belonging with "Being chosen" in 10.7, as what a chosen man does for others. It is not a case of choosing together. This is for the researcher to rule.

**What this verse shows of others around the choosing:**
- The chosen man stands for the others, before God, when they have forgotten God.
- The same others are jealous of him (v16) and embitter his spirit (v32–33).

## Proverbs 10:20: "The tongue of the righteous is choice silver; the heart of the wicked is of little worth"

"Choice" is *bāḥar* (H0977) as a description: tried, refined silver. No one chooses in the verse. The proverbs around it are about speech: *"The one who conceals hatred has lying lips"* (v18), *"whoever restrains his lips is prudent"* (v19), *"The lips of the righteous feed many"* (v21).

The researcher ruled on Proverbs 8:10 (F2) that it is not HIB as choosing, because "choice" describes the gold. The same holds here: "choice" describes the tongue.

The heart is in the verse, weighed: *"of little worth"*. It is set against the tongue of the righteous.

**What this verse shows:**
- Nothing about choosing.
- For speaking (10.5), outside this strand: what comes from the righteous is weighed as choice silver, and the heart of the wicked as of little worth. This is noted only. It is not placed.

## Isaiah 45:4: "I call you by your name, I name you, though you do not know me"

The oracle is to Cyrus: *"Thus says the Lord to his anointed, to Cyrus, whose right hand I have grasped"* (v1). The choosing is of Israel: *"For the sake of my servant Jacob, and Israel my chosen, I call you by your name, I name you, though you do not know me"* (v4). "Chosen" is *bāḥîr* (H0972).

Cyrus is the other, called for the sake of the chosen. Twice he is told he does not know God:
- *"I name you, though you do not know me"* (v4)
- *"I equip you, though you do not know me"* (v5)

Knowing is the purpose twice:
- for Cyrus: *"that you may know that it is I, the Lord, the God of Israel, who call you by your name"* (v3)
- for all peoples: *"that people may know, from the rising of the sun and from the west, that there is none besides me"* (v6)

**What this verse shows of others around the choosing:**
- A man who does not know God is called and equipped for the sake of the chosen.
- His not knowing does not stop his being used: *"I equip you, though you do not know me"*.
- Knowing is held out as the purpose, for him and for all peoples.
- The calling and equipping are God's acts (rule 72, A). The not-knowing is the man's.

## Isaiah 49:7: "to one deeply despised, abhorred by the nation … because of the Lord, who is faithful … who has chosen you"

The servant speaks first: *"The Lord called me from the womb, from the body of my mother he named my name"* (v1). He also speaks of his own discouragement and his trust in the same breath: *"But I said, 'I have labored in vain; I have spent my strength for nothing and vanity; yet surely my right is with the Lord, and my recompense with my God'"* (v4).

Then the Lord speaks *"to one deeply despised, abhorred by the nation, the servant of rulers: 'Kings shall see and arise; princes, and they shall prostrate themselves; because of the Lord, who is faithful, the Holy One of Israel, who has chosen you'"* (v7).

The others' inner acts are two:
- *"deeply despised"*: *bāzâ* (H0959) with the soul (*nepeš*, H5315J). It is the same verb as Michal's *"despised him in her heart"* (2Sa 6:16). The ESV renders the phrase "deeply despised". The text does not settle whose soul is meant.
- *"abhorred by the nation"*: *tāʿab* (*to abhor*; H8581)

Set against both: kings will see and rise, and princes will bow. The verse gives the reason in two parts: *"because of the Lord, who is faithful"*, and *"who has chosen you"*.

**What this verse shows of others around the choosing:**
- The chosen servant is despised and abhorred by others.
- The verse turns that around by the Lord's faithfulness and his choosing, not by anything the servant does.
- The servant's own inner state is in the passage: labour that seems in vain, and trust that his right is with the Lord (v4).

## Isaiah 58:5: "Is such the fast that I choose, a day for a person to humble himself?"

Here the inner act is what is weighed by God's choosing. The chapter opens on people who look devout:
- *"Yet they seek me daily and delight to know my ways, as if they were a nation that did righteousness … they delight to draw near to God"* (v2)
- *"'Why have we fasted, and you see it not? Why have we humbled ourselves, and you take no knowledge of it?'"* (v3)

The answer is about what is going on alongside the fast: *"Behold, in the day of your fast you seek your own pleasure, and oppress all your workers. Behold, you fast only to quarrel and to fight and to hit with a wicked fist"* (v3–4).

Then: *"Is such the fast that I choose, a day for a person to humble himself? Is it to bow down his head like a reed, and to spread sackcloth and ashes under him? Will you call this a fast, and a day acceptable to the Lord?"* (v5).

"Humble himself" in v5 is *ʿānâ* (*to afflict*; H6031B) with the soul (H5315I), the same phrase as *"humbled ourselves"* in v3. The self-humbling of the soul is what they offered. God asks whether that is what he chooses.

What he does choose is turned outward: *"Is not this the fast that I choose: to loose the bonds of wickedness, to undo the straps of the yoke, to let the oppressed go free, and to break every yoke? Is it not to share your bread with the hungry … and not to hide yourself from your own flesh?"* (v6–7).

"Delight" in v2 (twice) is *ḥāpēṣ* (H2654A). It is the same word as *"chose that in which I did not delight"* in Isaiah 66:4, read in §2a. In v3 *"your own pleasure"* is the noun from the same root (H2656).

**What this verse shows of others around the choosing:**
- A person's inner act, the humbling of the soul, is weighed by what God chooses.
- The self-humbling, done while seeking one's own pleasure and oppressing others, is not what he chooses (v3–5).
- What he chooses is turned toward others: freeing, sharing, not hiding from one's own flesh (v6–7).
- Delight to know his ways and to draw near (v2) can stand beside oppression in the same people.

## Jeremiah 33:24: "'The Lord has rejected the two clans that he chose'? Thus they have despised my people"

*"Have you not observed that these people are saying, 'The Lord has rejected the two clans that he chose'? Thus they have despised my people so that they are no longer a nation in their sight"* (v24).

Others look at the state of the chosen and draw a conclusion about God: he has rejected them. Their contempt rests on that conclusion. "Despised" is *nāʾaṣ* (*to spurn*; H5006). The measure is their own: *"in their sight"*. "Rejected" is *māʾas* (H3988A), the same verb as *"refuse the evil"* in Isaiah 7:15 (§2a).

God answers with the fixed order of creation: *"If I have not established my covenant with day and night and the fixed order of heaven and earth, then I will reject the offspring of Jacob and David my servant and will not choose one of his offspring to rule"* (v25–26). It ends: *"For I will restore their fortunes and will have mercy on them"* (v26).

**What this verse shows of others around the choosing:**
- Others judge from the chosen people's ruin that God has rejected them.
- Their contempt follows from that judgement, and it is measured *"in their sight"*.
- God's answer is that his choosing stands as fixed as day and night.

## What batch 2 adds

**What others do around a choice, in these verses:**
- grumble against the chosen man, and God counts it as against himself (Num 16:11; 17:5, 10)
- seek more than they were given, and are jealous (Num 16:10; Psa 106:16)
- name a choice as shame, and are angry at what it costs them (1Sa 20:30–31)
- despise the chosen in the heart, by the eyes of people (2Sa 6:16, 20)
- despise and abhor the chosen servant (Isa 49:7)
- conclude from the chosen people's ruin that God rejected them, and despise them in their own sight (Jer 33:24)
- ask to share the joy of the chosen (Psa 106:5)
- not know God, and still be called and equipped for the sake of the chosen (Isa 45:4–5)
- offer an inner act, the humbling of the soul, that God's choosing weighs and does not accept (Isa 58:3–5)

**Whose measure.** It comes up in four verses. Michal measures by *"the eyes of his servants' female servants"* (2Sa 6:20). The people in Jer 33:24 measure *"in their sight"*. Over against both, David celebrates *"before the Lord"* (2Sa 6:21), and Isa 49:7 gives the Lord's faithfulness and choosing. The same theme is in batch 1 (Deu 12:8) and in §2a ("whose measure").

**What God does in reply.** In Num 17:5 he makes the choice plain. In Isa 49:7 he promises that kings will bow. In Jer 33:25–26 he points to the fixed order of day and night. Under rule 72 these acts are God's and go to Ch 13. They are recorded here because each one answers what the others did.

**The same word in two verses.** Michal's *"despised"* (2Sa 6:16) and the servant *"deeply despised"* (Isa 49:7) are the same verb, *bāzâ* (H0959). The verses are side by side. The text does not join them.

**Not about choosing:** Judg 20:34 and Pro 10:20. Each has an inner-being note for another section (knowing and fear; speaking), noted and not placed.

**Already read:** Isa 7:16 and Isa 66:3, in §2a.
