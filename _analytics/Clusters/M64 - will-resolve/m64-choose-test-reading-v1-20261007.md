## Joshua 24:15 — "choose this day whom you will serve"

Joshua does not open with the choice. He opens with eleven verses in which the Lord speaks of what *he* did:
- *"I took your father Abraham from beyond the River"*
- *"I brought you out"*
- *"I gave them into your hand"*
- *"it was not by your sword or by your bow"* (v3–12)

The recital starts where the people's story started: *"your fathers lived beyond the Euphrates … and they served other gods"* (v2). Before Israel ever chose, the Lord had already taken Abraham out from among those gods. The choice put to the people at Shechem comes at the end of that history, not at the start of it.

The choice is not whether to serve. Serving is taken for granted: the fathers served gods beyond the River, and the Amorites serve the gods of the land. The only question is *whom*. So the choosing is a choice of master.

It begins in the eyes: *"if it is evil in your eyes to serve the Lord, choose this day"*. Joshua treats the people's own view of what is good and evil as where the choice starts, and he leaves it open to them. He even names the alternatives for them. Then he makes his own choice in front of them, before they answer: *"as for me and my house, we will serve the Lord."*

The people choose from memory: *"it is the Lord our God who brought us … up from the land of Egypt, … and who did those great signs in our sight"* (v17). Their choosing rests on what they saw.

Joshua's reply is not what a speaker would say if he only wanted agreement: *"You are not able to serve the Lord, for he is a holy God. He is a jealous God"* (v19). Choosing and being able to carry the choice out are not the same thing, and Joshua says so plainly. The people insist, *"No, but we will serve the Lord"* (v21).

At that point the choice turns back on the one who made it: *"You are witnesses against yourselves that you have chosen the Lord"* (v22). Once spoken, a choice becomes a testimony the chooser will be held to.

It also exposes what is still in the house: *"Then put away the foreign gods that are among you, and incline your heart to the Lord"* (v23). The people have just chosen the Lord, yet the foreign gods are still among them. So the choice spoken with the mouth has to be followed by two things:
- putting something away
- an inner turning, the heart inclined (H5186, "to stretch, bend")

Joshua does not take the spoken choice to be the whole of it.

The passage records how long the choice held: *"Israel served the Lord all the days of Joshua, and all the days of the elders who outlived Joshua and had known all the work that the Lord did for Israel"* (v31). The verse ties those years to the elders who *had known the work*. I read this as saying that the choice held as long as there were people who remembered what it had been made from. The verse states the timing; it does not state the cause.

**What choosing is here:**
- It is the answer to what God has already done.
- It is a choice of whom to serve, never whether to serve.
- It starts in how the matter looks to the chooser's eyes.
- It is made openly, and binds the one who makes it.
- It is not the same as being able to carry it out.
- It is not complete until the other gods are put away and the heart is bent toward the Lord.

## Psalm 119:30 — "I have chosen the way of faithfulness"

This verse sits in the stanza that begins *"My soul clings to the dust; give me life according to your word!"* (v25). Two verses before it the psalmist says *"My soul melts away for sorrow; strengthen me according to your word!"* (v28). The person who chooses here is low and spent, not standing strong over the options.

Around the choosing the psalmist is mostly asking:
- *"teach me"*
- *"Make me understand"*
- *"strengthen me"*
- *"Put false ways far from me"* (v26–29)

The false way is not refused by his own strength alone. He asks God to put it at a distance. Then he states the choice: *"I have chosen the way of faithfulness; I set your rules before me"* (v30). The choosing is joined at once to keeping something in view: he puts the rules in front of himself, where he can see them.

The next verse uses the same word as the first verse of the stanza:
- v25: *"My soul clings to the dust"* (H1692)
- v31: *"I cling to your testimonies, O Lord"* (H1692)

The verb is the same, and what it holds on to has changed. I read the choice in v30 as the turn between the two: the soul that was clinging to the dust now clings to the testimonies. The stanza places the verbs in this order; it does not name the change.

The stanza ends forward-looking, with a condition: *"I will run in the way of your commandments when you enlarge my heart!"* (v32). The choice has been made, but running in the chosen way waits on something God does to the heart.

**What choosing is here:**
- It is made from weakness.
- It is surrounded by prayer.
- It needs God to put the false way far off.
- It is held by keeping the rules in view and clinging to them.
- It turns what the soul clings to.
- It does not by itself give the heart room to run; that is asked of God.

## Isaiah 65:12 — "chose what I did not delight in"

This is an oracle. It opens with God, not with the people:
- *"I was ready to be sought by those who did not ask for me … I said, “Here I am, here I am,”"* (v1)
- *"I spread out my hands all the day to a rebellious people, who walk in a way that is not good, following their own devices"* (v2)

The wrong choosing in v12 is made in front of a God who is calling and holding out his hands.

The oracle shows what they turned to instead:
- sacrifices *"in gardens"*, sitting *"in tombs"*, eating *"pig’s flesh"* (v3–4)
- a pride that says *"do not come near me, for I am too holy for you"* (v5)
- they *"forget my holy mountain"* and *"set a table for Fortune and fill cups of mixed wine for Destiny"* (v11)

They put their trust in luck and fate.

The answer turns on their own word: *"I will destine you to the sword"* (v12). In Hebrew, "Destiny" (Meni, H4507) and "destine" (manah, H4487) come from the same root. They poured cups for Destiny, and the Lord destines them. What they chose to trust becomes the form their judgement takes.

The verse then gives the reason, and the reason is in the inner being: *"because, when I called, you did not answer; when I spoke, you did not listen, but you did what was evil in my eyes and chose what I did not delight in."* The choosing does not stand alone. It is the far end of not answering and not listening. When they turned away from the voice calling them, that was already a choice, and choosing what God does not delight in is where it ends.

There is a phrase to set beside Joshua 24. There, the choice turned on what was *"evil in your eyes"*. Here they *"did what was evil in my eyes"*. The measure has moved from their eyes to God's, and his delight is the standard their choice is judged by.

In the same oracle the word "chosen" is used of the others:
- *"my chosen shall possess it"* (v9)
- *"You shall leave your name to my chosen for a curse"* (v15)

So in one passage there are those who chose what God did not delight in, and those God calls *"my chosen"*. The outcome sets the two side by side and reaches the inner being on both sides: *"my servants shall sing for gladness of heart, but you shall cry out for pain of heart and shall wail for breaking of spirit"* (v14). The choice ends in the heart and spirit of the one who made it.

**What choosing is here:**
- It is the answer to a God who calls and holds out his hands.
- Not answering and not listening are already choosing.
- What a person trusts in, here Fortune and Destiny, comes back on them as their judgement.
- The measure is God's delight, not the chooser's own eyes.
- The end of the choice is felt in the chooser's heart and spirit.

## A note on the test

I read each verse on its own terms. Two things came up in more than one of them, and I only note them:
- **"In whose eyes":** Jos 24:15 has *"evil in your eyes"*, and Isa 65:12 has *"evil in my eyes"*.
- **What follows the choice:** in Jos 24 the heart must be inclined, in Psa 119 the heart must be enlarged, and in Isa 65 the heart ends in gladness or in pain.

Neither is offered as a pattern; whether they are one is a question for the remaining verses.
