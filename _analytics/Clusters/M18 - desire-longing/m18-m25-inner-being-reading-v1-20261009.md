## 1. Wanting life itself, and wanting to "see good"

**Verses:** Psa 34:12; 1Pe 3:10. Compared with Job 7:7 (in Ch 2).

**What the inner being does.** The psalm does not command anyone to want life. It assumes the want and asks it as a question: *"What man is there who desires life and loves many days, that he may see good?"* (Psa 34:12). The want has an aim: "that he may see good". Days are wanted for what can be seen in them.

**What the verses do with the want.** It is not answered with more days. It is answered with the mouth and the will: *"Keep your tongue from evil and your lips from speaking deceit. Turn away from evil and do good; seek peace and pursue it"* (34:13–14). The psalmist is teaching "the fear of the Lord" (34:11). So the want for life is put inside a lesson about fearing God, and it is worked out first in speech.

**Compare: two kinds of wanting in the same psalm.** *"The young lions suffer want and hunger; but those who seek the Lord lack no good thing"* (34:10). The lions are the picture of strength, and they still go hungry. **I read** the figure as a want that strength cannot satisfy, set against a want that is met in seeking.

**Why the relationship matters: Peter's setting.** Peter quotes the psalm in a different situation, people who wrong you: *"Do not repay evil for evil or reviling for reviling, but on the contrary, bless"* (1Pe 3:9). For Peter, the want for good days is tested in how you answer someone who reviles you.
- In the psalm it is about one's own tongue before God.
- In Peter it is about one's tongue toward an enemy.

The want is the same. The relationship decides what it requires.

**What lies behind it: the same phrase, lost.** Job uses the same words for the opposite state: *"Remember that my life is a breath; my eye will never again see good"* (Job 7:7, in Ch 2). The psalm's "desires life … that he may see good" and Job's "never again see good" are one phrase. **I read** that the want for life is a want to see good. Where the hope of seeing good is gone, life itself is weighed as a breath, and then loathed (Job 7:15–16, in Ch 2). The verses do not say this is a sequence. They use the one phrase for both states.

**Where:**
- **10.6, new paragraph *Wanting life*.** It comes before "Wanting can end in death", so the chapter moves from the want for life to the want turned toward death.
- **Ch 2 *Life weighed*:** one sentence pointing to the shared phrase with Job 7:7 (see §11 on Ch 2).

## 2. Pleasures that own a person, and wanting retrained

**Verses:** Tit 3:3; Tit 2:12; Luk 8:14. Compared with Luk 8:13, 15.

**The figure: slavery.** *"For we ourselves were once foolish, disobedient, led astray, slaves to various passions and pleasures, passing our days in malice and envy, hated by others and hating one another"* (Tit 3:3).
- A slave has a master. The one who pursues pleasure has become the thing that is pursued, owned by what he wanted.
- "Various" matters. There is not one master but many.
- The life of the slave is spent: "passing our days".

****I read** the figure as wanting that has changed sides. It began as the person's act and became the person's owner.**

**Relationships show it.** The verse does not stop at the inner state. It runs straight on to what happens between people: "malice and envy, hated by others and hating one another". In this verse the inner slavery and the outer hatred are one description. Paul does not separate them.

**What frees: not a stronger want.** *"But when the goodness and loving kindness of God our Savior appeared, he saved us, not because of works done by us in righteousness, but according to his own mercy, by the washing of regeneration and renewal of the Holy Spirit"* (3:4–5).
- The answer to being owned by pleasures is God's own "goodness and loving kindness" appearing.
- The verse rules out "works done by us".

**Compare the other Titus verse: a slave becomes a pupil.** *"For the grace of God has appeared … training us to renounce ungodliness and worldly passions, and to live self-controlled, upright, and godly lives"* (2:11–12).
- In 3:3 the passions are a master. In 2:12 grace is a teacher, and the person now does something: renounces.
- **I read** the change as one from being driven to being trained. The will that could not refuse is now taught to.

**And the wanting is not killed.** The same passage ends with Christ purifying *"a people for his own possession who are zealous for good works"* (2:14). Zeal is a strong wanting. The passions are renounced, and a people zealous for good is what is left. **I read** this as wanting redirected, not removed. The zeal word belongs to the jealousy family, noted for OT-95.

**Compare: thorns in the parable of the sower.** *"they are those who hear, but as they go on their way they are choked by the cares and riches and pleasures of life, and their fruit does not mature"* (Luk 8:14).

Explore the figure:
- **The thorns "grew up with it"** (8:7). They are not instead of the word. They grow beside it, in the same soil. **I read**: the word is received, and other wants grow in the same heart and take its room.
- **The three thorns are different things.** Cares are fear about what may be lost. Riches are having. Pleasures are enjoying. The anxious, the rich and the pleasure-seeker are choked in the same way.
- **"As they go on their way"** places it in ordinary life. It is gradual, not a single moment of rejection.
- **"Their fruit does not mature."** The word is not dead in them. It is stunted.

**Why they differ: the parable's four grounds.**

| Ground | What the parable says | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Rock | They "receive it with joy. But these have no root" (8:13) | Joy alone does not hold it; they fall away in testing |
| Thorns | Choked by cares, riches and pleasures (8:14) | Pleasure crowds it out; the fruit does not mature |
| Good soil | "Hold it fast in an honest and good heart, and bear fruit with patience" (8:15) | Fruit |

The difference the parable names is the heart's holding, and patience. It is not the absence of feeling: the rock had joy.

**Where:** **10.6, new paragraph *Wanting that owns the one who wants*.** It goes after "Desire can be evil in itself", with a pointer to Ch 14 for "renewal of the Holy Spirit" and to Ch 4 for Luk 8:15.

## 3. Enjoyment: the same words praised and condemned

**Verses:** Ecc 3:12; 5:18; 8:15. Beside Ecc 2:24–26 and 6:2 (already in 10.6). Compared with Luk 12:19–20 (in 10.6 and Ch 5).

**Where enjoyment comes in.** The Preacher, who calls everything vanity, three more times commends eating, drinking and joy. Each time it comes straight after something he cannot resolve:
- **3:12** follows *"he has put eternity into man's heart, yet so that he cannot find out what God has done from the beginning to the end"* (3:11).
- **5:18** follows the love of money that is never satisfied (5:10), the rich man whose *"full stomach … will not let him sleep"* (5:12), and the man who *"all his days … eats in darkness in much vexation and sickness and anger"* (5:17).
- **8:15** follows the vanity that *"there are righteous people to whom it happens according to the deeds of the wicked"* (8:14).

**I read** that enjoyment is not offered as a solution to any of these. He never answers the riddle. Enjoyment is how a person lives inside what the mind cannot settle.

**What lies behind it: a lot, a gift, a power.**
- The words he uses are "his lot", "the days of his life that God has given him", "this is God's gift to man" (3:13), "the gift of God" (5:19).
- Even the ability is given: *"to whom God has given wealth and possessions and power to enjoy them, and to accept his lot"* (5:19). Set this beside 6:2, already in 10.6: the man to whom God gives everything, *"yet God does not give him power to enjoy them"*.
- Having and enjoying are two different gifts. The second is the one that was missing.

**What it does inside.** *"For he will not much remember the days of his life because God keeps him occupied with joy in his heart"* (5:20). The brevity is still there ("the few days"). The heart is held by joy and does not brood over it. **I read** this as the opposite of 5:17's eating "in darkness": the same act of eating, a different inner state.

**Enjoyment is not alone.** In 3:12 it is joined to conduct: "to be joyful and to do good as long as they live". The passage ends in fear: *"God has done it, so that people fear before him"* (3:14).

**Compare: the same words in Luke 12.** The rich fool says to his soul, *"Soul, you have ample goods laid up for many years; relax, eat, drink, be merry"* (Luk 12:19). The Preacher says *"man has nothing better under the sun but to eat and drink and be joyful"* (Ecc 8:15). The words are almost the same. One is commended and one is called "Fool!" (Luk 12:20).

**Why different?** The verses themselves give the differences:

| | Rich fool (Luke 12) | The Preacher |
|---|---|---|
| Who it is said to | His own soul | Before God |
| What the days are | "Many years" assumed, his own | "The few days … that God has given him" |
| What the enjoyment rests on | Goods laid up | "His lot", received |
| What follows | "This night your soul is required of you" | "God keeps him occupied with joy in his heart" |

This is a clear case of a word changing character by circumstance (#1919). Eating, drinking and being merry are one act. They are a gift when received as one's given portion before God. They are folly when they rest on stored goods and on years one presumes to own.

**Where:**
- **10.6 Ecclesiastes paragraph**, widened. Ecc 2:24–26 is already there.
- **Ch 11 §4** *What changes the character*: a short entry for "eat, drink, be merry/joyful", Ecc 8:15 against Luk 12:19–20.

## 4. Not knowing what is good

**Verse:** Ecc 6:12. Previously held under OT-96. That hold is withdrawn: the filter is a working aid, not a decision.

**The question.** *"For who knows what is good for man while he lives the few days of his vain life, which he passes like a shadow?"* (Ecc 6:12). It closes the passage already in 10.6: the man who lacks nothing yet cannot enjoy it (6:2), and *"Better is the sight of the eyes than the wandering of the appetite"* (6:9).

**What it shows of the inner being.** Wanting assumes that a person knows what is good to want. The Preacher questions that assumption. The difficulty is not only getting the good. It is knowing which good it is.

**The figure: "passes like a shadow".** A shadow has shape but no substance, and it moves without being held. **I read** it as days that have form but cannot be grasped. That is why "what is good for man" cannot be fixed in advance.

**Compare with §3.** The book's own answer in 2:24, 3:12, 5:18 and 8:15 is not knowledge. It is receiving enjoyment as given. 6:12 is the question that the enjoyment passages live alongside. Both stay.

**Where:** **10.6 Ecclesiastes paragraph**, as the question at its end.

## 5. Longing aimed at God: looking, asking, delighting

**Verses:** Psa 27:4; Psa 16:11 (with 16:2–8); Psa 119:40, 77.

**One desire, narrowed.** *"One thing have I asked of the Lord, that will I seek after: that I may dwell in the house of the Lord all the days of my life, to gaze upon the beauty of the Lord and to inquire in his temple"* (Psa 27:4).
- **It is narrowed to one thing.** Elsewhere in 10.6 wanting is scattered: "the wandering of the appetite" (Ecc 6:9) and "various passions" (Tit 3:3).
- **It is both asked and sought.** It is a request to God and the person's own pursuit.
- **It is a desire to look:** "to gaze upon the beauty".

**Compare: the eye again.** 10.6 already has desire entering by the eye toward wrong (Mat 5:28; Pro 6:25), and the woman who "saw that the tree … was a delight to the eyes" (Gen 3:6; §6). In Psa 27 the act is the same, looking with desire, and it is turned to the Lord. **I read** that Scripture does not cure desiring by looking away. It changes what is looked at. The verses set these looks side by side by their words. The reading of them together is mine.

**What lies behind it: fear and abandonment.** The psalm opens *"whom shall I fear?"* with an army encamped (27:1–3). Later: *"For my father and my mother have forsaken me, but the Lord will take me in"* (27:10). So the one desire is spoken by someone under threat and left by his nearest.

**The relationship answers first.** *"You have said, 'Seek my face.' My heart says to you, 'Your face, Lord, do I seek'"* (27:8). The heart's seeking is a reply to an invitation. The desire does not start alone.

**Psalm 16: desire collapsed into God.**
- *"I have no good apart from you"* (16:2). This is the *ṭôb* word again, the one the filter had set aside as a qualifier. Here it carries the whole verse. Every good is gathered into the relationship.

Explore the figures:
- *"The Lord is my chosen portion and my cup; you hold my lot. The lines have fallen for me in pleasant places"* (16:5–6). Portion, cup, lot and the surveyor's lines are all pictures of an inheritance that is allotted.
- **I read** that desire here is satisfied by what is given and measured out, not by what is seized.
- Against it stands *"The sorrows of those who run after another god shall multiply"* (16:4). Desire that runs after something else multiplies sorrow.

**The right hand.**
- *"because he is at my right hand, I shall not be shaken"* (16:8)
- *"at your right hand are pleasures forevermore"* (16:11)

The Lord at my right hand steadies. At his right hand are pleasures. **I read** that the nearness that keeps a person unshaken is the same nearness that is the pleasure. The psalm uses the one position for both.

**Psalm 119: longing and delight, joined to asking for life.**
- *"Turn my eyes from looking at worthless things; and give me life in your ways"* (119:37), then *"Behold, I long for your precepts; in your righteousness give me life!"* (119:40). The eye is turned from worthless things, and the longing is set on the precepts. Yet the life is still asked for. The longing does not give it.
- *"Let your mercy come to me, that I may live; for your law is my delight"* (119:77). It comes just after *"in faithfulness you have afflicted me"* (119:75).
- Compare 119:70, already in 10.6: "their heart is unfeeling like fat, but I delight in your law". There, delight stands against other people's dullness. Here, it stands in his own affliction and is the ground on which he asks for mercy.

**Where:** **10.6, the paragraph "Wanting can be aimed at the living God"**, rewritten around looking (Psa 27), portion (Psa 16) and longing with asking (Psa 119).

## 6. Seeing that it is good: God's look, the creatures, the woman's look

**Verses:** Gen 1:25 (previously held under OT-96; the hold is withdrawn); Gen 2:9 (confirmed, in Ch 2). Read with Gen 3:6 and Gen 1:31.

**The phrase recurs.**
- *"And God saw that it was good"* (Gen 1:25). After man is made: *"God saw everything that he had made, and behold, it was very good"* (1:31). The first seeing in Scripture is God's. It looks at what he made and approves it.
- The trees he made are *"pleasant to the sight and good for food"* (Gen 2:9).
- Then: *"So when the woman saw that the tree was good for food, and that it was a delight to the eyes, and that the tree was to be desired to make one wise, she took of its fruit and ate"* (3:6).

**Compare.** "Saw that it was good" is first God's verdict on his work, and then the woman's verdict on the forbidden tree. The words are close ("good for food" is word for word in 2:9 and 3:6, and "pleasant" in 2:9 and "desired" in 3:6 are the same *ḥāmad* word).

**Why different?**
- God's seeing is a judgement on his own work.
- The woman's seeing is a judgement on something God had withheld. Her look goes on to "desired to make one wise", and the desire becomes taking.
- **I read** that what changes the character of the look is not the object, which really was "good for food" (2:9). It is whose verdict is in charge. Three acts are named in 3:6 in order: seeing, desiring, taking. The verse states the order. Whether it holds every time, the verse does not say.

**Where:**
- **10.6**, beside Mat 5:28 and Pro 6:25 in the paragraph on desire entering by the eye: Gen 3:6, with 2:9.
- **13.1 Delight**, or **13.4 Nature**: God's look of approval on what he made (Gen 1:25, 31). This bears on the parked image question (#1984). Your call where it sits.

## 7. The thirst answered, and thirst turned into a quarrel

**Verses:** Joh 4:14; Joh 6:35; Exo 17:3; Judg 15:18 (decision moved from "carried" to "new perspective"). Compared with Jer 2:13 (in Ch 2).

**Both crowds ask for the gift literally.**
- The woman: *"Sir, give me this water, so that I will not be thirsty or have to come here to draw water"* (Joh 4:15).
- The crowd: *"Sir, give us this bread always"* (6:34). They had sought him *"because you ate your fill of the loaves"* (6:26).
- Both times the answer moves from the thing to a person: *"I am the bread of life; whoever comes to me shall not hunger, and whoever believes in me shall never thirst"* (6:35).
- **I read** that the want is real and is not refused. It is met by coming and believing, acts toward someone, not by a supply.

**The figure: a spring, not a cistern.** *"The water that I will give him will become in him a spring of water welling up to eternal life"* (4:14). Set it beside Jeremiah, in Ch 2: *"they have forsaken me, the fountain of living waters, and hewed out cisterns for themselves, broken cisterns that can hold no water"* (Jer 2:13).
- A cistern holds what is poured into it, and a broken one loses it.
- A spring wells up from inside.
- "Everyone who drinks of this water will be thirsty again" (4:13) describes a want that must be refilled. "Never be thirsty again" describes a want met at its source, and the source is "in him".

**The same bodily thirst, answered three ways.**

| Who | What they say | To whom |
|---|---|---|
| Israel at Rephidim | *"Why did you bring us up out of Egypt, to kill us and our children and our livestock with thirst?"* (Exo 17:3) | Moses. It ends as testing the Lord: *"Is the Lord among us or not?"* (17:7) |
| Samson, "very thirsty" | *"You have granted this great salvation by the hand of your servant, and shall I now die of thirst?"* (Judg 15:18) | God, from what God has already done. Then "his spirit returned, and he revived" (15:19) |
| The psalmist | *"My soul thirsts for God"* (Psa 42:2, in 10.6) | God himself, as the thing thirsted for |

The need is the same. **What differs is to whom the thirst speaks, and what it remembers.**
- Israel remembers Egypt and accuses.
- Samson remembers the salvation and asks.
- **I read** that thirst itself does not decide the inner response. The relationship it is spoken into does.

**Where:** **10.6, "The thirst is answered"** paragraph, rewritten: spring and cistern, the literal ask, and the three answers to thirst. The wilderness craving paragraph (Num 11) gets a pointer to it.

## 8. Wanting shaped by fear: the road back to Egypt

**Verses:** Jer 42:22 (with 42:10–21); Exo 14:12. Compared with Num 11:18 (in 10.6).

**Jeremiah 42 in order.**
1. They ask: *"whatever the Lord our God says … we will do it"* (42:20).
2. God answers with a promise: *"Do not fear the king of Babylon … for I am with you"* (42:11).
3. They want the opposite: *"No, we will go to the land of Egypt, where we shall not see war or hear the sound of the trumpet or be hungry for bread"* (42:14).
4. The reply: *"the sword that you fear shall overtake you there … and the famine of which you are afraid shall follow close after you"* (42:16), and *"in the place where you desire to go to live"* (42:22).

**What lies behind the desire.** The verses name it: fear of war and of hunger. The place is desired as an absence: no war, no trumpet, no hunger. **I read**:
- What is wanted is not Egypt. It is freedom from what they fear.
- The fear goes with them. The things feared "follow close after".
- They had asked for God's word with the wanting already settled (42:20–21 states that they did not obey).

**Compare the sea.** *"it would have been better for us to serve the Egyptians than to die in the wilderness"* (Exo 14:12), said as *"they feared greatly"* (14:10). Fear makes slavery preferable. In the wilderness, craving made Egypt look better too: *"For it was better for us in Egypt"* (Num 11:18, in 10.6).

**Why the relationship matters: the answer is the same.**
- At the sea: *"Fear not, stand firm, and see the salvation of the Lord"* (Exo 14:13).
- In Jeremiah: *"Do not fear … for I am with you"* (Jer 42:11).

The answer to wanting shaped by fear is addressed to the fear, not to the want.

Three passages, at the sea, in the wilderness and after Jerusalem fell, name Egypt as the place wanted back. That is in the verses. **I read** it as the known past preferred to an unknown future with God. The verses do not say this.

**Where:**
- **10.6, new paragraph *Wanting shaped by fear***, after the wilderness paragraph.
- **10.12 Fear:** a pointer, with Exo 14:12 beside 14:10 and 14:13, already there.

## 9. Greed, and the possessions that hold the will

**Verses:** Pro 15:27; Mat 19:17–22 (§10); Psa 68:30.

**Who greed harms.** *"Whoever is greedy for unjust gain troubles his own household, but he who hates bribes will live"* (Pro 15:27).
- Already in 10.6, greed *"takes away the life of its possessors"* (Pro 1:19).
- Here it reaches his household.
- The answer is not a lack of desire. It is a counter-desire: he "hates" bribes. **I read** it as one passion held off by another.

**Possessions as the reason a will gives way.** *"When the young man heard this he went away sorrowful, for he had great possessions"* (Mat 19:22). Taken up in §10.

**What the nations want.** *"Trample underfoot those who lust after tribute; scatter the peoples who delight in war"* (Psa 68:30). The verse before says *"kings shall bear gifts to you"* (68:29).
- Tribute taken by force stands against gifts brought to God.
- Delight in war stands against the nations' coming: *"Cush shall hasten to stretch out her hands to God"* (68:31).
- **I read** delight that feeds on taking and on conflict, set in the same psalm beside giving and coming.

**Where:** **10.6, the possessions paragraph** (Pro 15:27, Mat 19:22), and **the delight list** (Psa 68:30, "on war and tribute").

## 10. The will invited, refused, bound and set on godliness

**Verses:** Mat 19:17–22; Joh 5:40 (with 5:42–44); Rut 3:13 (with 4:4–6); 2Ti 3:12. Compared with Isa 1:19 and 1Ti 6:9 (in 10.7).

**The will invited with an "if".** The man asks about doing: *"what good deed must I do?"* (Mat 19:16). Jesus answers twice with the will:
- *"If you would enter life, keep the commandments"* (19:17)
- *"If you would be perfect, go, sell … and come, follow me"* (19:21)

He has kept the commandments and still asks, *"What do I still lack?"* (19:20). He senses a lack. When the will is asked for the thing he holds, he *"went away sorrowful, for he had great possessions"* (19:22).

Probes:
- The will, the possessions and the feeling are named in one verse. The sorrow is a will that would not, while knowing what it lacked.
- The disciples ask *"Who then can be saved?"* and are told, *"With man this is impossible, but with God all things are possible"* (19:25–26). That fits Rom 9:16, already in 10.7: "not on human will".
- **Compare Isa 1:19**, *"If you are willing and obedient, you shall eat the good of the land"*. Both put the will before a good with an "if".
- **10.7's sentence "Isaiah 1 is the one place in these verses where the will is invited with an 'if'" is now wrong and must be restated.**

**What lies behind refusing.** *"you refuse to come to me that you may have life"* (Joh 5:40). The passage gives what lies behind it:
- *"you do not have the love of God within you"* (5:42)
- *"How can you believe, when you receive glory from one another and do not seek the glory that comes from the only God?"* (5:44)

The will refuses because the desire is set elsewhere, on glory from people. That is in the verses, not read in. They had searched the Scriptures (5:39), so the refusal is not a lack of knowing.

**A will that changes when the cost appears.** In Ruth, the nearer redeemer first says *"I will redeem it"* about the land (Rut 4:4). When he hears that Ruth comes with it: *"I cannot redeem it for myself, lest I impair my own inheritance"* (4:6). It is the same man in the same scene. His will turns when his own inheritance is touched.

Set against him is Boaz: *"if he is not willing to redeem you, then, as the Lord lives, I will redeem you"* (3:13).
- Boaz leaves room for the other man's will, by law and in public at the gate.
- He binds his own will by oath.
- Ruth's choice frames it: *"you have not gone after young men"* (3:10).

**I read** three wills in one story: one bound to self-interest, one bound by oath, and one, Ruth's, given in kindness.

**The will set on godliness, and what it meets.** *"Indeed, all who desire to live a godly life in Christ Jesus will be persecuted"* (2Ti 3:12). Paul has listed his "aim in life" and his sufferings (3:10–11).
- Compare 1Ti 6:9, in 10.7: *"those who desire to be rich fall into temptation, into a snare"*. It is the same verb of wanting.
- Wanting riches leads into a snare. Wanting godliness leads into persecution.
- **I read**: both wills meet trouble. The verses say which trouble, and that *"from them all the Lord rescued me"* (3:11).

**Where:** **10.7 *Willing***:
- Mat 19 under "The will invited", restating the Isaiah 1 sentence.
- Joh 5:40–44 under "Refusing God refuses something good", with what lies behind it.
- Ruth under "A plan meets another person's will".
- 2Ti 3:12 beside "The will turned toward harm".

**Ch 11 §4:** the redeemer's turning will (Rut 4:4–6).

## 11. God's delight and will

**Verses:** Eze 18:23, 32; 33:11; Psa 116:15; 1Sa 2:25; Pro 8:35–36; Joh 5:21; Joh 6:38–40; Joh 21:18–23.

**No pleasure in death, said to two kinds of hearer.**
- In Ezekiel 18 the people accuse: *"The way of the Lord is not just"* (18:25, 29). God answers with his pleasure, *"Have I any pleasure in the death of the wicked … and not rather that he should turn from his way and live?"* (18:23). He ends with *"make yourselves a new heart and a new spirit! Why will you die?"* and *"For I have no pleasure in the death of anyone"* (18:31–32).
- In Ezekiel 33 the people despair: *"Surely our transgressions and our sins are upon us, and we rot away because of them. How then can we live?"* (33:10). God answers on oath: *"As I live … I have no pleasure in the death of the wicked"* (33:11).
- **I read** that God's delight is told to the inner state of the hearer, to accusing and to despairing, with the same words.

**Death precious in his sight.** *"Precious in the sight of the Lord is the death of his saints"* (Psa 116:15). The psalmist had been in *"the snares of death"* (116:3) and was delivered (116:8). He answers *"You have loosed my bonds"* (116:16).
- Compare Ezekiel. God has no pleasure in the death of the wicked, yet the death of his saints is precious.
- The word is not delight. It is "precious, weighty". **I read**: not glad of it, but not careless of it. A saint's death costs something in God's sight.
- Compare Ch 5: Saul says to David, *"my life was precious in your eyes this day"* (1Sa 26:21). It is the same word, once between two men and once in the Lord's sight.

**A tension left standing.** *"But they would not listen to the voice of their father, for it was the will of the Lord to put them to death"* (1Sa 2:25). "Will" is the delight word.
- Set against Eze 18:32, "I have no pleasure in the death of anyone", the verses seem to pull apart.
- The passage gives the setting: sin *"against the Lord"* with no one to intercede (2:25a). It also sets Samuel beside them, growing *"in favor with the Lord and also with man"* (2:26).
- I do not reconcile the two. They stand as Isa 53:10 stands in 13.1.

**Favour found by finding.** *"For whoever finds me finds life and obtains favor from the Lord, but he who fails to find me injures himself; all who hate me love death"* (Pro 8:35–36).
- The figure: Wisdom is at her gates, and the one who finds is *"watching daily at my gates, waiting beside my doors"* (8:34).
- **I read** that favour comes by daily waiting, not by one finding.
- At the end love and hate are turned over. To hate wisdom is to love death, a love the person does not know he has.

**The Son's will.**
- *"so also the Son gives life to whom he will"* (Joh 5:21).
- *"For I have come down from heaven, not to do my own will but the will of him who sent me. And this is the will of him who sent me, that I should lose nothing of all that he has given me"* (6:38–39). The Father's will has content: *"that everyone who looks on the Son and believes in him should have eternal life"* (6:40).
- **13.1 *Willing* says Gethsemane is "the one place in these verses where two wills are named side by side and one is given up to the other". Joh 6:38 names the same, outside Gethsemane, so that sentence must be restated.**

**One will over another's life.**
- To Peter: *"when you were young, you used to dress yourself and walk wherever you wanted, but when you are old, you will stretch out your hands, and another will dress you and carry you where you do not want to go"* (Joh 21:18). The verse uses the want word twice: Peter's own will in youth, and an end he would not choose.
- Peter then looks at the other disciple: *"Lord, what about this man?"* (21:21). The answer: *"If it is my will that he remain until I come, what is that to you? You follow me!"* (21:22).
- **I read**: a man told his own will will be overruled turns to compare his lot with another's, and Christ's will answers both.
- The rumour that followed (21:23) shows the word being misread almost at once.

**Where:**
- **13.1 *Delight*:** a new item, "What he has no pleasure in" (Ezekiel 18, 33), and "precious in his sight" (Psa 116:15).
- **13.1 *Delight*, "He does what pleases him":** 1Sa 2:25, with the tension stated.
- **13.1 *Delight*, "Whom he is pleased with":** Pro 8:35.
- **13.1 *Willing*:** Joh 5:21, Joh 6:38–40 (restating the Gethsemane sentence) and Joh 21:22.
- **10.7 *Willing*:** Joh 21:18, the will overruled.

## 12. Delight in a person, against a father's will

**Verse:** 1Sa 19:1–7. Compared with 1Sa 18:1, 8–9.

**Two people, two inner states.** *"Saul spoke to Jonathan his son and to all his servants, that they should kill David. But Jonathan, Saul's son, delighted much in David"* (19:1). The command and the delight sit in one verse. The writer names him "Saul's son" just as he stands against Saul.

**What the delight does.**
1. It warns: *"Saul my father seeks to kill you"* (19:2).
2. It stands beside the father (19:3).
3. It speaks well: *"Let not the king sin against his servant David"* (19:4).
4. It reminds Saul of his own past joy: *"You saw it, and rejoiced"* (19:5).

Saul listens and swears, *"he shall not be put to death"* (19:6). **I read** delight in a person turning into intercession, carried out at risk inside a family.

**Compare the two words for Jonathan's bond, and Saul's turn.**
- 1Sa 18:1, in 10.10: *"the soul of Jonathan was knit to the soul of David"*. That is the bond.
- 19:1: "delighted much". That is the bond under threat.
- Saul once "rejoiced" at David (19:5). His turn began with a saying that *"displeased him"* and *"Saul eyed David from that day on"* (18:8–9).

**I read** delight and displeasure about one man, in a father and a son. The look (Saul "eyed" David) sits on the displeasure side. The jealousy side goes to OT-95.

**Where:** **10.10 *Loyalty and honour***, after 18:1.

## 13. Creatures' thirst, and man's heart, in one psalm

**Verse:** Psa 104:11 (with 104:10–18, 27–28).

**What is pictured.**
- *"You make springs gush forth in the valleys … they give drink to every beast of the field; the wild donkeys quench their thirst"* (104:10–11).
- The same list moves without a break to man: *"wine to gladden the heart of man, oil to make his face shine and bread to strengthen man's heart"* (104:15).
- Then *"These all look to you, to give them their food in due season"* (104:27).

**Compare.**
- The creature's thirst is met by God without its asking. The verses say the creatures "look to you".
- Man's heart is gladdened and strengthened by the same giving.
- Psa 42:1, in Ch 5, makes the comparison itself: *"As a deer pants for flowing streams, so pants my soul for you, O God"*.
- **I read** the creatures' thirst as the picture Scripture uses for the soul's. In Psalm 104 both are met by one open hand. Psa 145:16, in 10.6, says the same: "you satisfy the desire of every living thing".
- **The difference.** The donkey's thirst is met by water. The psalmist's thirst in Psalm 42 is for God himself.

**Where:**
- **13.4 *Nature*** (its first content): the creatures' thirst met by God, and their looking to him.
- **10.6:** a pointer at Psa 145:16.

Your call, since 13.4 is empty.
