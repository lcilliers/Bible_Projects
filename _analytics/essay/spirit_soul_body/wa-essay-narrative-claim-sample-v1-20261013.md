# Narrative and claims: a worked sample for review (v1, round 2)

**Date:** 2026-10-13 · **Escalation:** #2110 · **Author:** Claude Code · **Status:** for the researcher's review, rewritten in place after the researcher's correction. Read-only: no narrative file was changed.
**Goes with:** `wa-essay-narrative-record-db-specification-v1-20261013.md` (§3).

**The first version of this sample was wrong.** It cut the narrative into "claims" sentence by sentence, as if the narrative were a list. It counted quotations, chapter outlines and arrangement as claims. This version replaces it.

---

## 1. The researcher's correction (chat 2026-10-13, verbatim)

> *"the narrative is the story. It is written to give context, readability, and the story of the section. it needs to read as a whole, across sections and chapters. It is not a listing of claims. Claims are the factual statements in the story that is based or derived from sources (verses in the main, but also will include science). I would imagine that if the narrative make a statement that is derived and not explicitely stated in verses that it would have correlated claims. I can also imagine that a statement may draw from several claims. I can also imagine that a claim may be narrated in different sections (e.g. the heart narration my have a section of a claim while the remainder of the claim is in the feeling section). … by the way - just quoting the verse is not a claim, the interpretation of the verse or verses beyond the mere text of the verse, is the actual claim."*

## 2. What follows from it

- **The narrative** is the story. It is not cut up. It is recorded as passages only so that a claim can point to where it is told.
- **A claim** is a factual statement that goes **beyond the mere text** of its sources: what the verses are taken to mean, what they show together, what a word covers, what a count shows. Its sources are verses, and later science.
- **Not a claim:** a quotation; the story's wording, order and transitions; the book's outline and method; a pointer to another chapter.
- **Many to many:**
  - one claim can be **told in several places**, each carrying part of it (§3, K10 is told in five);
  - one passage can **draw on several claims** (§4, the opening of 10.12 draws on F1 and F2).
- A claim has **its own wording**, as a plain statement, apart from the narrative's wording. The narrative may tell it differently in each place.
- The column **"Beyond the text"** says why each item is a claim and not a quotation. If nothing goes beyond the text, it is not a claim.

## 5. What this shows for the register

- The same two pieces gave **94 "claims"** in the first version and give **21 claims** here.
- Several of the 21 are **told in more than one chapter**. K10 is told in five places, F6 in six, F2 in four. Today nothing records that these places carry one claim. If one is restated, the others are not seen.
- Identifying a claim needs **judgement**: what goes beyond the text, and where else the same claim is told. A script can find candidate places by shared verses (that is how the other places in §3 and §4 were found), but it cannot decide what the claim is. So the import of the narrative's claims needs reading, chapter by chapter. The specification says how, and what it costs.
