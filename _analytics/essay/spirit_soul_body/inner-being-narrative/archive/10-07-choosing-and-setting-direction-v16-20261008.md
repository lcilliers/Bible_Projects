# 10.7 Choosing and setting direction

Scripture does not stop to define choosing. It shows a person taking a direction in two ways. A choice is made between things set before them, and the heart is set, inclined and held to what was chosen. One speech holds both. At Shechem, Joshua says *"choose this day whom you will serve"* (Joshua 24:15). When the people have chosen, he says *"Then put away the foreign gods that are among you, and incline your heart to the Lord"* (24:23). The passage then records how long the choice held (24:31). I start from that speech and set the other verses beside it.

The clearest sentence on choosing is about a child: *"when he knows how to refuse the evil and choose the good"* (Isaiah 7:15). Refusing and choosing are named together as one knowing. The man whom the book of Job calls blameless is described by what he turned from: he *"feared God and turned away from evil"* (Job 1:1).

## The words

The English "choose" stands for a few words, and the verses keep each one's own range.
- ***Bāḥar*, "to choose".** It is the ordinary Hebrew verb. The ESV renders it in more than one way:
  - "choose": *"choose this day"* (Joshua 24:15)
  - "desire": *"all that you desire of me I will do for you"* (2 Samuel 19:38)
  - "to be chosen", in the sense of preferred: *"To get understanding is to be chosen rather than silver"* (Proverbs 16:16)
- ***Bāḥîr*, "chosen one".** It is a noun from the same root, for a person or a people God chose: *"Moses, his chosen one"* (Psalm 106:23); *"Israel my chosen"* (Isaiah 45:4).
- ***Māʾas*, "to refuse, reject, despise".** It is the other side of the same act:
  - *"refuse the evil"* (Isaiah 7:15)
  - *"this people has refused the waters of Shiloah"* (8:6)
  - Job's last word: *"therefore I despise myself, and repent"* (Job 42:6)
- ***Eklegomai*, "to choose".** It is the Greek verb used for the gathering in Jerusalem choosing the seven (Acts 6:5) and the church choosing men to send (15:22, 25).
- ***Authairetos*, "of one's own accord".** One Greek word: Titus goes *"of his own accord"* (2 Corinthians 8:17).

The verbs for setting a direction are words of the heart:
- **inclining:** *"incline your heart to the Lord"* (Joshua 24:23)
- **directing:** *"direct your heart to the Lord"* (1 Samuel 7:3)
- **setting before oneself:** *"I have chosen the way of faithfulness; I set your rules before me"* (Psalm 119:30). In this verse the choosing and the setting are one sentence.

The same choosing verb is used for God. When he chooses, a person is the one chosen, and what that means for the person is set out below, in *Being chosen*. God's own choosing, in his own right, is taken up with the other beings (13.1).

## Two sides

Refusing is itself a choice. The child knows *"how to refuse the evil and choose the good"* (Isaiah 7:15). The oracle around that verse shows refusing going both ways:
- **A refusal dressed in pious words.** Ahaz, offered a sign, says *"I will not ask, and I will not put the Lord to the test"* (7:12).
- **A refusal of what was gentle.** *"this people has refused the waters of Shiloah that flow gently"* (8:6).

Each refusal in that oracle has its own consequence.

Not answering is already choosing. *"when I called, you did not answer; when I spoke, you did not listen, but you did what was evil in my eyes and chose what I did not delight in"* (Isaiah 65:12). The verse sets the not listening and the wrong choice in one sentence.

A choice can be put entirely in the negative: *"Do not envy a man of violence and do not choose any of his ways"* (Proverbs 3:31). And it can be laid out with both its sides and both its ends:
- *"cease to do evil, learn to do good"* (Isaiah 1:16–17)
- *"If you are willing and obedient, you shall eat the good of the land"* (1:19)
- *"but if you refuse and rebel, you shall be eaten by the sword"* (1:20)

## What a choice rests on

In these verses a choice does not come from nowhere. Each verse names something that stands under the choice.

**Knowing how.** The child chooses *"when he knows how"* (Isaiah 7:15). The oracle of Isaiah 1 opens with that knowing missing: *"Israel does not know, my people do not understand"* (1:3). For the person God chooses, the order runs the other way, and knowing is what the choosing is for (*Being chosen*, below).

**Being taught.** The way to choose is taught, and the teaching comes from who the Lord is:
- *"Good and upright is the Lord; therefore he instructs sinners in the way"* (Psalm 25:8)
- *"Who is the man who fears the Lord? Him will he instruct in the way that he should choose"* (25:12)
- The one who chooses asks for it: *"Make me to know your ways, O Lord; teach me your paths"* (25:4); *"graciously teach me your law"* (119:29)
- Teaching and the heart are asked for together: *"Teach me your way, O Lord, that I may walk in your truth; unite my heart to fear your name"* (86:11)

In Psalm 119 the one choosing also asks God to do the refusing with him: *"Put false ways far from me"* (119:29).

**The fear of the Lord.** In Psalm 25 it is the one who fears the Lord whom the Lord instructs (25:12). Joshua puts fear before the choice in the same order: *"fear the Lord and serve him in sincerity and in faithfulness"* (Joshua 24:14), and then *"choose this day"* (24:15). What fear does to a choice is taken up below, in *Fear and choosing*.

**What has been seen and remembered.**
- The people at Shechem choose from memory: *"it is the Lord our God who brought us and our fathers up from the land of Egypt, out of the house of slavery, and who did those great signs in our sight"* (Joshua 24:17).
- Job's turning is made when he sees: *"now my eye sees you"* (Job 42:5).
- The psalmist keeps his eyes on what he chose: *"My eyes are ever toward the Lord"* (Psalm 25:15).

**What is good to the chooser.** Two verses make the chooser's own good the ground, and honour it: *"wherever it suits him"* (Deuteronomy 23:16), and *"whatever seems good to you"* (2 Samuel 19:38). In Hebrew both say "the good" to him, or in his eyes.

## What draws it

What a person wants is named beside what they choose. The account of wanting is in 10.6. Here the verses set it next to the choice.
- **Desire.** *"For they shall be ashamed of the oaks that you desired; and you shall blush for the gardens that you have chosen"* (Isaiah 1:29).
- **The soul's delight.** *"These have chosen their own ways, and their soul delights in their abominations"* (Isaiah 66:3).
- **Envy.** The pull is named before the choice, and the command reaches it: *"Do not envy a man of violence and do not choose any of his ways"* (Proverbs 3:31).
- **Being pleased.** *"what they said pleased the whole gathering, and they chose Stephen"* (Acts 6:5).
- **Earnestness.** Titus, *"being himself very earnest he is going to you of his own accord"* (2 Corinthians 8:17).
- **Preferring.** To choose can be to weigh one good above another: *"How much better to get wisdom than gold! To get understanding is to be chosen rather than silver"* (Proverbs 16:16). Proverbs 3 sets wisdom above all desire: *"nothing you desire can compare with her"* (3:15).

## Whose measure

The verses ask in whose eyes, and by whose delight, a choice is good. They give more than one answer, and the setting decides which.
- **The chooser's own eyes.** *"if it is evil in your eyes to serve the Lord, choose this day"* (Joshua 24:15). Joshua leaves the view to the people, and the turning away from the Lord could begin there.
- **The Lord's eyes.** *"you did what was evil in my eyes"* (Isaiah 65:12).
- **The Lord's delight, not met.** *"chose what I did not delight in"* (65:12); *"chose that in which I did not delight"* (66:4).
- **The Lord's delight, met.** *"who choose the things that please me and hold fast my covenant"* (Isaiah 56:4). "Please" is the same word as "delight" in 65:12 and 66:4.
- **The soul's own delight, set against his.** *"their soul delights in their abominations"* (66:3), again the same word.
- **Other people's eyes, set against his choosing.** When God chooses, the verses often name a second measure held by the people around: *"everyone doing whatever is right in his own eyes"*, set against *"the place that the Lord your God will choose"* (Deuteronomy 12:8, 11); Michal's *"before the eyes of his servants' female servants"* against David's *"before the Lord"* (2 Samuel 6:20–21); a people despised *"in their sight"* (Jeremiah 33:24); *"rejected by men but in the sight of God chosen"* (1 Peter 2:4). See *Others around a choice*, below.

"Good in your eyes" is not always the wrong measure. The king says *"whatever seems good to you"* to the old man who had fed him (2 Samuel 19:38), and there the chooser's own eyes are honoured. The setting changes what the same expression means (see *What changes the character*, below).

## Choosing before God

In these verses a person's choice is not the first move. It is made in front of a God who has already acted.
- At Shechem the choice comes after eleven verses of what the Lord did, beginning *"I took your father Abraham from beyond the River"* (Joshua 24:3).
- In Isaiah 65 the wrong choice is made in front of a God who says *"Here I am, here I am"* and *"I spread out my hands all the day to a rebellious people"* (65:1–2).
- At the council in Jerusalem the church's choosing follows Peter's word that *"God made a choice among you"* (Acts 15:7), in the same verb.

The verses also name God at work in a person's choosing, and set it beside the person's own accord without setting the two against each other:
- *"thanks be to God, who put into the heart of Titus the same earnest care I have for you"*, and *"being himself very earnest he is going to you of his own accord"* (2 Corinthians 8:16–17)
- the Macedonians gave *"of their own accord"*, and *"they gave themselves first to the Lord and then by the will of God to us"* (8:3, 5)
- the church's choice is joined to the Spirit's: *"it has seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to us"* (Acts 15:28)
- the psalmist chooses, and asks God for the room to carry it out: *"I will run in the way of your commandments when you enlarge my heart!"* (Psalm 119:32)

In 2 Corinthians 8, I read the earnest care God put into Titus's heart as becoming Titus's own: the same passage calls it both. The verses name both and do not explain how they fit.

## Being chosen

The verb a person uses to choose is also used when God chooses a person. Then the one who chooses is God. In these verses the inner life of the one chosen is not what made them chosen. It is what the choosing is for, and what follows from it.

**The choosing comes first.** When God chooses, the verses put the inner life of the chosen after the choosing, as its purpose:
- *"my servant whom I have chosen, that you may know and believe me and understand that I am he"* (Isaiah 43:10). The witnesses he chose are *"the people who are blind, yet have eyes, who are deaf, yet have ears"* (43:8).
- *"he chose us in him before the foundation of the world, that we should be holy and blameless before him"* (Ephesians 1:4).
- *"has not God chosen those who are poor in the world to be rich in faith"* (James 2:5).
- *"Blessed is the one you choose and bring near"* (Psalm 65:4) comes straight after *"When iniquities prevail against me, you atone for our transgressions"* (65:3).

One verse names God's choosing and a person's belief together: *"God chose you as the firstfruits to be saved, through sanctification by the Spirit and belief in the truth"* (2 Thessalonians 2:13). It does not say how the two fit.

When people choose a person, the order is the other way. The gathering in Jerusalem was told to *"pick out from among you seven men of good repute, full of the Spirit and of wisdom"* (Acts 6:3), and they chose Stephen, *"a man full of faith and of the Holy Spirit"* (6:5). They chose by what was already seen in him. It was seen in him to the end: *"full of the Holy Spirit"*, he prayed for those who stoned him, *"Lord, do not hold this sin against them"* (7:55, 60).

**What the chosen are moved to.** The verses set an inner response beside being chosen. Twice they name the choosing as its ground.
- **Celebrating.** David, rebuked by Michal for dancing before the ark: *"It was before the Lord, who chose me above your father and above all his house … and I will celebrate before the Lord"* (2 Samuel 6:21). Her measure was the eyes of the servants (6:20). His was the Lord. Being chosen had not kept him from being angry, afraid and unwilling when Uzzah died (6:8–10).
- **Not fearing.** *"Fear not, O Jacob my servant, Jeshurun whom I have chosen"* (Isaiah 44:2). The ground is who chose him: *"the Lord who made you, who formed you from the womb and will help you"*.
- **Satisfaction.** *"We shall be satisfied with the goodness of your house, the holiness of your temple!"* (Psalm 65:4).
- **Joy and singing.** *"So he brought his people out with joy, his chosen ones with singing"* (Psalm 105:43).
- **Gladness that another asks to share.** *"that I may rejoice in the gladness of your nation"* (Psalm 106:5). He asks it while confessing *"Both we and our fathers have sinned"* (106:6).

**What the chosen are called to.** Being chosen is given as the ground of commands:
- **Be strong and do it.** *"the Lord has chosen you to build a house for the sanctuary; be strong and do it"* (1 Chronicles 28:10), with *"serve him with a whole heart and with a willing mind, for the Lord searches all hearts"* (28:9).
- **Put on.** *"Put on then, as God's chosen ones, holy and beloved, compassionate hearts, kindness, humility, meekness, and patience"* (Colossians 3:12). What is put on is turned toward others: *"as the Lord has forgiven you, so you also must forgive"* (3:13); *"above all these put on love"* (3:14).
- **Stand firm.** *"stand firm and hold to the traditions that you were taught by us"* (2 Thessalonians 2:15).
- **Confirm it.** *"be all the more diligent to confirm your calling and election, for if you practice these qualities you will never fall"* (2 Peter 1:10). The qualities are added one to another, from faith to love (1:5–7). The effort follows what was given first: *"His divine power has granted to us all things that pertain to life and godliness"* (1:3).
- **Remember.** *"Remember the wondrous works that he has done … children of Jacob, his chosen ones!"* (Psalm 105:5–6); *"Remember these things, O Jacob … you will not be forgotten by me"* (Isaiah 44:21).

**Standing for others.** The chosen can stand between God and people who have turned from him. After the calf, *"he said he would destroy them—had not Moses, his chosen one, stood in the breach before him, to turn away his wrath from destroying them"* (Psalm 106:23). The psalm calls Moses chosen at the very point where he stands for people who *"forgot God, their Savior"* (106:21). It also says what those people did to him. They *"were jealous of Moses"* (106:16), and later *"they made his spirit bitter, and he spoke rashly with his lips"* (106:33). Standing for them did not keep him from being embittered by them. The anger he turned away is taken up in 10.13.

**Faith, and the truth.** Several verses name faith, and knowing the truth, for the chosen:
- *"the faith of God's elect and their knowledge of the truth, which accords with godliness"* (Titus 1:1)
- *"rich in faith"* (James 2:5)
- *"belief in the truth"* (2 Thessalonians 2:13)

For people already chosen, Paul prays for *"the eyes of your hearts enlightened, that you may know what is the hope to which he has called you"* (Ephesians 1:18). Faith is shown in what it does: *"faith by itself, if it does not have works, is dead"* (James 2:17).

**Being chosen is not a guarantee.** The verses say plainly that the chosen can fail:
- **A condition.** *"if you forsake him, he will cast you off forever"* (1 Chronicles 28:9).
- **Forgetting and despising.** The chosen people *"soon forgot his works"* and *"despised the pleasant land, having no faith in his promise"* (Psalm 106:13, 24). "Despised" is the refusing verb of Isaiah 7:15.
- **Not calling.** *"Yet you did not call upon me, O Jacob; but you have been weary of me, O Israel!"* (Isaiah 43:22).
- **Blindness.** Whoever lacks the qualities is *"so nearsighted that he is blind, having forgotten that he was cleansed from his former sins"* (2 Peter 1:9).

The way back is the Lord's remembering: *"For their sake he remembered his covenant"* (Psalm 106:45); *"I, I am he who blots out your transgressions for my own sake, and I will not remember your sins"* (Isaiah 43:25).

**Set against others.** In each passage the chosen stand beside others who choose otherwise, or cannot see:
- the idol-maker, whose heart is shut *"so that they cannot understand"* (Isaiah 44:18)
- those who *"refused to love the truth"* and *"had pleasure in unrighteousness"* (2 Thessalonians 2:10, 12)
- those who *"profess to know God, but they deny him by their works"* (Titus 1:16)
- an assembly that seats the rich and sends the poor to stand, having *"become judges with evil thoughts"* (James 2:4). God chose the poor: *"But you have dishonored the poor man"* (2:6).
- kings who *"are of one mind"* with the beast, against *"those with him"*, who *"are called and chosen and faithful"* (Revelation 17:13–14)

## Others around a choice

A choice does not stay between the one who chooses and the one chosen. Others stand near it: at the place God chose, turned toward it from far away, set against the person chosen, standing with them, or knowing that a choice was made. The verses seldom say what these others feel about the choosing itself. They say what they are to do and be around it, and what moves them.

**At the place God chose.** When God chooses a place, the people are not told to admire it. They are told what to be there. They are to rejoice: *"you shall rejoice before the Lord your God in all that you undertake"* (Deuteronomy 12:18). The joy rests on the blessing, not on the place: *"in which the Lord your God has blessed you"* (12:7). At the Feast of Booths the joy is what the blessing is for: *"because the Lord your God will bless you … so that you will be altogether joyful"* (16:15). The joy is never had alone. The servants, the Levite, *"the sojourner, the fatherless, and the widow"* are named in it (16:11), and the one rejoicing is told in the next breath, *"You shall remember that you were a slave in Egypt"* (16:12).

Fear is learned there too, at a meal: *"that you may learn to fear the Lord your God always"* (14:23). In the same passage the people spend their money *"for whatever you desire … whatever your appetite craves"*, and *"eat there before the Lord your God and rejoice"* (14:26). Fear of the Lord, a satisfied appetite and joy belong to the one meal, and the passage does not set them against each other. When a case is *"too difficult for you"*, the decision is sought at the place, and what is asked is *"you shall be careful to do according to all that they direct you"* (17:8, 10). Presumption is the opposite, and its end is that *"all the people shall hear and fear and not act presumptuously again"* (17:13). I read this as a person's own judgement reaching its limit and being asked to yield. The passage names the limit and the carefulness, not the struggle.

**Desire, near and far.** The soul's desire comes in twice, turned two ways. Away from the place it is given room. If the place is too far, *"you may eat within your towns whenever you desire"* (Deuteronomy 12:21). In the same words, desire draws a man to the place: a Levite *"may come when he desires"*, literally with all the desire of his soul, *"to the place that the Lord will choose"* (18:6). His coming is left to that desire. It is the account of wanting (10.6) seen from beside a choice.

**Turned toward it from far off.** For people far from the chosen city, the city is the direction of their prayer. Solomon's prayer takes the hardest case: the people in captivity because they sinned, *"for there is no one who does not sin"* (1 Kings 8:46). The turning happens in the enemy's land, in steps. First *"if they turn their heart"*, then words, *"We have sinned and have acted perversely and wickedly"* (8:47), then *"if they repent with all their heart and with all their soul … and pray to you toward … the city that you have chosen"* (8:48). "Turn" and "repent" are the one verb. Solomon prays it to the one who alone knows hearts (8:39), and he asks God himself to *"incline our hearts to him"* (8:58). Nehemiah prays from exile in the same verb, holding God to his word: *"if you return to me and keep my commandments and do them, … I will gather them and bring them to the place that I have chosen"* (Nehemiah 1:9). The return is to God, and the place is where he brings those who return.

**Set against the one chosen.** Several passages show others turning on someone chosen, and each names what moves them.
- **Grumbling, and seeking more.** When God's choice of Aaron was challenged, Moses named what lay under it: *"would you seek the priesthood also? … What is Aaron that you grumble against him?"* (Numbers 16:10–11). Psalm 106 calls it jealousy (106:16). God counts the grumbling as against himself, and makes his choice plain *"to cease from me the grumblings"* (Numbers 17:5). Once it was plain, the grumbling turned to terror: *"Are we all to perish?"* (17:13).
- **Anger at what a choice costs.** Saul's anger is kindled against Jonathan: *"do I not know that you have chosen the son of Jesse to your own shame"* (1 Samuel 20:30). He gives his reason: *"neither you nor your kingdom shall be established"* (20:31). The anger is for his house, and it ends in a spear thrown at his own son (20:33). Jonathan chose in love, *"for he loved him as he loved his own soul"* (20:17). His anger in reply is grief for the one he chose: *"he was grieved for David, because his father had disgraced him"* (20:34). He keeps the choice, and seals it with an oath in the name of the Lord (20:42).
- **Contempt in the heart.** Michal *"saw King David leaping and dancing before the Lord, and she despised him in her heart"* (2 Samuel 6:16). It was inward before it was spoken, and when it came out its measure was the servants' eyes (6:20).
- **Despised and abhorred.** The servant is addressed as *"one deeply despised, abhorred by the nation"* (Isaiah 49:7), in the same verb as Michal's. The same verse turns it round, not by anything the servant does: *"Kings shall see and arise … because of the Lord, who is faithful, … who has chosen you"*.
- **A verdict drawn from ruin.** People looked at the chosen in their ruin and said *"The Lord has rejected the two clans that he chose"*, and so *"despised my people so that they are no longer a nation in their sight"* (Jeremiah 33:24).
- **Hatred.** *"because you are not of the world, but I chose you out of the world, therefore the world hates you"* (John 15:19). The world loves *"its own"*. The verse names the choosing as the cause. The passage says the hatred fell first on Jesus (15:18), and puts under it *"because they do not know him who sent me"* (15:21).
- **Rejection after testing.** *"a living stone rejected by men but in the sight of God chosen and precious"* (1 Peter 2:4).

**With the chosen, and for them.** Others stand with the chosen too. The psalmist asks to be let in: *"Remember me, O Lord, … that I may rejoice in the gladness of your nation"* (Psalm 106:4–5). He asks God, not the chosen, and he asks it confessing his sin (106:6). Paul writes in chains: *"I endure everything for the sake of the elect, that they also may obtain the salvation"* (2 Timothy 2:10). The elder loves the elect lady *"in truth"*, *"because of the truth that abides in us"* (2 John 1–2): the love rests on the truth, not on her being chosen. And whoever believes in the stone God chose *"will not be put to shame"* (1 Peter 2:6). For those who do not believe, the same stone is *"a stone of stumbling"* (2:8).

**Knowing that a choice was made, and not knowing.** Knowing of a choice can settle a quarrel. In the dispute at Jerusalem, Peter appeals to what they all know: *"you know that in the early days God made a choice among you, that by my mouth the Gentiles should hear the word of the gospel and believe"* (Acts 15:7). The purpose of God's choosing one man lay in others, that they would hear and believe, and their hearts were *"cleansed … by faith"* (15:9). The debate falls silent (15:12). Paul knows that the Thessalonians are chosen from what he saw happen: *"because our gospel came to you not only in word, but also in power and in the Holy Spirit and with full conviction"* (1 Thessalonians 1:4–5).

Not knowing does not stop God from using a man for the sake of the chosen. To Cyrus: *"For the sake of my servant Jacob, and Israel my chosen, I call you by your name, I name you, though you do not know me"* (Isaiah 45:4). Knowing is still held out to him: *"that you may know that it is I"* (45:3). Of those outside the chosen, Paul says *"Israel failed to obtain what it was seeking. The elect obtained it, but the rest were hardened"* (Romans 11:7). They were seeking, and did not see. He refuses at once to make it the end: *"did they stumble in order that they might fall? By no means!"* (11:11).

**An inner act weighed by what God chooses.** In one passage what the others offer from within is set against God's own choosing. The people fast and complain, *"Why have we humbled ourselves, and you take no knowledge of it?"*, while *"in the day of your fast you seek your own pleasure, and oppress all your workers"* (Isaiah 58:3). God answers, *"Is such the fast that I choose, a day for a person to humble himself?"* (58:5). What he chooses is turned outward: *"to let the oppressed go free"*, *"to share your bread with the hungry"*, *"not to hide yourself from your own flesh"* (58:6–7). The humbling of the soul was not wrong in itself. It stood beside oppression in the same people, and it was not what he chooses. The same people *"delight to know my ways"* (58:2), in the same delight word as Isaiah 66:4 in *Whose measure*.

## Choosing together

A people can choose as one.
- Israel at Shechem: *"we will serve the Lord"*, and they become *"witnesses against yourselves"* (Joshua 24:21–22).
- The gathering in Jerusalem, out of being pleased (Acts 6:5).
- The church, *"having come to one accord"* (Acts 15:25).

Weighing a case together is also called choosing: *"Let us choose what is right; let us know among ourselves what is good"* (Job 34:4).

## A choice that is honoured

Choosing is a freedom a person can lose, and the law gives it back. The escaped slave *"shall dwell with you, in your midst, in the place that he shall choose within one of your towns, wherever it suits him. You shall not wrong him"* (Deuteronomy 23:16).

The king hands the choice to the man who fed him in the wilderness: *"all that you desire of me I will do for you"* (2 Samuel 19:38). Barzillai had already weighed his own age: *"Can I discern what is pleasant and what is not?"* (19:35). His choice is still honoured.

## Choosing from weakness

The one who chooses in these verses is often low:
- **sorrow:** *"My soul melts away for sorrow"* (Psalm 119:28)
- **guilt and trouble:** *"pardon my guilt, for it is great"*; *"I am lonely and afflicted"* (Psalm 25:11, 16)
- **one's own verdict on oneself:** *"Behold, I am a dry tree"* (Isaiah 56:3). The Lord answers that verdict: *"I will give them an everlasting name that shall not be cut off"* (56:5).
- **age:** *"Can I discern what is pleasant and what is not?"* (2 Samuel 19:35)
- **affliction, as Elihu reads it:** *"this you have chosen rather than affliction"* (Job 36:21)

## Fear and choosing

Fear is one of the things that stands under a choice (above, and 10.12), and it also acts on a choice already made.

**Fear can stop a choice.** The order came from outside, and fear held the hand back:
- The young man was told by his father to kill the captive kings, *"But the young man did not draw his sword, for he was afraid, because he was still a young man"* (Judges 8:20).
- Saul ordered his armor-bearer to kill him, *"But his armor-bearer would not, for he feared greatly"* (1 Samuel 31:4).

Fear can also hold a whole army to its word. The soldiers were faint with hunger and found honey, *"but no one put his hand to his mouth, for the people feared the oath"* (1 Samuel 14:26).

**Fear can set a direction as well as stop one.**
- Uriah *"was afraid and fled and escaped to Egypt"* (Jeremiah 26:21).
- When the hail was announced, *"whoever feared the word of the Lord among the servants of Pharaoh hurried his slaves and his livestock into the houses, but whoever did not pay attention to the word of the Lord left his slaves and his livestock in the field"* (Exodus 9:20–21). The fear of God's word is what made the difference in what people did.
- Proverbs turns the same word into a quality of the wise: *"One who is wise is cautious and turns away from evil, but a fool is reckless and careless"* (Proverbs 14:16). "Cautious" renders the fear word.

**When God speaks to fear before an act,** he does not only take the fear away. He sends the person forward, and he gives the reason:
- *"Be strong and courageous. Do not fear or be in dread of them, for it is the Lord your God who goes with you"* (Deuteronomy 31:6)
- *"Fear not, stand firm, and see the salvation of the Lord"* (Exodus 14:13)
- *"Be careful, be quiet, do not fear, and do not let your heart be faint"* (Isaiah 7:4)

The commands that come with "fear not" are commands to stand, to go on and to be still. The reason given is that God goes with them.

**The fear of the Lord is set before those who must decide.** Jehoshaphat told the judges: *"let the fear of the Lord be upon you. Be careful what you do, for there is no injustice with the Lord our God, or partiality or taking bribes"* (2 Chronicles 19:7). And when *"the dread of the Lord fell upon the people"*, *"they came out as one man"* (1 Samuel 11:7).

## Setting the heart

The choice at Shechem is spoken with the mouth, and Joshua does not take it as the whole. He calls at once for the heart: *"Then put away the foreign gods that are among you, and incline your heart to the Lord"* (Joshua 24:23). The people had just chosen the Lord, and the foreign gods were still among them. Samuel says it in nearly the same words: *"put away the foreign gods and the Ashtaroth from among you and direct your heart to the Lord and serve him only"* (1 Samuel 7:3). In both, putting something away goes with turning the heart.

Scripture speaks of the heart being set, inclined and directed, and of the state it is in:
- **Inclined, and asked for.** *"Incline my heart to your testimonies, and not to selfish gain!"* (Psalm 119:36). The one who prays this has just chosen (119:30), and asks God to do the inclining.
- **Whole or divided.** *"unite my heart to fear your name"* (Psalm 86:11), against *"a double heart"* (Psalm 12:2). Solomon's *"heart was not wholly true to the Lord his God"* (1 Kings 11:4).
- **Stubborn.** *"the stubbornness of their evil hearts"* runs through Jeremiah (Jeremiah 7:24).
- **Moved from within.** *"everyone whose heart stirred him, and everyone whose spirit moved him"* (Exodus 35:21) puts heart and spirit side by side in setting out to give.

## Holding a choice

A choice spoken is not yet a choice carried out, and the verses say so in several ways.
- **It binds the one who made it.** *"You are witnesses against yourselves that you have chosen the Lord"* (Joshua 24:22).
- **Choosing is not the same as being able.** *"You are not able to serve the Lord, for he is a holy God"* (Joshua 24:19). Joshua says this to people who have just chosen, and they answer *"No, but we will serve the Lord"* (24:21).
- **It is held by keeping it in view, and by clinging.** *"I have chosen the way of faithfulness; I set your rules before me. I cling to your testimonies, O Lord"* (Psalm 119:30–31). The verb for "cling" is the one used a few verses earlier: *"My soul clings to the dust"* (119:25). The one verb is used of the soul clinging to the dust and of the one who chose clinging to God's testimonies.
- **It is held by keeping and holding fast.** *"who keep my Sabbaths, who choose the things that please me and hold fast my covenant"* (Isaiah 56:4). The choice stands between a keeping and a holding on.
- **It is held while waiting.** *"May integrity and uprightness preserve me, for I wait for you"* (Psalm 25:21). The psalm ends still waiting and in trouble. The right choice has not ended the trouble.
- **Readiness has to be completed.** *"so that your readiness in desiring it may be matched by your completing it"* (2 Corinthians 8:11).
- **Those God has chosen are told to make it firm.** *"be all the more diligent to confirm your calling and election"* (2 Peter 1:10). See *Being chosen*.
- **It is held as a purpose set ahead.** *"I have purposed that my mouth will not transgress"* (Psalm 17:3). See *Planning*.

The choice at Shechem held for a time: *"Israel served the Lord all the days of Joshua, and all the days of the elders who outlived Joshua and had known all the work that the Lord did for Israel"* (Joshua 24:31). The verse ties those years to people who had known the work the choice was made from. It gives the timing; it does not give the cause.

## Planning

A choice settles a direction. A plan carries it ahead, into a time that has not yet come. Scripture gives the inner being this power plainly. Of the builders at Babel God says, *"this is only the beginning of what they will do. And nothing that they propose to do will now be impossible for them"* (Genesis 11:6). The words for it are the words for thinking: to devise, to reckon, to purpose (10.4). They are used for a woman buying a field, a king counting his soldiers, a sailor looking for a beach, and a man plotting on his bed. This section is about the looking ahead itself. Plans made against another person are in 10.10. The next section, *Willing*, is about how a person answers what others put forward. The two often appear in the same passage.

The place of it is the heart: *"The heart of man plans his way, but the Lord establishes his steps"* (Proverbs 16:9). In that one verse the plan is the person's own, and the steps are not. I read the rest of what the verses say about planning inside that verse.

**Weighing first.** Jesus asks his hearers to look ahead before they begin. A builder who lays a foundation and cannot finish is mocked: *"This man began to build and was not able to finish"* (Luke 14:30). *"Or what king, going out to encounter another king in war, will not sit down first and deliberate whether he is able with ten thousand to meet him who comes against him with twenty thousand?"* (14:31). If the answer is no, he sends for terms *"while the other is yet a great way off"* (14:32). The looking ahead here is honest about strength, and it acts early, while there is still time. Jesus uses it for following him: *"So therefore, any one of you who does not renounce all that he has cannot be my disciple"* (14:33).

The same weighing is praised in an ordinary life. The woman of Proverbs 31 *"rises while it is yet night"* (31:15). *"She considers a field and buys it; with the fruit of her hands she plants a vineyard"* (31:16). The verb for considering is a planning verb. She weighs it, and then she acts.

When something has happened that no one has seen before, the first call is to weigh it before acting. After the outrage at Gibeah, *"all who saw it said, 'Such a thing has never happened or been seen … consider it, take counsel, and speak'"* (Judges 19:30).

**Planning without knowing.** The fullest picture of planning ahead in these verses is a voyage. Paul warned the crew: *"I perceive that the voyage will be with injury and much loss"* (Acts 27:10). They listened to the pilot and the owner instead (27:11). The plan that followed is worded with all its uncertainty:
- *"the majority decided to put out to sea from there, on the chance that somehow they could reach Phoenix"* (27:12)
- *"Now when the south wind blew gently, supposing that they had obtained their purpose, they weighed anchor"* (27:13)
- after the storm, *"they noticed a bay with a beach, on which they planned if possible to run the ship ashore"* (27:39)

*"on the chance that somehow"*, *"supposing"*, *"if possible"*: Luke records the planners' own sense that they could not see far. The first plan was undone by the wind (27:14). The last was undone by a reef they had not seen (27:41).

Paul plans the same way, and says so. *"I have often intended to come to you (but thus far have been prevented)"* (Romans 1:13). His intending was real and often repeated, and something outside him held it back. James says what such planning should sound like: *"If the Lord wills, we will live and do this or that"* (James 4:15; see *Life and death set before a person*).

**A plan carries a person's word.** When Paul changed his travel plans, he was charged with being unreliable, and he answers with the inner question: *"Was I vacillating when I wanted to do this? Do I make my plans according to the flesh, ready to say 'Yes, yes' and 'No, no' at the same time?"* (2 Corinthians 1:17). He rests his answer on God: *"As surely as God is faithful, our word to you has not been Yes and No"* (1:18). In this passage, a plan is something said to others. When it changes, the change can be read as a divided heart.

**Planning together, and parting.** Plans are often made with others, and others may not agree.
- Paul and Barnabas planned to revisit the churches. *"Barnabas wanted to take with them John called Mark. But Paul thought best not to take with them one who had withdrawn from them"* (Acts 15:37–38). Each weighed the same past differently, and *"there arose a sharp disagreement, so that they separated from each other"* (15:39).
- Joseph of Arimathea sat in the council that sent Jesus to death, but he *"had not consented to their decision and action"* (Luke 23:51). He did not share their plan, and the next verse shows him acting on his own: *"This man went to Pilate and asked for the body of Jesus"* (23:52).

**Planning for another's good.** Darius *"planned to set him over the whole kingdom"* (Daniel 6:3), and the verse gives the reason: *"because an excellent spirit was in him"*. The plan for Daniel at once stirred others to look for a fault in him (6:4).

A plan can also be held against someone's good without anyone calling it a plot. The wise woman of Tekoa told David that by leaving Absalom in exile he had *"planned such a thing against the people of God"* (2 Samuel 14:13). Set beside it is God, who *"devises means so that the banished one will not remain an outcast"* (14:14). The king's planning is measured against God's (13.1, *Purposing*).

**A purpose held.** Some plans are aimed at one's own life. *"You have tried my heart, you have visited me by night, you have tested me, and you will find nothing; I have purposed that my mouth will not transgress"* (Psalm 17:3). The psalmist has set a course for his own speech ahead of time, and he states it under God's testing. He goes on: *"My steps have held fast to your paths"* (17:5).
- Barnabas, at Antioch, *"exhorted them all to remain faithful to the Lord with steadfast purpose"* (Acts 11:23). The verse says what moved him: he *"saw the grace of God"* and *"was glad"*.
- Paul lists his *"aim in life"* among what Timothy had followed, beside *"my faith, my patience, my love, my steadfastness"* (2 Timothy 3:10).
- David prays that God will keep what the people have set: *"keep forever such purposes and thoughts in the hearts of your people, and direct their hearts toward you"* (1 Chronicles 29:18). He asks God to hold the purpose in place.
- Paul uses a purpose word for where the mind is set: *"to set the mind on the flesh is death, but to set the mind on the Spirit is life and peace"* (Romans 8:6). *"For the mind that is set on the flesh is hostile to God"* (8:7). Chapter 7 gives the flesh.
- *"You keep him in perfect peace whose mind is stayed on you, because he trusts in you"* (Isaiah 26:3).

**Devised from one's own heart.** A plan can begin in the self and stay there. Jeroboam set up his own feast *"in the month that he had devised from his own heart"* (1 Kings 12:33). The month was his own invention, set against the feast in Judah (12:32). Nehemiah said to the plotters, *"No such things as you say have been done, for you are inventing them out of your own mind"* (Nehemiah 6:8). The same capacity that looks ahead can make up what is not there (10.4, *Imagination that makes gods*).

**The plan and its outcome.** The verses do not let a plan secure its own end.
- *"The heart of man plans his way, but the Lord establishes his steps"* (Proverbs 16:9).
- *"Take counsel together, but it will come to nothing; speak a word, but it will not stand, for God is with us"* (Isaiah 8:10).
- Gamaliel, of the apostles: *"if this plan or this undertaking is of man, it will fail; but if it is of God, you will not be able to overthrow them"* (Acts 5:38–39).
- Of those who plot against the king: *"Though they plan evil against you, though they devise mischief, they will not succeed"* (Psalm 21:11). Of a king's plans against strongholds: *"but only for a time"* (Daniel 11:24).
- Joseph, of his brothers: *"you meant evil against me, but God meant it for good"* (Genesis 50:20). The same verb is used of both.

The verses also show that what a person intends is seen before it is done and will be shown in full. *"The Lord searches all hearts and understands every plan and thought"* (1 Chronicles 28:9). The Lord *"will bring to light the things now hidden in darkness and will disclose the purposes of the heart"* (1 Corinthians 4:5). Paul draws a warning from it for himself: *"do not pronounce judgment before the time"* (4:5). He has just said, *"I do not even judge myself"* (4:3).

## Willing

Planning reaches ahead from the person. The will, in these verses, mostly answers what comes to the person. In the Hebrew narratives the word for being willing, *ʾāḇâ*, almost always stands just after someone has asked, urged, commanded, enticed, pleaded, warned or offered. And it is almost always a "no": *"would not"*, *"was not willing"*, *"would have none"*. In the Greek, the words for wanting and intending (*boulomai*, *boulēma*) belong to the same family as the words for planning and counsel. One of them, *boulēma*, is translated "plan" in one verse (Acts 27:43) and "will" in another (Romans 9:19). So the verses keep the will and the plan close, without making them one act.

**The will meets something put before it.** What is put forward varies:
- a request: *"Please let us pass through your land," but the king of Edom would not listen* (Judges 11:17)
- pressing, day after day, until *"the man would not spend the night"* (Judges 19:7–10)
- a command: Saul's guard *"would not put out their hand to strike the priests of the Lord"* (1 Samuel 22:17)
- an enticement, even from the nearest: *"your friend who is as your own soul entices you secretly … you shall not yield to him"* (Deuteronomy 13:6–8); *"My son, if sinners entice you, do not consent"* (Proverbs 1:10)
- a demand, answered with a line drawn: *"All that you first demanded of your servant I will do, but this thing I cannot do"* (1 Kings 20:9), after the elders said *"Do not listen or consent"* (20:8)
- a plea, and the stronger one shutting it out: Tamar said *"No, my brother, do not violate me"*, *"But he would not listen to her, and being stronger than she, he violated her"* (2 Samuel 13:12, 14)
- a warning: Abner told Asahel twice to turn aside, *"But he refused to turn aside"*, and he died (2 Samuel 2:21–23)

**The "no" can keep faith, and it can do violence.** The verb does not tell you which; the passage does, and it gives the reason every time.
- The king's servants would not kill the priests, and Doeg did (1 Samuel 22:17–18).
- David would not *"put out my hand against the Lord's anointed"*. He prayed that, as Saul's life had been precious in his sight, his own would be precious in the Lord's (26:23–24).
- The water from Bethlehem shows it most fully. David *"said longingly, 'Oh, that someone would give me water to drink'"* (2 Samuel 23:15). Three men broke through the Philistine camp to bring it. *"But he would not drink of it. He poured it out to the Lord and said, 'Far be it from me, O Lord, that I should do this. Shall I drink the blood of the men who went at the risk of their lives?'"* (23:16–17). His own longing was met, and he refused it for their sake, aloud and before the Lord (10.6 follows wanting).
- Against that stands Amnon's "would not" (2 Samuel 13:14), and the men of Gibeah who *"would not listen"* (Judges 19:25).

The same verb is used for a refusal that honours other people and for a refusal that tramples them.

**A refusal can be selective.** Saul and the people *"spared Agag and the best of the sheep … and would not utterly destroy them. All that was despised and worthless they devoted to destruction"* (1 Samuel 15:9). They refused only where they valued what they kept. God's word on it was *"he has turned back from following me"*, and *"Samuel was angry, and he cried to the Lord all night"* (15:11).

**Feeling holds the will.**
- Fear stops it. The armor-bearer *"would not, for he feared greatly"* (1 Samuel 31:4). David *"was afraid of the Lord that day … So David was not willing to take the ark"* (2 Samuel 6:9–10). Three months later *"it was told King David, 'The Lord has blessed the household of Obed-edom'"*, and he *"went and brought up the ark of God … with rejoicing"* (6:12). What he heard moved his will.
- Melted hearts stop it: *"Yet you would not go up"* (Deuteronomy 1:26), and *"Our brothers have made our hearts melt"* (1:28).
- Grief holds it. David, fasting for the child, *"would not"* rise or eat (2 Samuel 12:17).
- Jealousy fixes it: *"He will accept no compensation; he will refuse though you multiply gifts"* (Proverbs 6:35).

10.12 and 10.1 take these feelings up.

**Refusing God refuses something good.** The passages say what was offered:
- the land: *"It is a good land"* (Deuteronomy 1:25), and *"Yet you would not go up"* (1:26)
- rest: *"This is rest; give rest to the weary; and this is repose"; yet they would not hear* (Isaiah 28:12)
- being filled: *"Open your mouth wide, and I will fill it." But … Israel would not submit to me* (Psalm 81:10–11)
- his Spirit and his words: *"If you turn at my reproof, behold, I will pour out my spirit to you"* (Proverbs 1:23)
- cleansing, after reasoning: *"Come now, let us reason together … though your sins are like scarlet, they shall be as white as snow … If you are willing and obedient, you shall eat the good of the land; but if you refuse and rebel, you shall be eaten by the sword"* (Isaiah 1:18–20)

Isaiah 1 is the one place in these verses where the will is invited with an "if". Both ways are set before it, as life and death are in the next section.

**Where willing and planning are named together.** In three passages the refusal of God stands with the person's own counsel, devices or plan:
- *"Because they hated knowledge and did not choose the fear of the Lord, would have none of my counsel and despised all my reproof, therefore they shall eat the fruit of their way, and have their fill of their own devices"* (Proverbs 1:29–31). Not choosing, not willing, and one's own devices stand in one sentence.
- *"Israel would not submit to me. So I gave them over to their stubborn hearts, to follow their own counsels"* (Psalm 81:11–12).
- Isaiah 30 opens with *"stubborn children … who carry out a plan, but not mine"* (30:1). It ends: *"In returning and rest you shall be saved; in quietness and in trust shall be your strength." But you were unwilling, and you said, "No! We will flee upon horses"* (30:15–16). The refusal and their own plan come out of their mouth together.

I read these three as Scripture's own picture of the acts working together when they go wrong: the "no" to God, and a plan of one's own in its place. The verses say what follows: being given over, and being filled with one's own devices. They do not lay out a sequence that every person goes through.

The same pairing is shown going right. David gives Solomon the plan for the temple, *"all the work to be done according to the plan"* (1 Chronicles 28:19), and then says, *"with you in all the work will be every willing man who has skill"* (28:21). A plan comes from God, and the willing carry it out.

**A plan meets another person's will.**
- It can allow for it. Abraham's servant looks ahead: *"Perhaps the woman may not be willing to follow me"* (Genesis 24:5). Abraham answers with what God will do, *"he will send his angel before you"*, and with a way out, *"if the woman is not willing … you will be free from this oath"* (24:7–8).
- It can press it until it gives way. Absalom invited the king, who *"would not go"*. Then *"Absalom pressed him until he let Amnon and all the king's sons go with him"* (2 Samuel 13:25, 27). The plan ended in Amnon's death (13:28). When Joab *"would not come"* twice, Absalom set his field on fire (14:29–30).
- It can bend to a crowd. Pilate had said, *"I find no guilt in him"* (John 18:38). Then, *"wishing to satisfy the crowd"*, he *"released for them Barabbas"* (Mark 15:15).
- It can stand against a plan. The centurion, *"wishing to save Paul, kept them from carrying out their plan"* (Acts 27:43).
- Others can hold it back or help it on. *"when Paul wished to go in among the crowd, the disciples would not let him"* (Acts 19:30). *"when he wished to cross to Achaia, the brothers encouraged him"* (18:27).

**A resolve is still being weighed.** Joseph, *"being a just man and unwilling to put her to shame, resolved to divorce her quietly. But as he considered these things, behold, an angel of the Lord appeared to him in a dream"* (Matthew 1:19–20). He was unwilling to shame her, then he resolved, then he went on considering, and God spoke into the considering. Herod's intending went the other way. He was *"intending after the Passover to bring him out to the people. So Peter was kept in prison, but earnest prayer for him was made to God by the church"* (Acts 12:4–5). The intention was not carried out.

**The will steers.** *"Look at the ships also: though they are so large and are driven by strong winds, they are guided by a very small rudder wherever the will of the pilot directs"* (James 3:4). James uses the picture for the tongue (3:5; 10.5). In the picture, the will is what sets a whole ship's course.

**The will turned toward harm.** *"those who desire to be rich fall into temptation, into a snare"* (1 Timothy 6:9). Against this Paul sets *"godliness with contentment is great gain"* (6:6). *"whoever wishes to be a friend of the world makes himself an enemy of God"* (James 4:4), said after *"your passions are at war within you"* (4:1).

**The willing heart.** The will can also be given freely, and the verses say where that comes from.
- At the tabernacle, *"they came, everyone whose heart stirred him, and everyone whose spirit moved him"* (Exodus 35:21; see *Setting the heart*). *"All who were of a willing heart brought"* (35:22).
- After consecration, *"all who were of a willing heart brought burnt offerings"* (2 Chronicles 29:31).
- It is also asked of God: *"Create in me a clean heart, O God, and renew a right spirit within me … uphold me with a willing spirit"* (Psalm 51:10, 12).

So the willing heart is said both to rise in the person and to be upheld by God. Paul guards the freedom of another's will. He would have liked to keep Onesimus, *"but I preferred to do nothing without your consent in order that your goodness might not be by compulsion but of your own accord"* (Philemon 13–14).

**The will made absolute.** Of Nebuchadnezzar: *"Whom he would, he killed, and whom he would, he kept alive; whom he would, he raised up, and whom he would, he humbled"* (Daniel 5:19). The next verse says what happened inside him: *"But when his heart was lifted up and his spirit was hardened so that he dealt proudly, he was brought down"* (5:20). He had been offered counsel (4:27). He was driven out *"until he knew that the Most High God rules the kingdom of mankind and sets over it whom he will"* (5:21). His reason returned when he lifted his eyes to heaven (4:34). His son knew all this, and Daniel says to him, *"you his son, Belshazzar, have not humbled your heart, though you knew all this"* (5:22). Knowing did not move his will.

**A will under another.** *"So then it depends not on human will or exertion, but on God, who has mercy"* (Romans 9:16). And at Gethsemane: *"Father, if you are willing, remove this cup from me. Nevertheless, not my will, but yours, be done"* (Luke 22:42). God's will is in 13.1 (*Willing*).

## Life and death set before a person

Scripture sets life itself before the inner being as a choice. *"I have set before you life and death, blessing and curse. Therefore choose life, that you and your offspring may live"* (Deuteronomy 30:19). Turning that chooses life is a considered act: *"Because he considered and turned away from all the transgressions that he had committed, he shall surely live; he shall not die"* (Ezekiel 18:28). Chapter 2 gives the account.

Death is set before the inner being in the same words: *"Behold, I set before you the way of life and the way of death"* (Jeremiah 21:8).
- **A way can seem right and still end there.** *"There is a way that seems right to a man, but its end is the way to death"* (Proverbs 14:12).
- **A choice can be made with death in view.** Esau: *"I am about to die; of what use is a birthright to me?"* (Genesis 25:32).
- **A death can set the direction of a life.** *"he died for all, that those who live might no longer live for themselves but for him who for their sake died and was raised"* (2 Corinthians 5:15).

Plans are made inside a life that is brief. *"What is your life? For you are a mist that appears for a little time and then vanishes. Instead you ought to say, 'If the Lord wills, we will live and do this or that.'"* (James 4:14–15). Some choices are about life itself:
- *"Do not work for the food that perishes, but for the food that endures to eternal life"* (John 6:27)
- *"For the gate is narrow and the way is hard that leads to life, and those who find it are few"* (Matthew 7:14)

Chapter 2 ("Breath lent", "Life set before a person") gives the setting.

## What follows a choice

In these verses the outcome of a choice reaches the inner being, whether the choice was right or wrong.

**When the choice is right:**
- *"His soul shall abide in well-being, and his offspring shall inherit the land"* (Psalm 25:13). "Well-being" is the same word as "the good" in *"choose the good"* (Isaiah 7:15).
- *"The friendship of the Lord is for those who fear him, and he makes known to them his covenant"* (Psalm 25:14). The upright are *"in his confidence"* (Proverbs 3:32), which is the same word as "friendship", in the verse after *"do not choose any of his ways"*.
- *"these I will bring to my holy mountain, and make them joyful in my house of prayer"* (Isaiah 56:7), with *"a name better than sons and daughters"* (56:5).
- At Antioch *"they rejoiced because of its encouragement"*, and the men chosen were *"sent off in peace"* (Acts 15:31, 33).
- Barzillai: *"the king kissed Barzillai and blessed him"* (2 Samuel 19:39).
- The child who refuses the evil and chooses the good eats *"curds and honey"* (Isaiah 7:15), the food of *"everyone who is left in the land"* (7:22).

**When the choice is wrong:**
- **Shame at the thing chosen.** *"you shall blush for the gardens that you have chosen"* (Isaiah 1:29); *"you shall be put to shame"* (65:13); *"it is they who shall be put to shame"* (66:5).
- **The heart and the spirit.** *"you shall cry out for pain of heart and shall wail for breaking of spirit"* (Isaiah 65:14).
- **Their own fears.** *"I also will choose harsh treatment for them and bring their fears upon them"* (Isaiah 66:4).

**In three of Isaiah's oracles the outcome answers the choice in its own terms.** Each verse says it in its own figure:
- They set *"cups of mixed wine for Destiny"*, and the Lord says *"I will destine you to the sword"* (Isaiah 65:11–12). "Destiny" and "destine" share a root.
- They chose gardens and desired oaks, and *"you shall be like an oak whose leaf withers, and like a garden without water"* (1:30).
- They chose what he did not delight in, and *"I also will choose harsh treatment for them"* (66:4).

**The way back stays open.** In the same oracles the offer stands: *"Come now, let us reason together, says the Lord: though your sins are like scarlet, they shall be as white as snow"* (Isaiah 1:18); *"those in her who repent"* (1:27).

## Choices weighed by another

Another person can weigh someone's choices. In the book of Job the friends do this, and the verses show how far it reaches.
- **They read the choice from the words.** Eliphaz: *"For your iniquity teaches your mouth, and you choose the tongue of the crafty. Your own mouth condemns you, and not I"* (Job 15:5–6). He goes from the mouth to the heart: *"Why does your heart carry you away, and why do your eyes flash"* (15:12).
- **They weigh it as tasting.** Elihu: *"the ear tests words as the palate tastes food. Let us choose what is right"* (34:3–4).
- **They read it in suffering.** Elihu: *"this you have chosen rather than affliction"* (36:21).
- **They cannot make the choice for the other.** *"For you must choose, and not I"* (34:33).

The book does not let these verdicts have the last word. The Lord calls Job *"a blameless and upright man, who fears God and turns away from evil"* (1:8). To Eliphaz he says *"you have not spoken of me what is right, as my servant Job has"* (42:7). Elihu is not named in that verdict.

The refusing Elihu pressed is made in the end, in Elihu's own verb, when Job sees the Lord: *"but now my eye sees you; therefore I despise myself, and repent in dust and ashes"* (42:5–6).

## What changes the character

The same words change face with the setting, and the verses show why.
- **"Good" or "evil" in your eyes.**
  - It is the chooser's own sight, where turning from the Lord could begin (Joshua 24:15).
  - It is the chooser's own good, honoured by the king (2 Samuel 19:38).
  - It gives way to *"evil in my eyes"*, the Lord's sight (Isaiah 65:12).
  - *Why:* in Joshua and Isaiah the choice is between the Lord and other gods. With Barzillai it is where an old man who served will live.
- **Knowing good and evil.**
  - It is the knowing that stands under the choice of right and wrong (Isaiah 7:15–16).
  - It is the palate's knowing of the pleasant and the unpleasant, which fades at eighty (2 Samuel 19:35).
  - It is the weighing of words in a dispute (Job 34:3–4).
  - *Why:* the same knowing is turned to right and wrong, to taste, and to judgement.
- **Refusing.**
  - It is refusing the evil (Isaiah 7:15) and refusing the gentle waters (8:6).
  - It is refusing God's terms, as Elihu charges (Job 34:33).
  - It is Job turning it on himself in repentance (42:6).
  - *Why:* the act is the same; what it is aimed at decides whether it is right.
- **Choosing.** It is the choice of a master (Joshua 24:15), a place (Deuteronomy 23:16), a way (Psalm 119:30; Isaiah 66:3), a preference (Proverbs 16:16) and a verdict (Job 34:4). *Why:* it is turned to whatever is set before a person.
- **Delight.** It is the soul's own delight in what it chose (Isaiah 66:3), and it is the Lord's delight as the measure, either not met (65:12; 66:4) or met (56:4). *Why:* whose delight it is decides.
- **Choosing a person.**
  - God chooses before anything in the person, *"before the foundation of the world"* (Ephesians 1:4). He chooses people who are blind and deaf (Isaiah 43:8, 10), and the poor (James 2:5).
  - People choose by what is already seen: *"men of good repute, full of the Spirit and of wisdom"* (Acts 6:3).
  - *Why:* who chooses. With God, the inner life is what the choosing is for. With people, it is the measure.
- **Holiness.**
  - It is the purpose of the choosing: *"that we should be holy and blameless before him"* (Ephesians 1:4).
  - It is a name the chosen already carry, while the inner qualities are still to be put on: *"as God's chosen ones, holy and beloved"* (Colossians 3:12).
  - *Why:* the verses do not say. I set them side by side.
- **Desire beside a choice.**
  - The soul's desire for meat is given room away from the place God chose (Deuteronomy 12:21).
  - The same desire of the soul draws a Levite to that place (18:6).
  - The soul delights in its own abominations, beside a wrong choice (Isaiah 66:3).
  - *Why:* what the desire is turned toward. The first two are allowed in the same words. In the third it is what the soul delights in that decides.
- **Despising.**
  - Others despise the one chosen: Michal *"in her heart"*, by the eyes of people (2 Samuel 6:16, 20); the servant *"deeply despised"*, with kings to bow before him (Isaiah 49:7). It is the same verb in both.
  - People despise the chosen people, concluding God rejected them (Jeremiah 33:24).
  - The chosen people themselves *"despised the pleasant land"* (Psalm 106:24).
  - *Why:* who despises what. Each verse gives its own ground: honour before people, a verdict drawn from ruin, no faith in the promise.

- **Willing.**
  - It is a refusal that keeps faith (1 Samuel 22:17; 26:23), a refusal that honours others at a cost to oneself (2 Samuel 23:17), and a refusal that tramples a plea (2 Samuel 13:14).
  - It is God refused, though what he offered was good (Isaiah 28:12; 30:15).
  - It is a heart that gives freely (Exodus 35:22), and a will made absolute (Daniel 5:19).
  - *Why:* what is refused, and for whose sake. Each passage gives its reason: faith, honour, force, fear, grief, pride.

Chapter 11 ("What changes the character") gathers these with the other activities.

**Connections:**
- from feeling (10.1) and fear (10.12): fear stops a choice and sets a direction; fear is learned at the place God chose, and comes as terror on those who resisted his choice
- with feeling (10.1): rejoicing at the place God chose, shared with those who have least
- from knowing (10.2): the choice rests on knowing how, and on being taught
- from remembering (10.3): the choice is made from what was seen and remembered, and held while it is remembered
- from thinking (10.4) and wanting (10.6): desire, delight, envy and preferring stand beside the choice
- with speaking (10.5): a spoken choice makes the chooser a witness against himself
- into the deed (the path in 10.0)
- before God (10.9): a choice made before a God who has acted
- with others (10.10): choosing together, choices weighed by another, the chosen turned toward others and set against them, others set against the chosen (grumbling, anger, contempt, hatred, rejection), and others standing with them and for them
- with anger (10.13): Saul's anger at his son's choice, and Moses standing in the breach
- with others (10.10): plotting against another; a will pressed, bent to a crowd, held back or helped by friends
- with feeling (10.1), fear (10.12) and wanting (10.6): fear, grief and jealousy hold the will; longing refused for others' sake
- with life set before a person (Chapter 2)
- God's own choosing, purposing and willing: with the other beings (13.1)
