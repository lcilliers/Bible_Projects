# 10.10 Relating to others

The inner being is not private. It reaches out, is reached, and is bound to other people.

- **Love lives in the soul.** "The soul of Jonathan was knit to the soul of David, and Jonathan loved him as his own soul" (1 Samuel 18:1). The lover in the Song seeks "him whom my soul loves" (Song 3:1).
- **Hatred starts in the heart.** "You shall not hate your brother in your heart" (Leviticus 19:17). The law names the heart as the place of hatred. Esau's hatred began as heart-speech: "Esau said to himself, 'The days of mourning for my father are approaching; then I will kill my brother Jacob'" (Genesis 27:41). The next verse says Rebekah was told.
- **The spirit registers the presence and absence of friends.** "My spirit was not at rest because I did not find my brother Titus there" (2 Corinthians 2:13). Later, "his spirit has been refreshed by you all" (2 Corinthians 7:13). Friends "refreshed my spirit as well as yours" (1 Corinthians 16:18).
- **The spirit is the manner of approach.** "Shall I come to you with a rod, or with love in a spirit of gentleness?" (1 Corinthians 4:21).
- **Inner states spread.** The fainthearted soldier is sent home before battle "lest he make the heart of his fellows melt like his own" (Deuteronomy 20:8). "Whoever is fearful and trembling, let him return home" (Judges 7:3).
- **Joy at another's fall is watched.** "Do not rejoice when your enemy falls, and let not your heart be glad when he stumbles" (Proverbs 24:17).

- **Thinking happens together.** The disciples "had argued with one another about who was the greatest" (Mark 9:34). Shared reasoning can become a shared deed: "they said to themselves, 'This is the heir. Let us kill him'" (Luke 20:14).
- **Reasoning together can become a quarrel.** The disciples' reasoning about greatness became "an argument … as to which of them was the greatest" (Luke 9:46). Paul tells the church to welcome the one who is weak in faith, "but not to quarrel over opinions" (Romans 14:1), and to "do all things without grumbling or disputing" (Philippians 2:14).
- **Thinking can make distinctions it should not.** When the rich man is given the good seat and the poor man told to stand: "have you not then made distinctions among yourselves and become judges with evil thoughts?" (James 2:4).
- **The heart can be absent from the words.** "'Eat and drink!' he says to you, but his heart is not with you" (Proverbs 23:7).
- **Love does not keep the count.** Love "is not irritable or resentful" (1 Corinthians 13:5). The Greek word behind "resentful" is the reckoning word: love does not reckon up wrong. It is the same word used of God, who in Christ was "not counting their trespasses against them" (2 Corinthians 5:19). Paul, deserted at his trial, prays: "May it not be charged against them!" (2 Timothy 4:16). The one who has done wrong asks for the same thing: "Let not my lord hold me guilty or remember how your servant did wrong" (2 Samuel 19:19). David answered, "You shall not die", and gave him his oath (19:23). And where there is trust, the count is not even asked for: "they did not ask for an accounting from the men into whose hand they delivered the money … for they dealt honestly" (2 Kings 12:15).

One person can hold another's life. Ben-hadad's servants hoped in a king's mercy: "we have heard that the kings of the house of Israel are merciful kings … Perhaps he will spare your life" (1 Kings 20:31). Israel's leaders kept others alive by their word: "let them live, lest wrath be upon us, because of the oath that we swore to them" (Joshua 9:20). Life is also asked of God for another (Chapter 2).

Some hold to another even to death:
- Ruth to Naomi: "Where you die I will die, and there will I be buried. May the Lord do so to me and more also if anything but death parts me from you" (Ruth 1:17).
- Ittai to David: "wherever my lord the king shall be, whether for death or for life, there also will your servant be" (2 Samuel 15:21).
- Peter: "Even if I must die with you, I will not deny you!" (Matthew 26:35).
- Paul, to friends who wept: "What are you doing, weeping and breaking my heart? For I am ready not only to be imprisoned but even to die in Jerusalem for the name of the Lord Jesus" (Acts 21:13).

Paul says how seldom it is done for another: "For one will scarcely die for a righteous person—though perhaps for a good person one would dare even to die" (Romans 5:7). He sets Christ's death under how a brother is treated: "By what you eat, do not destroy the one for whom Christ died" (Romans 14:15).

A person can be answerable for another's death, or turn another back from it. God tells Ezekiel, "If I say to the wicked, 'You shall surely die,' and you give him no warning … that wicked person shall die for his iniquity, but his blood I will require at your hand" (Ezekiel 3:18). "Whoever brings back a sinner from his wandering will save his soul from death" (James 5:20). John makes love the sign of life itself: "We know that we have passed out of death into life, because we love the brothers. Whoever does not love abides in death" (1 John 3:14). And kindness reaches even the dead: "May he be blessed by the Lord, whose kindness has not forsaken the living or the dead!" (Ruth 2:20).

How a person rates himself, or another, can be put in the words "a dead dog". Mephibosheth to David: "What is your servant, that you should show regard for a dead dog such as I?" (2 Samuel 9:8). Abishai, of Shimei: "Why should this dead dog curse my lord the king?" (2 Samuel 16:9).

A son rates himself, and a father will not hear it out. The younger son prepares his words: *"I am no longer worthy to be called your son. Treat me as one of your hired servants"* (Luke 15:19). *"But while he was still a long way off, his father saw him and felt compassion, and ran and embraced him and kissed him"* (15:20). The son begins the speech, *"Father, I have sinned against heaven and before you. I am no longer worthy to be called your son"* (15:21), and the father speaks over it: *"Bring quickly the best robe, and put it on him"* (15:22). The line about the hired servant is never said. The father saw him first, from far off, and the compassion came before any word. I read that the son's own rating of himself is set aside, not argued with.

## Care, discouragement and hiding between people

What one person carries inside can be for another, or done to another.
- **Care for another is a good anxiety.** Paul's "daily pressure on me of my anxiety for all the churches" is to be weak with the weak (2 Corinthians 11:28–29). Members of one body are to "have the same care for one another" (1 Corinthians 12:25). Section 10.1 sets out the difference between this care and anxiety for oneself.
- **Losing heart is done by another.** "Fathers, do not provoke your children, lest they become discouraged" (Colossians 3:21). Every use of the words for losing heart has another party behind it (10.1).
- **A whisperer separates friends.** "A whisperer separates close friends" (Proverbs 16:28), and "where there is no whisperer, quarreling ceases" (26:20) (10.5).
- **People hide from people, and hide one another.** The weaker side hides: "When the wicked rise, people hide themselves" (Proverbs 28:28). Rahab and Obadiah hid others to keep them alive (Joshua 2:4; 1 Kings 18:4). And no one can hide from a friend who turns: "it is not an adversary who deals insolently with me — then I could hide from him. But it is you, a man, my equal" (Psalm 55:12–13). Section 10.11 follows all of this.

## Fear between people

Fear runs between people in several directions. The fear of God stands behind how one person treats another. People speak to one another's fear. And fear is put into others, sometimes on purpose.

### The fear of God and the weak

In the law, the fear of God is often the "but" that follows a wrong:
- "You shall not curse the deaf or put a stumbling block before the blind, but you shall fear your God" (Leviticus 19:14).
- "You shall not rule over him ruthlessly but shall fear your God" (Leviticus 25:43).

The deaf man cannot hear the curse, the blind man cannot see the block, and the servant cannot answer back. I read the "but you shall fear your God" as saying that God sees what the victim cannot. The fear of God is named where no one else would stop the wrong.

The verses show it working that way.
- "The midwives feared God and did not do as the king of Egypt commanded them, but let the male children live" (Exodus 1:17). Their fear of God outweighed a king.
- Joseph, holding his brothers' lives: "Do this and you will live, for I fear God" (Genesis 42:18).
- Nehemiah would not burden the people as earlier governors had: "But I did not do so, because of the fear of God" (Nehemiah 5:15).

They also show its absence where the weak are harmed. Abraham expected to be killed: "There is no fear of God at all in this place" (Genesis 20:11). Amalek attacked "when you were faint and weary, and cut off your tail, those who were lagging behind you, and he did not fear God" (Deuteronomy 25:18). The ones struck were the ones lagging behind.

### Reverence for people

The same word is used of reverence for people. "Every one of you shall revere his mother and his father" (Leviticus 19:3). Twice the verses say why a leader was held in awe, and both times the reason is God's work in him. "The Lord exalted Joshua in the sight of all Israel, and they stood in awe of him just as they had stood in awe of Moses" (Joshua 4:14). Israel "stood in awe of the king, because they perceived that the wisdom of God was in him to do justice" (1 Kings 3:28).

### Speaking to another's fear

People speak to one another's fear as God does, with a reason. The midwife says to Rachel, "Do not fear, for you have another son" (Genesis 35:17). David says to Mephibosheth, the grandson of the king he replaced, "Do not fear, for I will show you kindness for the sake of your father Jonathan" (2 Samuel 9:7). Elisha says to his servant, "Do not be afraid, for those who are with us are more than those who are with them" (2 Kings 6:16).

Joseph's reason goes further. His brothers feared revenge, and he answered, "Do not fear, for am I in the place of God? … So do not fear; I will provide for you and your little ones" (Genesis 50:19, 21). He would not take the place of God, which is the place from which judgment would come. The same words can be spoken in bad faith (10.5).

### Fear that arrives with a person

A person's coming can bring fear, because of what it might mean. When Samuel came to Bethlehem, "the elders of the city came to meet him trembling and said, 'Do you come peaceably?'" (1 Samuel 16:4). Ahimelech "came to meet David, trembling", and asked, "Why are you alone, and no one with you?" (1 Samuel 21:1). The question each asks shows what they feared.

Some people carry fear with them because of their power. Of Nebuchadnezzar: "because of the greatness that he gave him, all peoples, nations, and languages trembled and feared before him. Whom he would, he killed" (Daniel 5:19). The fear followed the power over life and death, and the verse says that power was given by God. Fear can fall on a crowd because of one person: officials "helped the Jews, for the fear of Mordecai had fallen on them" (Esther 9:3).

And refusing to be afraid can provoke. When Haman saw Mordecai, "that he neither rose nor trembled before him, he was filled with wrath against Mordecai" (Esther 5:9). The man who would not tremble became the one Haman set out to destroy (5:14).

### Making others afraid

Fear is also made. Ahithophel planned to strike David "while he is weary and discouraged and throw him into a panic, and all the people who are with him will flee" (2 Samuel 17:2). The panic was the weapon: frightened people scatter. Words are used the same way (10.5). Psalm 10 names this among the things God will end: he does justice "so that man who is of the earth may strike terror no more" (Psalm 10:18).

The sufferer can become someone others fear: "an object of dread to my acquaintances; those who see me in the street flee from me" (Psalm 31:11). Here the fear isolates the one who is already in trouble.

### Trembling for another

The trembling words are also used of fear *for* someone. Eli "was sitting on his seat by the road watching, for his heart trembled for the ark of God" (1 Samuel 4:13). Elisha says to the Shunammite, with the same root, "See, you have taken all this trouble for us" (2 Kings 4:13). Her care for them is a kind of trembling. When Esther heard of Mordecai in sackcloth, "the queen was deeply distressed" (Esther 4:4).

### Fear that hardens, and fear that gathers

Fear of a person can harden into enmity, as it did in Saul (1 Samuel 18:29; 10.1). The fear of God gathers people instead: "I am a companion of all who fear you, of those who keep your precepts" (Psalm 119:63). "Then those who feared the Lord spoke with one another" (Malachi 3:16).

## Anger between people

Much of what Scripture says about anger is about anger between people.
- **Kinship stops strife, or feeds it.** Abram: "Let there be no strife between you and me … for we are kinsmen" (Genesis 13:8). He gave Lot the choice. In another quarrel, kinship is the weapon: "Because the king is our close relative. Why then are you angry over this matter?" (2 Samuel 19:42). And "a brother offended is more unyielding than a strong city" (Proverbs 18:19).
- **Anger at a brother is judged like murder, and worship waits on peace.** "Everyone who is angry with his brother will be liable to judgment … First be reconciled to your brother, and then come and offer your gift" (Matthew 5:22, 24).
- **The anger of the powerful decides others' fate.** "A king's wrath is like the growling of a lion" (Proverbs 19:12). Those under it plead (Genesis 44:18), read it as a sign ("if he is angry, then know that harm is determined by him", 1 Samuel 20:7), or reach it through a go-between (Acts 12:20). Its favour has a ground too: *"A servant who deals wisely has the king's favor, but his wrath falls on one who acts shamefully"* (Proverbs 14:35); *"his favor is like dew on the grass"* (19:12).
- **The law allows for heat.** The cities of refuge exist *"lest the avenger of blood in hot anger pursue the manslayer and overtake him, because the way is long, and strike him fatally, though the man did not deserve to die, since he had not hated his neighbor in the past"* (Deuteronomy 19:6). The Hebrew is literally "while his heart is hot". The law does not deny the grief. It puts a distance between the heat and the act, because the heat will not tell the innocent from the guilty.
- **The oppressor's wrath is shown with its end in view.** The king of Babylon "ruled the nations in anger with unrelenting persecution" (Isaiah 14:6). The taunt over him begins "How the oppressor has ceased, the insolent fury ceased!" (14:4), and ends "The whole earth is at rest and quiet" (14:7).
- **A leader's anger can yield to a reason.** Moses was angry at Aaron's sons. When Aaron explained, "when Moses heard that, he approved" (Leviticus 10:20).
- **Anger is learned.** "Make no friendship with a man given to anger, nor go with a wrathful man, lest you learn his ways and entangle yourself in a snare" (Proverbs 22:24–25).
- **Strife has a source inside.** "Your passions are at war within you" (James 4:1). Chapter 10.6 follows that.
- **It wears a household down.** "It is better to live in a corner of the housetop than in a house shared with a quarrelsome wife" (Proverbs 21:9). The image is "a continual dripping on a rainy day" (27:15): duration, not a storm.
- **The one attacked feels it inside.** "In anger they bear a grudge against me. My heart is in anguish within me" (Psalm 55:3–4).
- **The trembling word is used of quarrel and rage.** Joseph sends his brothers home with "Do not quarrel on the way" (Genesis 45:24), and the word is *rāgaz*, "to tremble". "If a wise man has an argument with a fool, the fool only rages and laughs, and there is no quiet" (Proverbs 29:9).
- **Love does not get provoked.** Love "is not irritable" (1 Corinthians 13:5).
- **One person's sin can bring trouble on many.** David, under the plague, asks to bear it alone: "these sheep, what have they done? Please let your hand be against me and against my father's house" (2 Samuel 24:17).
- **Vengeance is left to God.** "Never avenge yourselves, but leave it to the wrath of God … if your enemy is hungry, feed him" (Romans 12:19–20).

Several times Scripture answers human anger not with a rebuke but with a question, or a new way of seeing.
- Joseph to his brothers: "it was not you who sent me here, but God" (Genesis 45:8).
- The father to his angry elder son: "this your brother was dead, and is alive" (Luke 15:32). The son had said "this son of yours" (15:30).
- Jesus to the disciples, indignant at two of their number: "whoever would be great among you must be your servant" (Matthew 20:26).

Twice the text ends before the angry one answers: Jonah, and the elder brother.

## Plotting against another

The power to look ahead (10.7, *Planning*) is turned most often, in these verses, against another person. The words are the planning words: to devise, to purpose, to plan. They are used here of harm worked out in advance. What thinking does in plotting is in 10.4, and what the tongue does in 10.5. This section is about what passes between the one who plots and the one plotted against.

**It is planned in the heart.** *"who plan evil things in their heart and stir up wars continually"* (Psalm 140:2). The prophets name the heart as the place where it has to be stopped: *"let none of you devise evil against another in your heart"* (Zechariah 7:10). Zechariah says it again among the things God hates: *"do not devise evil in your hearts against one another, and love no false oath, for all these things I hate"* (8:17).

**It is often done together, and it invites others in.** A son is told what to do when the invitation comes: "My son, if sinners entice you, do not consent. If they say, 'Come with us, let us lie in wait for blood; let us ambush the innocent without reason'" (Proverbs 1:10–11). The will's "no" is set right against the group's plan (10.7, *Willing*).
- *"I hear the whispering of many—terror on every side!—as they scheme together against me, as they plot to take my life"* (Psalm 31:13)
- *"In Heshbon they planned disaster against her: 'Come, let us cut her off from being a nation!'"* (Jeremiah 48:2)
- The council, after Caiaphas spoke: *"So from that day on they made plans to put him to death"* (John 11:53). Then the plan widened: *"the chief priests made plans to put Lazarus to death as well, because on account of him many of the Jews were going away and believing in Jesus"* (12:10–11). The plan grew to take in a man whose life was itself the reason people believed.
- Ezekiel is shown *"the men who devise iniquity and who give wicked counsel in this city"* (Ezekiel 11:2).
- Kings at one table: *"their hearts shall be bent on doing evil. They shall speak lies at the same table"* (Daniel 11:27), and *"plots shall be devised against him"* (11:25). A conqueror's plan is told to those it is aimed at, so that they can flee: "Flee, wander far away … For Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon has made a plan against you and formed a purpose against you" (Jeremiah 49:30).

**It is aimed at the quiet and the weak.** The verses name who it is aimed at:
- *"In arrogance the wicked hotly pursue the poor"* (Psalm 10:2)
- *"against those who are quiet in the land they devise words of deceit"* (Psalm 35:20)
- *"The wicked plots against the righteous and gnashes his teeth at him"* (Psalm 37:12)
- Gog: *"thoughts will come into your mind, and you will devise an evil scheme and say, 'I will go up against the land of unwalled villages. I will fall upon the quiet people who dwell securely'"* (Ezekiel 38:10–11)

**It hides behind something friendly.** *"Sanballat and Geshem sent to me, saying, 'Come and let us meet together at Hakkephirim in the plain of Ono.' But they intended to do me harm"* (Nehemiah 6:2). They sent four times. Nehemiah's answer was not to argue about their intent but to keep to his own work: *"I am doing a great work and I cannot come down"* (6:3). Saul's offer of his daughter was the same: *"Now Saul thought to make David fall by the hand of the Philistines"* (1 Samuel 18:25). 10.11 gives the hidden trap.

**It presses another's will.** Absalom invited the king to his sheepshearing. The king "would not go", but "Absalom pressed him until he let Amnon and all the king's sons go with him" (2 Samuel 13:25, 27). Then he ordered Amnon's death (13:28). When Joab "would not come", twice, Absalom had his field set on fire, and Joab came (14:29–31). Pilate had said of Jesus, "I find no guilt in him" (John 18:38). Yet, "wishing to satisfy the crowd," he "released for them Barabbas" (Mark 15:15). A will can be pushed until it gives way, and in these passages that is how the plan was carried out.

**It accuses the other of a plan.** Sanballat's fifth letter turned the charge round: *"you and the Jews intend to rebel; that is why you are building the wall"* (Nehemiah 6:6). The council said the same to the apostles: *"you intend to bring this man's blood upon us"* (Acts 5:28). Peter answered, *"We must obey God rather than men"* (5:29). Nehemiah named it as made up: *"you are inventing them out of your own mind"* (6:8). The next verse gives their aim: *"they all wanted to frighten us, thinking, 'Their hands will drop from the work'"* (6:9). I read this as a plan to harm that works by pinning a plan on the other person.

**The one plotted against.** The verses also give the inner life of the person a plot is aimed at.
- **He may not know.** *"But I was like a gentle lamb led to the slaughter. I did not know it was against me they devised schemes"* (Jeremiah 11:19). He hands the matter to the one who sees inside: *"O Lord of hosts, who judges righteously, who tests the heart and the mind … to you have I committed my cause"* (11:20).
- **He prays to be guarded, and that the plan will not go forward.** *"Guard me, O Lord, from the hands of the wicked; preserve me from violent men, who have planned to trip up my feet"* (Psalm 140:4). *"Grant not, O Lord, the desires of the wicked; do not further their evil plot"* (140:8). *"Let them be turned back and disappointed who devise evil against me!"* (Psalm 35:4).
- **He trusts while it goes on.** After the whispering and the scheming, *"But I trust in you, O Lord; I say, 'You are my God.' My times are in your hand"* (Psalm 31:14–15). The plotters plan his time, and he puts his times in God's hand.
- **Someone else may plead.** Esther *"fell at his feet and wept and pleaded with him to avert the evil plan of Haman the Agagite and the plot that he had devised against the Jews"* (Esther 8:3). Haman had *"plotted against the Jews to destroy them, and had cast Pur"* (9:24).
- **Someone else may stop it.** In the shipwreck, *"The soldiers' plan was to kill the prisoners, lest any should swim away and escape. But the centurion, wishing to save Paul, kept them from carrying out their plan"* (Acts 27:42–43). One man's wish for one prisoner stopped a plan against all of them.

**It is named, and checked.**
- *"Whoever plans to do evil will be called a schemer"* (Proverbs 24:8), and the next verse says what it is: *"The devising of folly is sin"* (24:9).
- *"If you have been foolish, exalting yourself, or if you have been devising evil, put your hand on your mouth"* (Proverbs 30:32). The check is to stop before the plan reaches the mouth. The verse after it names where pressing leads: *"pressing anger produces strife"* (30:33).
- In the law, the false witness who planned another's ruin receives his own plan: *"you shall do to him as he had meant to do to his brother"* (Deuteronomy 19:19), *"And the rest shall hear and fear"* (19:20). 10.4 follows what is devised coming back.

Plotting is also turned against God (10.9).

## Loyalty and honour

Loyalty between people was sworn by life. Ittai to David: "As the Lord lives, and as my lord the king lives, wherever my lord the king shall be, whether for death or for life, there also will your servant be" (2 Samuel 15:21). Elisha to Elijah: "As the Lord lives, and as you yourself live, I will not leave you" (2 Kings 2:2). David's lament says it of Saul and Jonathan: "In life and in death they were not divided" (2 Samuel 1:23). Chapter 2 ("Living before God") gives the oath.

Delight in a person can stand against a father. *"And Saul spoke to Jonathan his son and to all his servants, that they should kill David. But Jonathan, Saul's son, delighted much in David"* (1 Samuel 19:1). The writer names him "Saul's son" just where he stands against Saul. The delight acts. Jonathan warns David (19:2). He stands beside his father in the field (19:3). He speaks well of David: *"Let not the king sin against his servant David"* (19:4). And he reminds Saul of Saul's own joy: *"You saw it, and rejoiced"* (19:5). Saul listens and swears that David shall not be put to death (19:6). Jonathan's bond is named twice: his soul *"knit to the soul of David"* (18:1, above), and here, in the danger, his delight. Saul's turn had begun with a saying that *"displeased him"*, and *"Saul eyed David from that day on"* (18:8–9). I read delight and displeasure about one man, in a son and a father, with the look on the side of displeasure (10.6, *Jealousy and envy*).

Honour is joined to life: "Honor your father and your mother, that your days may be long in the land" (Exodus 20:12). And patience with others is named as glory: "Good sense makes one slow to anger, and it is his glory to overlook an offense" (Proverbs 19:11).

## Friends, company and care

Job pictures friends who fail as a dry stream: *"My brothers are treacherous as a torrent-bed, as torrential streams that pass away, which are dark with ice, and where the snow hides itself. When they melt, they disappear; when it is hot, they vanish from their place"* (Job 6:15–17). Caravans turn aside to them and perish (6:18). A wadi runs full in the cold, when no one needs it, and is dry in the heat, when the travellers come. I read friends who are there when they are not needed and gone when they are.

Against it the Preacher sets company: *"Two are better than one … For if they fall, one will lift up his fellow. But woe to him who is alone when he falls and has not another to lift him up! Again, if two lie together, they keep warm, but how can one keep warm alone?"* (Ecclesiastes 4:9–11).

Care for another's body is something Job swears to before God: *"if I have seen anyone perish for lack of clothing, or the needy without covering, if his body has not blessed me, and if he was not warmed with the fleece of my sheep"* (Job 31:19–20). The body that was warmed is pictured as blessing the one who warmed it.

Two ways of rousing oneself toward another stand in the same book. *"The upright are appalled at this, and the innocent stirs himself up against the godless. Yet the righteous holds to his way"* (Job 17:8–9). But in his oath Job disowns the other: *"If I have rejoiced at the ruin of him who hated me, or exulted when evil overtook him"* (31:29), *"I have not let my mouth sin by asking for his life with a curse"* (31:30). It is the same act of rousing oneself, approved in one place and refused in the other. The difference is what it is aimed at: wrong itself, or a person's ruin. Proverbs puts the two powers between people in one line: *"Hatred stirs up strife, but love covers all offenses"* (Proverbs 10:12). Hatred stirs things up to the surface. Love lays something over them.

People hold wishes about one another, and Paul fears what disappointment would bring: *"For I fear that perhaps when I come I may find you not as I wish, and that you may find me not as you wish—that perhaps there may be quarreling, jealousy, anger, hostility, slander, gossip, conceit, and disorder"* (2 Corinthians 12:20). Each side has a wish about the other. I read expectation between people as the ground on which quarrel and jealousy grow, named by the one who fears it.

Another's thirst can be met, refused or used. The King names it as his own: *"I was thirsty and you gave me drink"*, and the righteous ask, *"Lord, when did we see you hungry and feed you, or thirsty and give you drink?"* (Matthew 25:35, 37). The others ask the same: *"Lord, when did we see you hungry or thirsty or a stranger or naked or sick or in prison, and did not minister to you?"* (Matthew 25:44). The answer is *"as you did it to one of the least of these my brothers, you did it to me"* (Matthew 25:40). Neither side knew whom they met. I read that what was done or not done to the thirsty was done to him, whether they knew it or not.

Boaz meets a stranger's thirst: *"when you are thirsty, go to the vessels and drink what the young men have drawn."* Ruth answers, *"Why have I found favor in your eyes, that you should take notice of me, since I am a foreigner?"* (Ruth 2:9–10). Jael meets Sisera's: *"Please give me a little water to drink, for I am thirsty. So she opened a skin of milk and gave him a drink and covered him"* (Judges 4:19), and then she took the tent peg (4:21; 10.5). The same act, given drink and covered, is care in one tent and a trap in the other.

Thirst can be left unmet by those who should meet it. In the siege, *"Even jackals offer the breast; they nurse their young; but the daughter of my people has become cruel … The tongue of the nursing infant sticks to the roof of its mouth for thirst; the children beg for food, but no one gives to them"* (Lamentations 4:3–4). The animals are set above the people. And the worker thirsts among what he makes for another: *"they tread the winepresses, but suffer thirst … yet God charges no one with wrong"* (Job 24:11–12; 10.9).

Company can be too much. *"If you have found honey, eat only enough for you, lest you have your fill of it and vomit it. Let your foot be seldom in your neighbor's house, lest he have his fill of you and hate you"* (Proverbs 25:16–17). The neighbour is set beside honey: sweet, and turned by too much into its opposite. The word for *seldom* is the word for *precious*. I read that the visit is kept rare so that it stays welcome.

Love between two people has its own time in the Song: *"that you not stir up or awaken love until it pleases"* (Song of Songs 2:7; 10.6).

**Connections:** with feeling (10.1), since strife is anger between people and fear spreads; with God (10.9), whose fear governs the treatment of others; with speaking (10.5) and thinking (10.4); with planning (10.7), since plotting is looking ahead turned against another; with feeling, since inner states spread and care is for others (10.1; Chapter 11); with hiding and disclosing (10.11); with life and death (Chapter 2), since love and loyalty reach to death.

Chapter 11 draws together the patterns that run through all of this.
