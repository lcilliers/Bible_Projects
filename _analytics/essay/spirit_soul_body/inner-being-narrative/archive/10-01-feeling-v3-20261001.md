# 10.1 Feeling

Every one of the inner words feels, but not all in the same way.

- **The heart** has the widest range: joy, grief, fear, courage, anxiety, love, contempt, despair.
- **The soul** is above all bitter, longing, weary and troubled. "Why are you cast down, O my soul, and why are you in turmoil within me?" (Psalm 42:5).
- **The spirit** is troubled, crushed, faint, provoked, and hot or cool in temper. "My spirit faints within me" (Psalm 143:4). "He who has a cool spirit is a man of understanding" (Proverbs 17:27).
- **The flesh** trembles, faints and sings (Chapter 6).

A firm heart is not afraid: "He is not afraid of bad news; his heart is firm, trusting in the Lord" (Psalm 112:7). A hardened heart does not fear God: "why do you … harden our heart, so that we fear you not?" (Isaiah 63:17).

A feeling is not a single moment. Scripture follows it as it rises, settles in the body, stays or passes, and turns into something else. Anger is the first feeling I have followed through all its verses, so I follow it through this chapter. I expect other feelings to show their own course as I read them.

## How a feeling rises

Very often, something is seen or heard first.
- "The men were indignant and very angry, because he had done an outrageous thing in Israel" (Genesis 34:7). They had just heard of it.
- "I was very angry when I heard their outcry and these words" (Nehemiah 5:6).
- "When Haman saw that Mordecai did not bow down or pay homage to him, Haman was filled with fury" (Esther 3:5).
- Paul's "spirit was provoked within him as he saw that the city was full of idols" (Acts 17:16).

What is perceived may not even be true. Potiphar heard his wife's story, "his anger was kindled", and Joseph went to prison (Genesis 39:19–20). The officials "were enraged at Jeremiah" over a charge he had just called "a lie" (Jeremiah 37:14–15). The feeling acts on what it believes it has seen.

Anger often rises where a will is blocked:
- Balaam beats his donkey, and says why: "Because you have made a fool of me" (Numbers 22:29).
- Naaman: "Naaman was angry and went away, saying, 'Behold, I thought that he would surely come out to me'" (2 Kings 5:11).
- Nebuchadnezzar is refused, and "the expression of his face was changed" (Daniel 3:19).

It rises where a heart that was lifted up is checked. Uzziah "when he was strong, he grew proud" (2 Chronicles 26:16). When the priests withstood him, "Uzziah was angry" (26:19). The king's anger burned when "the heart of the king was merry with wine" and his queen refused him (Esther 1:10–12). Chapter 11 follows this threshold further.

And it rises on behalf of someone else. Jonathan "rose from the table in fierce anger … for he was grieved for David" (1 Samuel 20:34). When Jesus saw the children turned away, "he was indignant" (Mark 10:14).

## Where it goes in the body

A feeling that cannot reach its cause sinks into the body. "Cain was very angry, and his face fell" (Genesis 4:5). Ahab went home "vexed and sullen … lay down on his bed and turned away his face and would eat no food" (1 Kings 21:4).

Vexation that goes on and on reaches the eyes. "My eye has grown dim from vexation" (Job 17:7). "My eye wastes away because of grief" (Psalm 6:7). It keeps a person awake: "Even in the night his heart does not rest" (Ecclesiastes 2:23). Held-in anger presses from within. Elihu says, "my belly is like wine that has no vent … I must speak, that I may find relief" (Job 32:19–20).

The body also shows when a feeling is resolved. Hannah, provoked "year by year", wept and would not eat (1 Samuel 1:7). After she had poured out her soul and been blessed, she "went her way and ate, and her face was no longer sad" (1:18).

## How long it stays

Feelings have a course in time, and Scripture watches it.
- **It can pass.** Rebekah sends Jacob away "until your brother's anger turns away from you, and he forgets what you have done to him" (Genesis 27:45). Years later, "Esau ran to meet him and embraced him" (33:4).
- **It can be kept forever.** Of Edom, Esau's nation: "his anger tore perpetually, and he kept his wrath forever" (Amos 1:11).
- **It can lodge and be cherished.** "Anger lodges in the heart of fools" (Ecclesiastes 7:9). "The godless in heart cherish anger" (Job 36:13).
- **It can be fed by a plan.** Rebekah is told that "your brother Esau comforts himself about you by planning to kill you" (Genesis 27:42). The anger is soothed, and kept alive, by imagining what it will do.
- **It repeats.** "A man of great wrath will pay the penalty, for if you deliver him, you will only have to do it again" (Proverbs 19:19).
- **It is given a limit.** "Be angry and do not sin; do not let the sun go down on your anger, and give no opportunity to the devil" (Ephesians 4:26–27). A day, not a lifetime.

Remembering and forgetting are bound up with this course. Section 10.3 follows them.

## Where it turns

A feeling can turn back on the one who has it. "You who tear yourself in your anger" (Job 18:4). Joseph forbids it: "do not be distressed or angry with yourselves because you sold me here" (Genesis 45:5). Jonah, angry at God's mercy, asks to die: "Yes, I do well to be angry, angry enough to die" (Jonah 4:9).

It can heat a person inside. "Fret not yourself" in Psalm 37 is literally *do not heat yourself*: "Refrain from anger, and forsake wrath! Fret not yourself; it tends only to evil" (Psalm 37:8). The psalm puts other movements in place of the heat: "Trust in the Lord … Commit your way to the Lord … Be still before the Lord and wait patiently for him" (37:3, 5, 7).

It can pass into another feeling. David was angry when God struck Uzzah, and then "David was afraid of the Lord that day" (2 Samuel 6:8–9). And it can be held together with grief. Jesus "looked around at them with anger, grieved at their hardness of heart" (Mark 3:5). His anger and his grief have the same object, and the next thing he does is heal.

## When feeling changes character

The same feeling is not always the same thing.
- **Anger** can heal, as Jesus's does (Mark 3:5). It can stop its ears and stone a man (Acts 7:57–58). It can be "a rage that has reached up to heaven" (2 Chronicles 28:9). "Be angry and do not sin" (Ephesians 4:26) tells me anger itself is not forbidden. What it does, and how long it stays, is the question.
- **Indignation** is the disciples' complaint at a woman's gift: "Why this waste?" (Matthew 26:8). It is also one of the fruits of a grief that turns to God: "what indignation, what fear, what longing, what zeal" (2 Corinthians 7:11).
- **Vexation**, *ka'as*, is what a person does to another ("her rival used to provoke her grievously", 1 Samuel 1:6), what a person bears ("I have been speaking out of my great anxiety and vexation", 1:16), and even something that does good: "Sorrow is better than laughter, for by sadness of face the heart is made glad" (Ecclesiastes 7:3).
- **Provoking** is what love does not do. It "is not irritable" (1 Corinthians 13:5). Yet believers are told, "let us consider how to stir up one another to love and good works" (Hebrews 10:24), with the same Greek word.

What makes the difference, as far as the verses show:
- **What the feeling is for.** Is it on behalf of someone wronged, or for my own will?
- **What it is joined with.** Grief makes it tender; jealousy makes it hard: "jealousy makes a man furious … He will accept no compensation" (Proverbs 6:34–35).
- **How long it stays.**
- **Whether it is seen truly.**
- **The answer it meets.** "A soft answer turns away wrath, but a harsh word stirs up anger" (Proverbs 15:1).
- **Whether it is brought before God.** "Be angry, and do not sin; ponder in your own hearts on your beds, and be silent" (Psalm 4:4).

Chapter 11 (§4) sets these beside what the other activities show.

## Feeling and thinking

Feeling and thinking run into each other. "When the cares of my heart are many, your consolations cheer my soul" (Psalm 94:19). A thought can bring a feeling that reaches the body: Belshazzar saw the writing on the wall, and "his thoughts alarmed him; his limbs gave way, and his knees knocked together" (Daniel 5:6). A remembered fear goes on being turned over: "Your heart will muse on the terror" (Isaiah 33:18). A mind that will not settle stalls the whole person: "How long will you go limping between two different opinions?" (1 Kings 18:21). And the Hebrew word for meditating also means to moan: "I moan like a dove" (Isaiah 38:14).

Feeling also drives thinking. Saul's anger at a song becomes a calculation: "what more can he have but the kingdom?" (1 Samuel 18:8). Section 10.4 follows that.

Feelings pass between the inner being and the body in both directions. "A glad heart makes a cheerful face" (Proverbs 15:13). "A joyful heart is good medicine" (Proverbs 17:22). Going the other way: "wine to gladden the heart of man, oil to make his face shine and bread to strengthen man's heart" (Psalm 104:15). "Oil and perfume make the heart glad" (Proverbs 27:9).

## God feels

God feels in heart and soul too. "The Lord regretted that he had made man on the earth, and it grieved him to his heart" (Genesis 6:6). "I will rejoice in doing them good … with all my heart and all my soul" (Jeremiah 32:41). God's anger is also in Scripture, and how the human being meets it is the subject of Chapter 13.
