# 10.1 Feeling

Every one of the inner words feels, but not all in the same way.

- **The heart** has the widest range: joy, grief, fear, courage, anxiety, love, contempt, despair.
- **The soul** is above all bitter, longing, weary and troubled. "Why are you cast down, O my soul, and why are you in turmoil within me?" (Psalm 42:5).
- **The spirit** is troubled, crushed, faint, provoked, and hot or cool in temper. "My spirit faints within me" (Psalm 143:4). "He who has a cool spirit is a man of understanding" (Proverbs 17:27).
- **The flesh** trembles, faints and sings (Chapter 6).

A feeling is not a single moment. Scripture follows it as it rises, settles in the body, stays or passes, and turns into something else. Anger and fear are the feelings I have followed through their verses so far. Anger is followed below through that course. Fear has a section of its own, because its words have to be understood before its course makes sense. I expect other feelings to show their own course as I read them.

## How a feeling rises

Very often, something is seen or heard first.
- "The men were indignant and very angry, because he had done an outrageous thing in Israel" (Genesis 34:7). They had just heard of it.
- "I was very angry when I heard their outcry and these words" (Nehemiah 5:6).
- "When Haman saw that Mordecai did not bow down or pay homage to him, Haman was filled with fury" (Esther 3:5).
- Paul's "spirit was provoked within him as he saw that the city was full of idols" (Acts 17:16).

What is perceived may not even be true. Potiphar heard his wife's story, "his anger was kindled", and Joseph went to prison (Genesis 39:19–20). The officials "were enraged at Jeremiah" over a charge he had just called "a lie" (Jeremiah 37:14–15). The feeling acts on what it believes it has seen.

Anger often rises where a will is blocked:
- Balaam beats his donkey, and says why: "Because you have made a fool of me" (Numbers 22:29).
- Naaman: "Naaman was angry and went away, saying, 'Behold, I thought that he would surely come out to me'" (2 Kings 5:11).
- Nebuchadnezzar is refused, and "the expression of his face was changed" (Daniel 3:19).

It rises where a heart that was lifted up is checked. Uzziah "when he was strong, he grew proud" (2 Chronicles 26:16). When the priests withstood him, "Uzziah was angry" (26:19). The king's anger burned when "the heart of the king was merry with wine" and his queen refused him (Esther 1:10–12). Chapter 11 follows this threshold further.

And anger rises on behalf of someone else. Jonathan "rose from the table in fierce anger … for he was grieved for David" (1 Samuel 20:34). When Jesus saw the children turned away, "he was indignant" (Mark 10:14).

## Where it goes in the body

A feeling that cannot reach its cause sinks into the body. "Cain was very angry, and his face fell" (Genesis 4:5). Ahab went home "vexed and sullen … lay down on his bed and turned away his face and would eat no food" (1 Kings 21:4).

Vexation that goes on and on reaches the eyes. "My eye has grown dim from vexation" (Job 17:7). "My eye wastes away because of grief" (Psalm 6:7). It keeps a person awake: "Even in the night his heart does not rest" (Ecclesiastes 2:23). Held-in anger presses from within. Elihu says, "my belly is like wine that has no vent … I must speak, that I may find relief" (Job 32:19–20).

The body also shows when a feeling is resolved. Hannah, provoked "year by year", wept and would not eat (1 Samuel 1:7). After she had poured out her soul and been blessed, she "went her way and ate, and her face was no longer sad" (1:18).

## How long it stays

Feelings have a course in time, and Scripture watches it.
- **It can pass.** Rebekah sends Jacob away "until your brother's anger turns away from you, and he forgets what you have done to him" (Genesis 27:45). Years later, "Esau ran to meet him and embraced him" (33:4).
- **It can be kept forever.** Of Edom, Esau's nation: "his anger tore perpetually, and he kept his wrath forever" (Amos 1:11).
- **It can lodge and be cherished.** "Anger lodges in the heart of fools" (Ecclesiastes 7:9). "The godless in heart cherish anger" (Job 36:13).
- **It can be fed by a plan.** Rebekah is told that "your brother Esau comforts himself about you by planning to kill you" (Genesis 27:42). The anger is soothed, and kept alive, by imagining what it will do.
- **It repeats.** "A man of great wrath will pay the penalty, for if you deliver him, you will only have to do it again" (Proverbs 19:19).
- **It is given a limit.** "Be angry and do not sin; do not let the sun go down on your anger, and give no opportunity to the devil" (Ephesians 4:26–27). A day, not a lifetime.

Remembering and forgetting are bound up with this course. Section 10.3 follows them.

## Where it turns

A feeling can turn back on the one who has it. "You who tear yourself in your anger" (Job 18:4). Joseph forbids it: "do not be distressed or angry with yourselves because you sold me here" (Genesis 45:5). Jonah, angry at God's mercy, asks to die: "Yes, I do well to be angry, angry enough to die" (Jonah 4:9).

It can heat a person inside. "Fret not yourself" in Psalm 37 is literally *do not heat yourself*: "Refrain from anger, and forsake wrath! Fret not yourself; it tends only to evil" (Psalm 37:8). The psalm puts other movements in place of the heat: "Trust in the Lord … Commit your way to the Lord … Be still before the Lord and wait patiently for him" (37:3, 5, 7).

Anger can pass into another feeling. David was angry when God struck Uzzah, and then "David was afraid of the Lord that day" (2 Samuel 6:8–9). And it can be held together with grief. Jesus "looked around at them with anger, grieved at their hardness of heart" (Mark 3:5). His anger and his grief have the same object, and the next thing he does is heal.

## Fear

Scripture never stops to define fear. It shows it, and it shows it in the whole person. Saul "was afraid, and his heart trembled greatly" (1 Samuel 28:5). The psalmist says, "My flesh trembles for fear of you" (Psalm 119:120). Eliphaz says, "dread came upon me, and trembling, which made all my bones shake" (Job 4:14). In these verses fear is not an opinion about danger. It is something that happens in the heart and the body together. It shows on the face: when the king asked Nehemiah why his face was sad, "Then I was very much afraid" (Nehemiah 2:2). Chapter 6 follows it into the flesh and the bones.

The verbs show something more. In these verses fear is not something the person does. It comes upon him. "Fear and trembling come upon me, and horror overwhelms me" (Psalm 55:5). "Trembling took hold of them there" (Psalm 48:6). The person is the one acted on.

Fear has a cause outside and often a reason inside. The first fear in the Bible names both: "I heard the sound of you in the garden, and I was afraid, because I was naked, and I hid myself" (Genesis 3:10). Adam heard a sound outside him, and the reason was something he knew about himself. Moses "was afraid, and thought, 'Surely the thing is known'" (Exodus 2:14). Here too, the fear rises when what is hidden inside meets what is coming from outside.

Often the cause is simply outside: what is seen and heard. "When Saul and all Israel heard these words of the Philistine, they were dismayed and greatly afraid" (1 Samuel 17:11). It rises at an arrival: the elders "came to meet him trembling and said, 'Do you come peaceably?'" (1 Samuel 16:4). It rises at a discovery: "Isaac trembled very violently" (Genesis 27:33). It rises in the night, before something not clearly seen: "A spirit glided past my face; the hair of my flesh stood up" (Job 4:15). It can have two causes at once: the people sat "trembling because of this matter and because of the heavy rain" (Ezra 10:9).

### The words

The English word "fear" covers several Hebrew words, and they do not all mean the same thing.
- ***Yārēʾ*, "to fear".** This is the ordinary word. It is used for fear of an enemy and for fear of God, and turned round it describes the one who is feared: God is "awesome", the wilderness "terrifying" (Deuteronomy 10:17; 8:15).
- ***Paḥad*, "to dread".** This is the dread that falls on a person. Yet once the same word is used of joy: "your heart shall thrill and exult" (Isaiah 60:5).
- ***Ḥārad*, "to tremble".** This is the body's trembling. Yet Eli "was sitting on his seat by the road watching, for his heart trembled for the ark of God" (1 Samuel 4:13). That is trembling *for* something he loved, not *before* a threat.
- ***Rāgaz*, "to tremble, be stirred".** The earth quakes with it. A person is stirred with it to anger, "Be angry, and do not sin" (Psalm 4:4), and to grief: David "was deeply moved" and wept for Absalom (2 Samuel 18:33).

I read these words as naming a stirring of the inner being, a shaking. The verse and its situation decide whether that shaking is fear, anger, grief, care or joy. That is why the same root can be rendered so many ways.

### What fear does

Fear does not stay inside. It moves the person, and the verses follow where it moves them.
- **It drives away.** Adam hid. Uriah "was afraid and fled and escaped to Egypt" (Jeremiah 26:21). At Sinai "the people were afraid and trembled, and they stood far off" (Exodus 20:18).
- **It holds back the hand.** "The young man did not draw his sword, for he was afraid" (Judges 8:20). Section 10.7 follows this.
- **It silences.** "Samuel was afraid to tell the vision to Eli" (1 Samuel 3:15). Section 10.5 follows this.
- **It bends speech into a lie.** Sarah said "I did not laugh," "for she was afraid" (Genesis 18:15).
- **It takes the wrong voice.** "I feared the people and obeyed their voice" (1 Samuel 15:24). Section 10.8 follows this.
- **It hardens.** It began because "the Lord was with him but had departed from Saul" (1 Samuel 18:12). It ended: "Saul was even more afraid of David. So Saul was David's enemy continually" (18:29).
- **It spreads.** The fearful man is sent home "lest he make the heart of his fellows melt like his own" (Deuteronomy 20:8).
- **It stays, and what it fears can follow.** "The sword that you fear shall overtake you there" (Jeremiah 42:16). "For the thing that I fear comes upon me, and what I dread befalls me" (Job 3:25). Under the curse it fills every hour: "In the morning you shall say, 'If only it were evening!' and at evening you shall say, 'If only it were morning!'" (Deuteronomy 28:67).

One question from God holds several of these together: "Whom did you dread and fear, so that you lied, and did not remember me, did not lay it to heart? … and you do not fear me?" (Isaiah 57:11). The verse sets two fears against each other. They dreaded someone else, and they did not fear God. The lie and the forgetting stand between the two. Proverbs puts the same thing in one line: "The fear of man lays a snare, but whoever trusts in the Lord is safe" (Proverbs 29:25).

### The fear of God

The same word, *yārēʾ*, is used of fearing God, and here it does the opposite of what the fear of a threat does.

Exodus 20 shows both on one day. The people saw the thunder and the smoking mountain, "were afraid and trembled, and they stood far off", and asked that God not speak to them, "lest we die" (Exodus 20:18–19). Moses answered: "Do not fear, for God has come to test you, that the fear of him may be before you, that you may not sin" (20:20). In one sentence he takes one fear away and sets another in its place. I read the first as the fear that drives a person away from God, and the second as a fear that keeps a person from sin. The next verse says simply: "The people stood far off, while Moses drew near to the thick darkness where God was" (20:21).

This second fear is not a passing fright. It belongs to the heart.
- God longs for it: "Oh that they had such a heart as this always, to fear me" (Deuteronomy 5:29).
- God promises to give it: "I will put the fear of me in their hearts, that they may not turn from me" (Jeremiah 32:40).
- It can also be present only on the lips: "their fear of me is a commandment taught by men", while "their hearts are far from me" (Isaiah 29:13).

The fear of a threat makes a person run. This fear, in God's own words, keeps a person from turning away. And what it brings is the reverse of what terror brings.
- confidence: "In the fear of the Lord one has strong confidence, and his children will have a refuge" (Proverbs 14:26)
- life: "a fountain of life" (14:27)
- understanding: "the beginning of knowledge" (Proverbs 1:7)
- a turning from evil: "The fear of the Lord is hatred of evil" (Proverbs 8:13)

It sits with joy: "Serve the Lord with fear, and rejoice with trembling" (Psalm 2:11). And it trembles, but at God's word, not at a threat: "this is the one to whom I will look: he who is humble and contrite in spirit and trembles at my word" (Isaiah 66:2).

### What changes it

What makes fear one thing or the other? The verses point to a few things.
- **What it is turned toward.** At the sea Israel "feared greatly" as Egypt marched (Exodus 14:10). After the crossing, "the people feared the Lord, and they believed in the Lord" (14:31). It is the same people and the same word, with a different object, and the change came after God had acted. The sailors with Jonah went the same way: afraid in the storm, then, when the sea was calm, "the men feared the Lord exceedingly" (Jonah 1:5, 16).
- **A deliberate turning.** Isaiah is told to move his fear: "do not fear what they fear, nor be in dread. But the Lord of hosts, him you shall honor as holy. Let him be your fear, and let him be your dread" (Isaiah 8:12–13). The fear is not removed. It is given a different object.
- **Whether the heart is in it.** That is the difference between fear "taught by men" and fear put "in their hearts" (Isaiah 29:13; Jeremiah 32:40). A hardened heart does not fear God at all: "why do you … harden our heart, so that we fear you not?" (Isaiah 63:17).
- **Who speaks to it, and why.** Words of comfort can be a lie. Jael says "do not be afraid" to Sisera before the tent peg (Judges 4:18). When God says it, he gives a reason, and the reason is mostly himself: "Fear not, Abram, I am your shield" (Genesis 15:1); "fear not, for I am with you" (Isaiah 41:10).

### Meeting fear

In the verses, fear of a threat is not argued away. It is met by someone. God's "fear not" comes with "I am with you" (Isaiah 41:10), "I am your shield" (Genesis 15:1), "I have redeemed you" (Isaiah 43:1). And a person can meet it the same way from his own side: "When I am afraid, I put my trust in you" (Psalm 56:3), and a verse later, "in God I trust; I shall not be afraid" (56:4). The fear is named, and then turned toward God. Of the one who is steady, the psalm says: "He is not afraid of bad news; his heart is firm, trusting in the Lord" (Psalm 112:7).

The verses that come closest to saying what fear itself is are in the New Testament: "There is no fear in love, but perfect love casts out fear. For fear has to do with punishment" (1 John 4:18). I have not yet read the Greek words for fear, and this account will need to be read again when I have.

The fear of God as a way of life, and God's "fear not", are followed further in 10.9. Fear between people is in 10.10, and dread under God's anger in Chapter 13.

## When feeling changes character

The same feeling is not always the same thing.
- **Anger** can heal, as Jesus's does (Mark 3:5). It can stop its ears and stone a man (Acts 7:57–58). It can be "a rage that has reached up to heaven" (2 Chronicles 28:9). "Be angry and do not sin" (Ephesians 4:26) tells me anger itself is not forbidden. What it does, and how long it stays, is the question.
- **Indignation** is the disciples' complaint at a woman's gift: "Why this waste?" (Matthew 26:8). It is also one of the fruits of a grief that turns to God: "what indignation, what fear, what longing, what zeal" (2 Corinthians 7:11).
- **Vexation**, *ka'as*, is what a person does to another ("her rival used to provoke her grievously", 1 Samuel 1:6), what a person bears ("I have been speaking out of my great anxiety and vexation", 1:16), and even something that does good: "Sorrow is better than laughter, for by sadness of face the heart is made glad" (Ecclesiastes 7:3).
- **Fear** is forbidden and commanded, sometimes in one sentence (Exodus 20:20). The word for the fear that makes people run is the word for the fear that keeps them from turning away. The section on fear above sets out what changes it.
- **Provoking** is what love does not do. It "is not irritable" (1 Corinthians 13:5). Yet believers are told, "let us consider how to stir up one another to love and good works" (Hebrews 10:24), with the same Greek word.

What makes the difference, as far as the verses show:
- **What, or whom, it faces.** Fear of the army and of the sea is followed, after God has acted, by fear of the Lord (Exodus 14:10, 31; the section on fear above).
- **What the feeling is for.** Is it on behalf of someone wronged, or for my own will?
- **What it is joined with.** Grief makes it tender; jealousy makes it hard: "jealousy makes a man furious … He will accept no compensation" (Proverbs 6:34–35).
- **How long it stays.**
- **Whether it is seen truly.**
- **The answer it meets.** "A soft answer turns away wrath, but a harsh word stirs up anger" (Proverbs 15:1).
- **Whether it is brought before God.** "When I am afraid, I put my trust in you" (Psalm 56:3). "Be angry, and do not sin; ponder in your own hearts on your beds, and be silent" (Psalm 4:4).

Chapter 11 (§4) sets these beside what the other activities show.

## Feeling and thinking

Feeling and thinking run into each other. "When the cares of my heart are many, your consolations cheer my soul" (Psalm 94:19). A thought can bring a feeling that reaches the body: Belshazzar saw the writing on the wall, and "his thoughts alarmed him; his limbs gave way, and his knees knocked together" (Daniel 5:6). A remembered fear goes on being turned over: "Your heart will muse on the terror" (Isaiah 33:18). A mind that will not settle stalls the whole person: "How long will you go limping between two different opinions?" (1 Kings 18:21). And the Hebrew word for meditating also means to moan: "I moan like a dove" (Isaiah 38:14).

Feeling also drives thinking. Saul's anger at a song becomes a calculation: "what more can he have but the kingdom?" (1 Samuel 18:8). Section 10.4 follows that.

Feelings pass between the inner being and the body in both directions. "A glad heart makes a cheerful face" (Proverbs 15:13). "A joyful heart is good medicine" (Proverbs 17:22). Going the other way: "wine to gladden the heart of man, oil to make his face shine and bread to strengthen man's heart" (Psalm 104:15). "Oil and perfume make the heart glad" (Proverbs 27:9).

## God feels

God feels in heart and soul too. "The Lord regretted that he had made man on the earth, and it grieved him to his heart" (Genesis 6:6). "I will rejoice in doing them good … with all my heart and all my soul" (Jeremiah 32:41). God's anger is also in Scripture, and how the human being meets it is the subject of Chapter 13.

**Connections:**
- with speaking (10.5) and choosing (10.7): fear holds back the mouth and the hand
- with right and wrong (10.8): fear that leads into wrong
- with thinking (10.4) and speaking (10.5): anger reckons, and held anger is released in words
- with remembering and forgetting (10.3): how long a feeling stays
- with the body (Chapter 6)
- with others (10.10): feelings spread (Deuteronomy 20:8), strife is anger between people, and fear is spoken to by others
- with God (10.9, Chapter 13): anger at God, fear before God and the fear of God, and life under God's anger
