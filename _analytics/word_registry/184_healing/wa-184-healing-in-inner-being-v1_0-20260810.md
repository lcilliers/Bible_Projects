## Preamble — the field and the spine

Healing is a wide field. Registry 184 gathers forty-four Hebrew and Greek terms — verbs and nouns for *to heal*, *to cure*, *health*, *soundness*, *remedy*, *wholeness*, *binding a wound* — and, at its edges, the neighbouring vocabularies of *saving*, *living*, and *peace*. What holds the field together is a single act underneath all the words: **something broken is restored to soundness.** The break may be in the body, in the inner person, or in the bond between a people and their God, and the field moves across all three.

This is, first, a bodily field. Most incidences are of a sick or wounded body made well. But it does not stay in the body. Repeatedly the movement crosses the boundary in both directions — an inner state showing itself as bodily soundness or its loss, and a bodily healing reaching into the heart, the spirit, the will. That crossing is where the field speaks to the programme's governing question, and it is what this synthesis foregrounds.

The field presents seven facets, observed from the evidence rather than imposed: **(A) the healing act**, **(B) the healer**, **(C) the means**, **(D) health as a settled state**, **(E) the healing of the inner being and of relational and national breach**, **(F) the hinge where healing pivots into saving, living, and peace**, and **(G) the undoing — where healing is withheld.**

The spine question for every movement is the same: *how does this operate in the inner being* — what is its source or cause, what is its inner operation, and what is its effect on heart, body, behaviour, or will. Counts inform the reading but do not decide it; a movement attested once is still a movement.

*A note on evidence: this synthesis rests on a sampling layer — one example verse per unique span. Its claims are extract-level. A verse-exhaustive pass would be required to settle distribution and the borderline cases registered at the end.*

## Part A — The healing act

At the centre of the field is the plain act: the sick and the wounded are made well. In the Gospels the verb is *therapeuō* — Jesus and his sent ones anoint and heal the sick (*Mark 6:13*), he answers a plea with "**I will come and heal him**" (*Matthew 8:7*), he goes about "**healing every disease**" (*Matthew 4:23*), and the twelve are given power to "**cure diseases**" (*Luke 9:1*). Alongside it runs *iaomai*, which carries the note of completeness and immediacy: a daughter is "**healed instantly**" (*Matthew 15:28*), and at a word a paralysed man is told "**Jesus Christ heals you**" and rises at once (*Acts 9:34*). The noun *iama* names it as a thing given — "**gifts of healing**" (*1 Corinthians 12:9*); *iasis* names the public "**sign of healing**" that cannot be denied (*Acts 4:22*).

In the Hebrew scriptures the act is *rapha*. Water is "**healed**" and stays so (*2 Kings 2:22*); a nation is struck and then, in the same breath, restored — the Lord is "**striking and healing**" (*Isaiah 19:22*). The act can be commissioned, refused, completed, or left incomplete, but its shape is constant: a movement from ruin toward soundness.

The inner-being note already sounds here. The act is often released by something interior in the one healed — most sharply where faith is named as the hinge on which the healing turns ("your faith has **made you well**", *Matthew 9:22*, under the saving-verb treated in Part F). The body is the site of the operation; the disposition is frequently its antecedent.

---

## Part B — The healer

The field is unusually clear about its source. Overwhelmingly, **God is the one who heals.** He names himself by it — "**I am the Lord, your healer**" (*Exodus 15:26*) — and the naming is not incidental but a covenant self-description. He is the one "**who heals all your diseases**" (*Psalm 103:3*), who says "**I will restore health to you, and your wounds I will heal**" (*Jeremiah 30:17*). Because he is the source, the sufferer's movement is to turn to him: "**Heal me, O Lord, and I shall be healed**" (*Jeremiah 17:14*) — a line in which the cure is wholly sourced outside the self, the human part being only the turning.

Under God, and subordinate to him, stand human healers. There are physicians — Joseph commands "**the physicians**" to embalm his father (*Genesis 50:2*) — and the field can ask after them with longing when they are absent: "**Is there no physician there?**" (*Jeremiah 8:22*). But the human means is shown as limited and sometimes futile: a woman "**could not be healed**" though she had spent everything on physicians (*Luke 8:43*). The physician is real but not ultimate.

At the dark edge of this facet stands the **refused healer.** In a collapsing society a man is pressed to take charge and answers, "**I will not be a healer**" (*Isaiah 3:7*). This is an inner-being movement in its own right: not the failure of skill but the withholding of the will — a refusal to bind what is broken.

## Part C — The means

Where the act is named by its mechanism, the field turns concrete and bodily. The commonest means is the **binding of a wound.** *Chavash* is the verb of the bandage: the Lord "**binds up** the brokenness of his people, and heals the wounds inflicted by his blow" (*Isaiah 30:26*); Pharaoh's broken arm has "**not been bound up**, to heal it by binding it with a bandage" (*Ezekiel 30:21*); and the untended body of a people is one whose sores "**are not pressed out or bound up**" (*Isaiah 1:6*). The dressing of the wound is the healing made visible and manual.

Beside binding stand **balm and medicine.** Where these fail, the field says so flatly — "**no medicine for your wound**" (*Jeremiah 30:13*), and of Egypt, "in vain you have used many **medicines**; there is no healing for you" (*Jeremiah 46:11*). There is the **new flesh** that forms over a wound — the very word *tealah* carries that picture, and it appears in the verdict "there is no **healing** for you" (*Jeremiah 46:11*). There are **leaves for healing** on the trees by the temple river (*Ezekiel 47:12*), and there is the healing that is "**refreshment to your bones**", a "**healing to your flesh**" (*Proverbs 3:8*). And there is the simple **easing** of a hurt, named by its absence: "there is no **easing** your hurt; your wound is grievous" (*Nahum 3:19*).

Read as inner-being movements, the means are almost entirely somatic — they operate on flesh, wound, and bone. Their inner weight is carried indirectly: the tended wound is a sign of care extended, the untended wound a sign of care withheld. Where the means turns figurative — the bandaged brokenness of a *people* (Isaiah 30:26) — the movement passes into Part E.

## Part D — Health, soundness, wholeness

Distinct from the act and the means is the **resulting state**: to *be* well, sound, whole. The Greek *hugiainō*/*hugiēs* names the settled condition — "**those who are well** have no need of a physician" (*Luke 5:31*), a hand "**restored, healthy** like the other" (*Matthew 12:13*), a man made to stand "**well**" (*Acts 4:10*). *Holoklēria* names the fullness of it: a lame man given "**perfect health**" in open view (*Acts 3:16*).

Two incidences push this state directly into the inner being. The first is the blessing "that you may be in **good health**, as it goes well with your soul" (*3 John 2*) — where bodily health is set in deliberate parallel with the soul's welfare, the outer condition measured against the inner. The second is the lament in which the loss of soundness is sourced to the inner and moral: "there is no **soundness** in my flesh because of your indignation; there is no **health** in my bones because of my sin" (*Psalm 38:3*). Here the movement runs inward-outward with unusual clarity: sin and divine indignation (the inner and relational cause) produce the un-soundness of flesh and bone (the bodily effect). Health, in this facet, is not a merely physical fact; it is a barometer of a deeper condition.

## Part E — Healing of the inner being, and of relational and national breach

This is the facet in which the field speaks most directly to the programme's question, and it is richer than the bodily material alone would suggest.

**Healing that operates through a disposition.** The noun *marpe* — elsewhere a wound's cure — is repeatedly turned inward. "A **tranquil** heart gives life to the flesh, but envy makes the bones rot" (*Proverbs 14:30*): here an inner state (tranquillity) is itself the healing agent, and its opposite (envy) the disease that rots the body. "A **gentle** tongue is a tree of life, but perverseness in it breaks the spirit" (*Proverbs 15:4*): speech becomes the instrument by which the inner being is healed or fractured. "**Calmness** will lay great offenses to rest" (*Ecclesiastes 10:4*): an interior composure defuses relational rupture. And gracious words are "sweetness to the soul and **health** to the body" (*Proverbs 16:24*) — the healing running from words, through the soul, into the body. The direction here inverts the usual picture: the inner condition is not only the *object* of healing but its *source*.

**Healing of waywardness and of the broken heart.** God says "I will **heal** their apostasy; I will love them freely" (*Hosea 14:4*) — the object of the healing is not a body but a moral-spiritual condition, and its effect is a will turned and love freely given. The Spirit-anointed servant is sent "to **bind up** the brokenhearted" (*Isaiah 61:1*) — the wound-dressing vocabulary of Part C laid directly on the heart. And the reverse, the un-healed inner being, is heard in "my **joy** is gone; grief is upon me; my heart is sick within me" (*Jeremiah 8:18*) — the absence of the very brightening that would heal.

**The reviving of the spirit.** At the edge of the field, healing meets the reviving of life. Jacob, hearing his son lives, has "the **spirit** of their father Jacob **revived**" (*Genesis 45:27*) — an interior restoration. Hezekiah prays "**restore me to health** and make me live" (*Isaiah 38:16*), pairing healing and living in one plea. (These reviving incidences sit on a boundary registered below.)

**Healing of the collective.** The field moves, too, to peoples and nations. The Lord binds up "the brokenness of **his people**" (*Isaiah 30:26*); Egypt, struck and healed, "will return to the Lord" (*Isaiah 19:22*); health is restored to Zion, "for whom no one cares" (*Jeremiah 30:17*); a whole people's "**healing** shall spring up speedily" as their righteousness goes before them (*Isaiah 58:8*). Here the movement is to a collection rather than an individual, and the wound is relational — a breach of favour between a people and God, healed by restoration rather than by medicine.

## Part F — The hinge: heal ↔ save ↔ live ↔ shalom

Healing does not have a fixed border. In several of its words the meaning **pivots on its object** — the same term carrying bodily healing in one verse and something larger in the next. This is the field's hinge, and it is genuine, not imposed.

**Heal and save.** The verb *sōzō* is the sharpest pivot. It is bodily rescue — a woman is "**made well**" by touching a garment (*Matthew 9:21–22*), a crippled man "**has been healed**" (*Acts 4:9*), a sick friend "will **recover**" (*John 11:12*). And it is the salvation of the soul — believers are "**saved**" by his blood (*Romans 5:9*), the implanted word is able "to **save** your souls" (*James 1:21*). One word holds both, and the physical healing reads as continuous with the larger deliverance. Its compound *diasōzō* pivots the same way: those who touch the fringe are "**made well**" (*Matthew 14:36*), while shipwrecked men are "**brought safely**" to land (*Acts 27:44*).

**Heal and live.** *Chayah* — to live — reaches into healing at its restoration edge: a sick king asks whether he shall "**recover**" (*2 Kings 1:2*), Hezekiah after his sickness has "**recovered**" (*Isaiah 38:9*), the psalmist is "**restored… to life**" from the pit (*Psalm 30:3*). To be healed, in this vocabulary, is to be made to live again.

**Heal and peace.** *Shalom* is the widest of the pivots — completeness, welfare, safety, and health all in one word. It names bodily "**health**" in the bones (*Psalm 38:3*), "**welfare**" won through bitterness (*Isaiah 38:17*), "**prosperity**" (*Jeremiah 33:9*), the "**well-being**" the Lord forms (*Isaiah 45:7*). Healing, seen through *shalom*, is one mode of a larger wholeness — the restoration of a completeness that is at once bodily, relational, and settled.

The hinge is presented here as the shape the words take, not as a doctrinal verdict. But its inner-being weight is plain: in this vocabulary the body's healing is never merely the body's. It is read as of a piece with living, with peace, and with being saved.

---

## Part G — The undoing: when there is no healing

The field carries its own negation, and the method requires it be recorded rather than smoothed away. Against every act of healing stands the possibility that healing is **withheld or exhausted.**

There is the wound "broken **beyond healing**" (*Proverbs 6:15*), the "**incurable** disease" (*2 Chronicles 21:18*), the people mocked until "there was no **remedy**" (*2 Chronicles 36:16*), the vessel that "can never be **mended**" (*Jeremiah 19:11*). There is Egypt's verdict, "there is no **healing** for you" (*Jeremiah 46:11*), and the flat "no **medicine** for your wound" (*Jeremiah 30:13*), and "no **easing** your hurt" (*Nahum 3:19*). There is the great king who "is not able to **cure** you or heal your wound" (*Hosea 5:13*), the physician sought and not found while "the health of the daughter of my people" is "not… restored" (*Jeremiah 8:22*), and the healer who refuses the role (*Isaiah 3:7*, Part B).

Read as an inner-being movement, the undoing marks the point at which the breach — of body, of nation, of covenant — has passed beyond the reach of the operation. It is not despair for its own sake; it is the boundary that gives the healing its weight. Where healing can be refused, withheld, or exhausted, its arrival is not automatic but gift.

## Reflection — interpretation and application (labelled; not conclusions)

**Interpretation.** Across the field, healing repeatedly crosses the boundary between body and inner being, and it crosses in *both* directions. Sin and indignation produce un-soundness of flesh and bone (*Psalm 38:3*); a tranquil heart gives life to the flesh while envy rots the bones (*Proverbs 14:30*); gracious words heal the body and sweeten the soul (*Proverbs 16:24*). Healing is therefore not only something done *to* the body from outside; it is something that can arise *from* an inner condition and reach *into* one. The healer, meanwhile, is overwhelmingly God — human physicians and material means are real but subordinate and sometimes fail — so that even where the sufferer's own disposition (faith, turning, repentance) is the antecedent, the enablement is sourced beyond the self. And the hinge vocabulary (*sōzō*, *chayah*, *shalom*) suggests that in this field bodily healing is read as continuous with living, with peace, and with being saved. I hold this as the shape the words take, not as a settled doctrine of what healing *is*.

**Application — cultures and interest groups.** For clinical and psychosomatic readers, the two-way body–inner traffic will resonate, though the text's sourcing of ultimate healing to God will land very differently across faith and secular frames. For pastoral and counselling work, Part E — the tranquil heart, the bound-up brokenhearted, the healing carried by gentle speech — is the directly usable core. For communities in which illness already carries shame or is read as divine judgement, the sin-and-sickness link (*Psalm 38:3*) can wound as readily as it can illuminate, and needs careful handling. And against any reading that treats healing as guaranteed, the undoing facet (Part G — "no healing", "incurable", "no remedy") is the necessary counterweight; it should not be suppressed, because it is the field's own limit on itself.

**Application — translation.** The pivot-words are the hardest to render. A single term that carries *heal / save / live / peace* cannot be caught by one English word, and every choice narrows the field. When translators split *shalom* into "well", "welfare", "health", "safety", and "peace", or *sōzō* into "made well" and "saved", they fragment a unity the source languages hold together — and the English reader loses the hinge that lets bodily healing point beyond itself. Naming this in a study Bible or margin, rather than resolving it silently, would preserve for the reader what the vocabulary is doing.

*I do not press these to a verdict. The field's movements have been described; what healing finally is, ontologically, is left open for the wider study to weigh against the rest of the corpus.*
