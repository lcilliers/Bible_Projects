## ISA-vineyard · Isa 5:1–7 · [D, multi] — love's disappointed labour; justice sought, bloodshed found

**The reading.** A love song that turns to grief: "**My beloved had a vineyard on a very fertile hill. He dug it and cleared it of stones, and planted it with choice vines… and he looked for it to yield grapes, but it yielded wild grapes**" (vv.1–2). The owner appeals: "**What more was there to do for my vineyard that I have not done?**" (v.4). Then the key is given: "**he looked for justice, but behold, bloodshed; for righteousness, but behold, an outcry!**" (v.7). The finding is **a *tended* love met by *wild* fruit — every care given, and the return a bitter contradiction of what was sought (justice → bloodshed; righteousness → outcry)** *(stated)*. The operation is **the interior figured from *God's* side — the ache of love that has done everything and been answered by the opposite; the sin is not merely failure but the *perversion* of the very good that was cultivated** *(inferred: the parable's inner-being weight sits in the owner's *disappointed expectation* — "he **looked for** (qavah — waited, hoped) grapes… **looked for** justice" (vv.2,4,7): the reading exposes God as one who *hopes* over his people and can be grieved, "**what more was there to do?**" (v.4) — a wounded lament, not cold verdict; and the specific horror is the *wordplay of perversion* — mishpat (justice) → mispach (bloodshed), tsedaqah (righteousness) → tse'aqah (outcry): the fruit is not just absent but *inverted*, the near-identical sound marking how narrowly-yet-totally the good has curdled into its opposite; the finding is that a heart lavishly tended by God and yielding *wild grapes* is a peculiarly grievous thing — the failure of *response* to sustained love, and the turning of cultivated ground to the very cry it should have prevented)*.

**Characteristics surfaced:** *love's disappointed hope (God who "looks for" fruit and grieves, "what more was there to do?")* (new — a gem: God as one who hopes over his people and can be grieved); *the perversion of cultivated good (justice→bloodshed, righteousness→outcry — the good curdled into its near-opposite)* (thread — sin as inversion, w/ 5:20); *wild fruit from tended ground (the failure to respond to sustained care)* (thread — unresponsiveness to love, w/ Isa 1:2-3).

## ISA-woes · Isa 5:8–30 · [D, multi] — the six woes: the grammar of a self-justifying, value-inverted heart

**The reading.** Six "woes" dissect the corrupt interior. (1) Insatiable acquisition — "**woe to those who join house to house, who add field to field, until there is no more room**" (v.8). (2) Appetite that inflames itself — those who "**run after strong drink**… but they do not regard the deeds of the LORD" (vv.11–12). (3) Defiant sin that dares God — "**woe to those who draw iniquity with cords of falsehood**… who say, 'Let him be quick, let him speed his work, that we may see it'" (vv.18–19). (4) The inversion of all value — "**woe to those who call evil good and good evil, who put darkness for light and light for darkness**" (v.20). (5) Self-conceit — "**woe to those who are wise in their own eyes, and shrewd in their own sight**" (v.21). (6) Heroism bent to corruption — "heroes at drinking wine… who **acquit the guilty for a bribe**" (vv.22–23). The finding is **a catalogue of the *self-justifying, value-inverted* interior — greed without limit, appetite that drowns perception, sin grown insolent, the deliberate confusion of good and evil, and the pride of self-wisdom** *(stated)*. The operation is **six angles on one disorder: the self as its *own* measure — acquiring without limit, judging by its own eyes, redefining good and evil to suit itself, until perception itself is corrupted ("they do not regard the deeds of the LORD", "wise in their own eyes")** *(inferred: read as a set, the woes trace how the autonomous self *corrupts its own faculties* — acquisitiveness (v.8) crowds out everyone else ("made to dwell alone"); drink and revelry (vv.11–12) *dull the God-perceiving faculty* ("they do not regard the deeds of the LORD… go into exile for lack of **knowledge**", v.13); insolence (vv.18–19) mocks God to hurry his judgment; then the two deepest — v.20, "**call evil good and good evil**", the *deliberate reversal of the moral compass itself* (not weakness but a re-labelling of reality), and v.21, "**wise in their own eyes**", the root: a self that is its own final authority; the finding is that these are not six unrelated vices but the anatomy of *autonomy* — the interior that has made itself the measure has thereby corrupted appetite, perception, and moral judgment together, and the darkness at v.30 ("if one looks to the land, behold, darkness") is the outer image of a self that put "darkness for light" within)*.

**Characteristics surfaced:** *insatiable acquisition (joining house to house until one dwells alone)* (thread — greed that isolates, w/ Ecc, Hab 2:9); *appetite that dulls God-perception ("they do not regard the deeds of the LORD")* (new — pleasure drowning the faculty that sees God); *sin grown insolent (daring God to hurry his work)* (new — mockery as advanced corruption); *the inversion of the moral compass ("call evil good and good evil")* (thread — a gem: sin as deliberate re-labelling of reality, w/ 5:7, Amos 5:7); *self-wisdom as the root ("wise in their own eyes")* (thread — a gem: the autonomous self as its own final authority, w/ Pro 3:7, 26:12); *the corruption of the faculties together (appetite, perception, moral judgment)* (new — autonomy anatomised).

## Chapter-level notes
- **Surfaced:** love's disappointed hope; the perversion of cultivated good; the six woes; insatiable acquisition; appetite dulling God-perception; insolent sin; the inversion of good and evil; self-wisdom as root. Chapter 5 pairs the *grieved lover* (God who looked for grapes) with the *anatomy of autonomy* (the self as its own measure) — the wild grapes of vv.1–7 named vice by vice in vv.8–30.
- **Threads:** God who hopes/grieves over his people (→ 63:9-10); the inversion of good/evil (→ 29:16, the potter/clay reversal); self-wisdom (→ 10:13, Assyria's "by my wisdom"); darkness within becoming darkness without (→ 8:22, 9:2).
