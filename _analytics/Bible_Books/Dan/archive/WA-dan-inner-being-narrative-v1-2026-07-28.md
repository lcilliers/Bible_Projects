# How a Person's Inner Life Moves, in the Book of Daniel

This is not a summary of what happens in the book of Daniel. It is an account of something narrower and stranger: what the book actually shows — and refuses to show — about what goes on inside a person. What moves someone to act. What overwhelms them. What restores them. What they say about their own hearts, or what other people say about their hearts. And, just as often, where the text simply stops, mid-scene, and tells you nothing at all about what a person facing the worst moment of their life was thinking or feeling.

The book covers roughly seventy years, four kings, one exile, and a series of visions that reach centuries beyond the story's own present. It is not one story with one moral. It is a set of scenes, and what they show about the inner life of the people in them doesn't add up to a single lesson — it adds up to a set of threads, some of which tie off cleanly and some of which never do. What follows takes them one at a time.

---

## Hearts lifted up: the shape pride takes, and how it ends

The clearest single thread running through the whole book is what happens inside a person once they start to think of themselves as the reason for their own greatness — and what happens to them afterward.

It surfaces first, faintly, in the opening chapter: Nebuchadnezzar besieges Jerusalem, and the text assumes there is some resolve or hostility driving him, though it never says what it is — the siege is simply there, its interior unspecified. A little later he takes the temple's gold vessels and deposits them in the treasury of his own god, and again the text won't say whether this was worship, trophy-taking, or simple administration. These early instances are all guesswork built on bare acts.

The turn becomes explicit and named only in chapter 4, when Nebuchadnezzar, standing on his palace roof and looking out over the city he built, says: "Is not this great Babylon, which I have built by my mighty power as a royal residence and for the glory of my majesty?" Every clause is "I," "my," "my." It is the most concentrated act of self-credit anywhere in the book, and it comes immediately after he has already, once, acknowledged that "the Most High God" had done everything for him — so the boast isn't ignorance, it's a reversal. While the words are still in his mouth, a voice interrupts from heaven and tells him the kingdom has left him. He is driven out, reduced to eating grass like an ox, until — as the text puts it twice — he comes to know that "the Most High rules the kingdom of men and gives it to whom he will." When his reason at last returns, he lifts his eyes to heaven and blesses God, in almost the exact words he used before his fall.

Daniel, retelling this same story to Belshazzar a generation later, uses a sharper phrase for what went wrong in Nebuchadnezzar: "his heart was lifted up." Then Daniel turns the same accusation directly on Belshazzar himself — "you have not humbled your heart, though you knew all this" — and adds a distinction the text draws quite deliberately: Nebuchadnezzar sinned without precedent to warn him; Belshazzar sinned with his own father's story sitting right there as evidence, and did it anyway. That same night, a hand writes on the wall, and by morning Belshazzar is dead and his kingdom given to others.

The pattern recurs a third time in Daniel's own vision of the ram and the goat, where a rising power is described in language that could have come straight out of chapter 4: "in his own mind he shall become great." And a fourth time, at greater length, in the long vision of chapter 11, where a king's heart is "stirred up" against his enemies, then "exalted," then two kings' hearts together are said to be "bent on doing evil" while they lie to each other at the same table, then a king's heart is "set against the holy covenant," and finally a king "exalts himself and magnifies himself above every god" and speaks "astonishing things against the God of gods." Each time the pattern is named again, almost point for point: the heart turns inward and upward, and every time, without exception, something outside the person breaks it back down. Whether that final undoing is described as a hand, a voice, a sealed decree, or simply "he shall be broken — but by no human hand," the text is consistent that it never comes from within the proud figure himself.

What the text never does, in any of these four cases, is explain the moment the turn happens. Nebuchadnezzar's "heart was lifted up" is described in a passive construction — it happened to him as much as by him. Nobody narrates the instant pride takes hold; the reader only sees its result, spoken aloud, and then its answer.

## What terror looks like on a person, and who doesn't have it

The book is unusually willing to describe fear as a physical event, not just a feeling.

Nebuchadnezzar's first crisis is described this way: "his spirit was troubled, and his sleep left him." A disturbance inside him produces a bodily consequence — he cannot sleep — before any threat, decree, or action follows from it. Belshazzar's fear, decades later, is described far more completely: "the king's color changed, and his thoughts alarmed him; his limbs gave way, and his knees knocked together." Four things happen to him in sequence — his face, his mind, his limbs, his knees — moving from the visible surface of the body inward to his thoughts and back outward to something involuntary. When the assembled experts can't help him, the exact same description repeats, intensified: "greatly alarmed." His officials, watching, are given their own separate word — not fear, but bewilderment — a detail the text bothers to distinguish rather than lumping everyone's reaction together.

The same wording resurfaces in an unexpected place. Years later, Daniel has his own vision, and afterward: "my thoughts greatly alarmed me, and my color changed." It is the identical phrase used of Belshazzar's terror, now applied to the man who has spent the whole book calmly interpreting other people's nightmares. For once, the interpreter is shown feeling exactly what everyone he has ever interpreted for felt. A vision later, an angelic figure — Gabriel, by name, the first heavenly being in the book to be named at all — approaches Daniel directly, and Daniel is "frightened and fell on my face," then falls into an unconscious, face-down sleep. This is a more immediate, physical collapse than anything shown of him earlier. And in the very next vision he receives, the aftermath is worse again: he lies "sick for some days," a level of physical toll the book gives to no other figure, before he can even resume his ordinary duties — and even then he says plainly that he was "appalled by the vision and did not understand it," despite having just been given a detailed explanation of it.

Set against all of this is one deliberate absence. When Daniel learns that a new law makes prayer to any god but the king punishable by death, the text says only that he went to his upper room, opened the same windows he always opened, knelt in the same posture, three times a day, "as he had done previously" — and prayed. No fear-word appears anywhere in the verse. Every other person in the book who faces a serious threat is given some stated inner reaction — dismay, dread, trembling. Daniel, facing a den of lions with his eyes open, is given none. It is the one moment in the whole book where a person under direct, known, mortal threat shows no fear at all, and the contrast is total enough that it reads as deliberate, even though the text never comments on it.

The three men thrown into the furnace get something similar but not quite the same. Before being bound and cast in, they tell the king plainly: "our God whom we serve is able to deliver us... but if not, be it known to you, O king, that we will not serve your gods." Their resolve is stated in their own words, ahead of time. But the text never says what they felt in the actual moment of being bound and thrown into the fire — that specific instant sits in a gap between their stated resolve beforehand and their calm reappearance afterward, unaddressed.

## Wanting to know, and finally admitting you don't

Nebuchadnezzar's very first crisis in the book is a mind that will not settle until it knows something: "my spirit is troubled to know the dream." His experts cannot give him the answer, and his fury at their failure nearly gets an entire class of people killed. Daniel, brought the same demand under the same death sentence hanging over him, responds completely differently: he asks a calm question — "why is the decree of the king so urgent?" — devises a plan, and waits. Two men facing the identical problem, driven by the same need to know, moving from two entirely different inward places: one agitated, one composed.

That contrast doesn't resolve into a simple moral about calm versus panic, because later in the book Daniel is shown wanting to understand just as urgently as any king ever did, and not getting an answer either. After his first great vision he writes it down, but adds: "my spirit within me was anxious, and the visions of my head alarmed me" — the same anxious hunger he once watched in someone else, now his own. He seeks out an attendant and asks directly for the meaning, then presses further, asking a second, more specific question about the one detail that disturbed him most. He gets an answer. But in the very next vision, even after Gabriel personally arrives, touches him, restores his strength, and gives him a detailed, historically specific explanation naming actual kingdoms by name — Media, Persia, Greece — Daniel still closes the chapter by saying: "I was appalled by the vision and did not understand it."

This isn't a contradiction the text tries to smooth over. It surfaces again at the very end of the book. In the last chapter, after hearing a solemn oath about how long a coming crisis will last, Daniel says, as plainly as anyone says anything in this book: "I heard, but I did not understand." He asks what the outcome of it all will be, and is told, in effect, to let it go — "the words are shut up and sealed until the time of the end." The book's very last statement about understanding draws a hard line: "none of the wicked shall understand, but those who are wise shall understand." Daniel, who has spent the whole book being the one other people come to for understanding, ends it having just admitted, in his own voice, that this time it was withheld from him too.

In between, the same word — understanding — gets used for something much darker. In the long political vision of chapter 11, a king "understands riddles," and later "pays attention" to those who abandon their faith while paying no attention at all to the gods of his own ancestors. The same capacity that let Daniel see what others couldn't is shown, in someone else, put entirely in the service of cunning and contempt. The text doesn't explain why the same word can mean both; it simply uses it both ways, verses apart, and leaves the reader to notice.

## Where God reaches: outcomes, thoughts, or the will itself

A question opens in the very first chapter and never entirely closes: when the text says the Lord "gave" someone into another person's power, does that reach only what happens to them — their circumstances, their outcomes — or does it reach the person's own inward disposition too?

The clearest attempt at an answer comes in the second chapter, when Daniel tells Nebuchadnezzar, at length, everything God has given him: "the kingdom, the power, and the might, and the glory," plus dominion over every living thing. It is the single most generous statement of divine giving toward Nebuchadnezzar anywhere in the book — and every single item in the list is something you could point to, not something you could feel. Rule, strength of office, honor, scope of authority. Nothing on the list is a disposition, a resolve, a character trait. It's the strongest evidence in the whole book for the idea that God's giving to this king was entirely a matter of circumstance, not of what was going on inside him — which would help explain why nothing in the text ever attributes the interior of Nebuchadnezzar's later pride, or his earlier hostility toward Jerusalem, to God at all. Those seem to be his own.

But the book doesn't let that reading settle either. In the very same speech, Daniel also tells the king that God has revealed to him — directly, personally — what his own dream's strange images meant, and that "thoughts... came to you as you lay in bed." That's a third thing again: not circumstance, and not quite disposition either, but the actual content of a private thought, given to a foreign king who has done nothing to deserve it and shows no particular faith because of it. So by chapter 2 the book has already shown God reaching a person's circumstances, a person's private thoughts, and — separately, for Daniel and his friends — a person's favor and gifts of wisdom, all without ever quite saying whether the deepest layer, a person's own resolve or character, is ever touched from outside at all.

The sharpest test of this question comes in the furnace. Three men are ordered to worship a golden image on pain of death, and they refuse — stating plainly that their god might not save them, and that they will refuse regardless. Nothing in that moment is circumstantial; it is disposition and nothing else, stated by the three in their own voice, held apart from any promise of rescue. And then, once they walk out of the fire unburned, Nebuchadnezzar himself — the very king who ordered the furnace — says something the book has not said quite this directly before: God "sent his angel and delivered his servants, who trusted in him." For the first time, a rescue is tied explicitly, by an eyewitness's own testimony, to what those three people actually believed, not merely to what happened to them. It's the strongest single piece of evidence anywhere in the book that a person's own resolve can be exactly what God is shown honoring — though the book never turns this into a general rule, and elsewhere — Nebuchadnezzar's own pride, or the crowd of officials who comply with the image-decree for reasons never stated — it stays silent on whether the same thing is true in the other direction.

## The people the story doesn't ask about

Some of the most striking things in this book are the places where a person is standing in the middle of the worst moment of their life, and the text simply moves past them without a word about what it was like.

Early on, Jehoiakim, king of Judah, is delivered into Nebuchadnezzar's hand — and that's it. He never speaks, never reacts, never appears again in the surrounding chapters. What being conquered felt like to him is not recorded, not even as a rumor.

A chief official named Ashpenaz is told explicitly, in his own words, that letting Daniel skip the king's food could cost him his head. Chapters later, the moment finally arrives when Daniel and his three friends are brought before the king to be judged — the exact moment Ashpenaz's stated fear would be either realized or relieved — and the text gives him nothing. It moves straight to the king's own reaction. Whatever relief or dread Ashpenaz felt at that instant is simply not there.

The starkest case in the early chapters is a group of court advisers condemned to death because they couldn't interpret a king's dream. Three verses earlier, this same group had spoken at length, defending themselves, reasoning with the king. Then the order goes out to kill them, and the text tells us only that "the wise men were about to be killed" and that the executioners went looking for Daniel too — nothing about what it was like, for people who had just been arguing their own case, to learn they were about to die for someone else's failure.

The same silence returns, more troublingly, in the den of lions. After Daniel is pulled out unharmed, the king orders the men who accused him thrown in — "they, their children, and their wives." The lions kill them before they even reach the bottom of the den. No reason is ever given anywhere for why the children and wives were included in the sentence at all; it is not paralleled anywhere else in the book, and the text offers nothing to explain it.

And when the king's own soldiers are ordered to cast the three men into that earlier furnace, the flames — heated seven times hotter than usual on the king's own furious order — kill the soldiers who did the throwing, before the three men Daniel's friends themselves are shown surviving inside it. The text records the soldiers' deaths as a bare fact and then never mentions them again. No one asks what became of them or what it was like.

There is also a quieter version of this same silence, one that isn't about death at all. When Nebuchadnezzar, astonished at the sight of four men walking unburned in the furnace, later falls on his face and orders offerings brought to Daniel — an act of what looks like worship — the text never says how Daniel felt about it, whether he accepted the honor, redirected it, or said nothing. And a chapter later, when Daniel is given a purple robe, a gold chain, and made third ruler in the kingdom — this on the very night a hand has just written on the wall that the kingdom itself is ending — Belshazzar goes ahead and gives Daniel the reward anyway, and nothing in the text says what was going through the king's mind as he did it, moments after hearing his own end pronounced.

Even Daniel's own renaming, at the very start of the book, follows this pattern. He and his three friends are given new Babylonian names by the chief eunuch, and nowhere — not there, not when Nebuchadnezzar recalls it years later, not when the queen recalls it to Belshazzar still later — does anyone ever say how the four of them felt about losing their names. The closest thing to an answer the text ever gives is behavioral, not stated: by the time Daniel is speaking to Belshazzar, his friends are simply called Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego without any further comment, as though the names had, by then, become just what they were called.

## The body as a place where the inner life shows through

Several times, the book lets a person's face, limbs, or strength carry information their words don't give.

Nebuchadnezzar's sleeplessness at the very start of the book is the first instance — no words are given for his inner disturbance, only its physical result. Later, mid-rage at the three men's defiance, "the expression of his face was changed" — and the word used for "expression" there is, in the underlying language, the very same word used throughout that chapter for the golden "image" he built. It's not something the text draws attention to or claims as deliberate wordplay, but it sits there: the king's own face becomes, in that moment, an image altered by fury, right as his golden image is being refused worship.

Belshazzar's collapse — color, thoughts, limbs, knees — is the fullest such description anywhere in the book, and it happens twice in nine verses, worse the second time. Daniel's own physical toll, across his later visions, escalates chapter by chapter: alarm and a changed complexion after the first vision; being physically overwhelmed, prostrate, and unconscious after the second; and then, after a vision still later, days of literal sickness. And in his final vision, the description is the most complete of all — no strength left in him at all, his appearance "fearfully changed," and then total collapse into a mute, unconscious heap, face to the ground, from which he only slowly recovers.

What recovers him is touch. A hand touches him and sets him trembling. A figure touches his lips, and he can speak again. A third touch — the text doesn't say for certain whether it's the same figure each time — leaves him strengthened enough to keep listening, and Daniel says so himself, in his own words: "for you have strengthened me." The book doesn't explain who is doing the touching in each case, only that each touch produces a specific, named change in him.

## Being told you are loved, in the middle of being afraid

There's a small phrase that appears three times in the last four chapters of the book, always spoken to Daniel by name, always in the same breath as a command or a comfort: "greatly loved." Gabriel says it first, right as he arrives to explain a vision Daniel has just spent a chapter confessing his own sin over. It's said again twice more in the very next vision, once right after Daniel has been left trembling on his hands and knees by an unnamed touch, and once again just after he's been physically restored. Each time, the phrase arrives paired with an instruction — understand, stand up, do not fear — as if the value placed on Daniel is itself part of what steadies him enough to keep listening. The text never explains what makes him "greatly loved" beyond what it has already shown: that from the first day he set his heart to understand and humbled himself, his words were heard.

## Worship: given, demanded, refused, and never quite explained

Worship moves through this book in an unsettled way, changing direction without ever being fully accounted for.

After Nebuchadnezzar sees the four men unharmed in the furnace, he not only praises their God but earlier, and separately, in an episode two chapters before that, he had already fallen on his face before Daniel and ordered an offering brought to him — a human being, not God. It's never made clear in the text whether that homage was meant for Daniel personally, for Daniel's God as revealed through him, or for the whole overwhelming event at once; all three readings are left standing, none settled. Then, one chapter later, that exact same word for worship reappears — now describing what an entire empire is ordered, on pain of death, to render to a golden image Nebuchadnezzar himself has built. The very word once used for Nebuchadnezzar's own worship of Daniel becomes the word for the worship he now demands of everyone else, toward an image of his own making. Whether that's meant as a pointed reversal or simply the ordinary word for bowing down, reused without special weight, the text never says — but the two scenes sit only a few verses apart from each other.

Everyone complies with the decree except three men, who refuse and are thrown into the fire for it. What the text never resolves is what that mass compliance actually was — habit, fear, indifference, or genuine belief in the image — because the three who didn't comply are the only data point given for what an alternative even looked like, and even they only show what refusal looks like, not what compliance meant to everyone else.

A generation later, a different king, Darius, moves through his own version of this question in stages the text lets you watch happen in real time. He first calls the God who might save Daniel simply "your God" — a hope, not yet a claim. Once Daniel is pulled from the den alive, Darius calls him "servant of the Most High God." By the time he writes his own decree to the empire, he calls him "the God of Daniel" — a personal, testified name, not a title borrowed from someone else's report. It's a different route to conviction than either Belshazzar's, who only ever says "I have heard" about Daniel's God, secondhand and untested, or Nebuchadnezzar's, who arrives at his own conviction only after living through the judgment himself.

## Flattery: a thing done to people, never a thing felt by the one doing it

Deception recurs across the book, almost always described from the outside — as a method used on someone else — and never as something the person doing the deceiving is shown feeling from within.

A steward reporting Daniel's discovery to the king reframes it, without quite lying, as his own finding rather than Daniel's — "I have found among the exiles a man..." — and the text lets the self-interest sit there as a possible reading, without confirming it. In the long political vision toward the end of the book, one ruler after another is said to obtain a kingdom "by flatteries," to "seduce with flattery" people who have already abandoned their own faith, to be joined by others "with flattery." Every single time, flattery is described as something done to somebody else — never as a feeling or intention disclosed from inside the person doing it. The text is willing to tell you a king's heart was "bent on doing evil," or that two kings sat at the same table "speaking lies" to each other's faces — but the specific mechanism of flattery itself stays external, a method named by its effect on its target rather than by anything felt by the one using it.

## Turning the face: the same gesture, toward devotion and toward conquest

One small phrase does two very different kinds of work in this book. When Daniel begins to pray after reading Jeremiah's prophecy, the text says he "turned his face" toward God — a phrase built from the idea of deliberately orienting yourself toward something. Chapters later, in the long vision about kings and wars, an invading ruler is described "setting his face" to invade with the strength of his whole kingdom, then turning his face toward the coastlands to conquer them, then turning his face back home in eventual defeat. It's the same idiom, applied three times to a campaign of conquest, that had earlier been used once for a man kneeling by an open window. The text never remarks on the overlap. It just uses the same words for two people orienting their whole selves toward two entirely different things.

## Confession that never gives a name

Daniel's long prayer in the ninth chapter is the most sustained first-person interior passage in the whole book, and it is built almost entirely out of collective language — "we have sinned," "we have not listened," "our kings, our princes, our fathers" — repeated across more than a dozen verses, in stacked and overlapping terms for wrongdoing: sinned, done wrong, acted wickedly, rebelled, turned aside, not listened, not obeyed, dealt treacherously. Nowhere in that entire confession does the prayer single out one king, one official, one ordinary person, and say: this is what they, specifically, did or felt. The failure belongs to everyone and to no one in particular.

The one exception is a single clause, easy to miss, in the verse right after the prayer ends, where the narration recaps what Daniel had just been doing and specifies that he was "confessing my sin and the sin of my people Israel" — two things, named separately, where the prayer itself had only ever said "we." It's the one place in the whole passage where Daniel quietly steps out from the collective "we" and names his own sin as something distinct from everyone else's, though even there he doesn't say what it was.

The prayer closes by explicitly refusing one kind of claim: "we do not present our pleas before you because of our righteousness, but because of your great mercy." And it uses, in petition, the very same word for "hearing" that the confession had just used, again and again, to describe the nation's own failure to listen — Daniel asking God to do, toward them, the one thing they are confessing they never did toward God.

## What is given, what is taken away, and for how long

Across the whole book, when something is stripped from someone as judgment, it is almost never stripped forever. Nebuchadnezzar's kingdom is taken from him for "seven periods of time" — bounded, not permanent — because a stump was deliberately left in the ground when the rest of the tree was cut down, a detail the text later explains was left there on purpose, as the very thing that makes his eventual restoration possible. In the vision of the four beasts, the first three lose their power but are explicitly allowed to go on living "for a season and a time"; only the fourth, the strangest and most violent of them, is destroyed outright. A hostile power's reach is limited to "a time, times, and half a time." A sanctuary's desecration is bounded at "2,300 evenings and mornings," after which it is restored.

Only one thing in the whole book is ever described as unbounded on purpose, twice over, in the same breath: the kingdom finally given to "one like a son of man," and, in nearly identical language a few verses later, to "the people of the saints of the Most High" — a kingdom that "shall not pass away" and "shall not be destroyed." Whether that individual figure and that collective people are the same thing described twice, or two different things standing side by side, the text never says. But everywhere else, judgment comes with a built-in expiration date; only this, at the very top of the book's vision of history, is ever said to have none.

---

## Enduring, being refined, and waiting for something that hasn't come yet

Late in the book, a strange thing is said about suffering: that some of the wise "shall stumble, so that they may be refined, purified, and made white, until the time of the end." Their setbacks are not just endured — they are given a stated purpose, a reason attached to them, which is unusual in a book that elsewhere leaves so much suffering entirely unexplained. That same language returns in the very last chapter, generalized now to "many," no longer only to the wise specifically: "many shall purify themselves and make themselves white and be refined."

And the book's very last words are not an answer to any of its open questions, but a blessing on the act of waiting itself — "blessed is he who waits" — followed by a private, personal promise to Daniel, and to no one else: "you shall rest, and shall stand in your allotted place at the end of the days." After chapters of visions he could not fully understand, a vision he chose to keep to himself, an illness that laid him low for days, and an admission, twice, in his own words, that understanding had not been given to him this time — the book closes not by finally explaining everything to him, but by promising him rest.

---

## What stays open

Not everything above resolves. Whether God's reach into a person ever goes as deep as their own will and character, or stops at their circumstances, is tested from several directions across the book and never settled one way. Whether the crowd who bowed to the golden image out of fear felt anything like devotion is never asked in a way the text answers. Why the children and wives of the conspirators were thrown to the lions alongside them is never explained anywhere. What Daniel felt about being renamed, being worshipped, being promoted the night his king's kingdom ended — none of it is given. And Daniel himself, who spends most of the book as the one other people turn to for understanding, ends it having said, more than once and in his own words, that this time he did not understand at all — only that he was loved, and that rest was coming.
