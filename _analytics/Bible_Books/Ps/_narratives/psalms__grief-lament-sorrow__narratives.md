**groaning** — Psa 5:1  ·  `H1901:groan#1`

_Story_

The psalm begins not with polished words but with a groan. Before he can shape a single request, the man is simply making a wordless sound of distress, and his first act is to ask God to pay attention to that sound rather than to any argument. The groan rises straight out of him - nothing outside is blamed for it, and it is not felt in any one part of the body but is the whole self aching. He does not throw it out at random; he turns it deliberately toward God and asks to be understood beneath his words. Nothing here rates how loud or how strong it is, and no result is recorded - the point is only that the deepest part of a person can speak to God in a groan before it can speak in sentences.

**weeping** — Psa 6:6  ·  `H1832:weep#1`

_Story_

Every night the man cries until his bed is soaked and his couch is drenched. This is grief that has become physical - tears pouring out in the dark hours, night after night, until the bedding itself is wet through. Nothing outside is named as the cause; the weeping simply overflows from the whole of him, not from any single part. The account is careful to mark this off from mere tiredness: this is the active flood of sorrow, the visible measure of how much he is hurting. It does not put a number on the tears, but the drenched bed says enough - a person coming apart quietly, alone, every night.

**groans** — Psa 12:5  ·  `H0603:groan#1`

_Story_

Here the groan belongs not to the singer but to the poor and the plundered - the people who have been robbed and have nothing left but the sound of their suffering. Their groan is not weak: it reaches God and moves him to stand up and act. It is a wordless cry, rising from the whole of crushed people, and it is exactly this sound that God answers. Nothing measures how loud it is, and the account keeps the rescue tied to the cry itself rather than treating it as a separate outcome. The lesson is stark and hopeful at once: the bare groan of the helpless is enough to make God rise.

Weeping, seen this way, is bound to the morning that ends it.
