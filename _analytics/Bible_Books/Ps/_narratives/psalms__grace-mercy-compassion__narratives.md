**generous** — Psa 37:21  ·  `H2603:generou#1`

_Story_

In a psalm that holds two kinds of people side by side, this moment catches the good man with his hand open: he is generous and he gives. It is not a passing feeling but a settled choice of the will. The wicked man in the same lines borrows and never pays back, always grasping; the righteous man simply gives, and the giving is the generosity — the two are one thing. The text does not say what part of him this comes from, how strongly he feels it, or what it leads to next; it does not even say where inside him it lives, only that it is the whole man. What stands out, plainly, is a freedom to give that the tight-fisted never have — and that freedom is the mark of a good inner life.

_Reading_

Set inside a psalm that contrasts two interiors, this reading catches the righteous man precisely at his open hand: 'the righteous is generous and gives' (101 sense). It is classed not as feeling but as volition (102 type) — an act of the will, chosen giving, not a mood. The bearer is the righteous man himself (105), and the operation names the contrast directly: the wicked borrows and does not repay, but the righteous is generous and gives, open-handedness read as a mark of the righteous interior (106). Its target is generosity itself (107) and its coupling is the welded pair generous-and-gives (112) — disposition and deed fused so that the giving is the generosity, not a separate consequence of it. The discovery (114) sharpens the point: the righteous interior is marked by a freedom to give that the grasping wicked lacks. Read as a characteristic (115), it is located wholly within the man as an interior state (116 internal:ib-state). The silences are telling and must be read: no seat is recorded (104 none) — the generosity is not lodged in any bodily organ, it is simply a state of the whole man; no manner is given (108 none) — the text does not colour how he gives, only that he does; and source (103), intensity (109), specifier (110), effect (111) and prohibition (113) are all absent — scripture does not trace where this open-handedness rises from, does not grade it, does not narrow it, records no downstream result, and does not fence it as forbidden. What is left is bare and clean: the will to give, welded to the giving, standing as the visible signature of a righteous inner life against the closed fist of the wicked.

**grace** — Psa 45:2  ·  `H2580:grac#1`

_Story_

In this royal wedding poem, grace is not something the king does but something he carries: a charm and winning favour that rests on his very words — grace is 'poured upon his lips.' It is a quality he has, a bearing, not an act reaching out to anyone. Notice what the poem does not say: this grace goes out to no one and accomplishes nothing here; it simply adorns him, and God has blessed him for it. It sits in no single part of him, has no measured strength, and leads to nothing further that is named. What we are left with is grace as pure attractiveness — favour and charm resting on a man — before it is ever a kindness shown to someone else.

_Reading_

In the royal wedding song this reading finds grace not as a deed but as a bearing: 'grace (chen) is poured upon your lips' (101 sense) — favour, charm, the winsomeness of the king's speech. It is classed as status (102 type), a standing quality of the person rather than an action performed, and this classification governs the whole reading. The bearer is the king (105). Strikingly, the two verbs of process go silent: operation is none (106) and target is none (107) — grace here does not move out toward anyone or do anything; it rests on him as an attribute. What is recorded instead is its manner: poured on his lips (108) — the one positive descriptor, locating the grace in his gracious, winsome speech. The coupling ties it to consequence — the graciousness for which God blessed him (112, a spanned pair) — yet the blessing is read as the qualifier, not the characteristic itself. The discovery (114) confirms the gracious character expressed in his words. It is a characteristic (115), an interior state (116 internal:ib-state). The absences carry weight: no seat (104 none) — the charm is not organ-lodged; and with operation and target both 'none', grace here is emphatically not an outgoing act of mercy but a possessed quality, a winsomeness that simply adorns the man. Source (103), intensity (109), specifier (110), effect (111) and prohibition (113) are all absent — no origin, degree, narrowing, result or fence is given. The finding is that within this family grace can appear as sheer attractive standing, favour resting on a person, before it is ever a thing done to another.

## Psa 55:1-5

> To the choirmaster : with stringed instruments . A Maskil of David . Psa 55:1 Give ear to my prayer , O God , and hide not yourself from my plea for mercy ! **mercy** — Psa 55:1  ·  `H8467:mercy#1`

_Story_

In this reading mercy is not something given but something desperately asked for. The psalmist, surrounded by dread and inner anguish, cries to God: do not hide from my plea. This plea is the very heart of his prayer — a humbled cry that claims nothing as owed and asks only for undeserved kindness. It is aimed entirely at God, reaching out beyond himself. The text does not say where in him it starts, how loud it is, or what it achieves; it simply shows a person in trouble turning upward, empty-handed, and begging for grace he cannot demand. Where grace can be a charm a man possesses, here it is a favour he lacks and must ask for.

_Reading_

Here mercy is not shown but sought: the psalmist begs God, 'hide not yourself from my plea for mercy' — a plea for grace, a supplication (101 sense, techinnah). It is classed as action (102 type): this mercy-word names the humbled cry itself, a thing the interior does. The bearer is the psalmist (105); the operation is to plead for favour (106); the target is God (107). The coupling is telling — twinned with the prayer (112, a spanned pair): the plea for mercy and the prayer are bound together, the supplication the inner posture beneath the outward prayer. The discovery (114) reads it exactly so: techinnah is the humbled cry that expects nothing by right, only mercy — the posture beneath the prayer. Its locus is external:god (116): unlike the possessed graciousness of the king, this movement reaches outside the self toward God as its whole horizon. The silences frame the humbling: no seat (104 none) — the plea is not lodged in an organ but is the man's whole reaching; no manner (108 none) — the cry is not otherwise coloured; and source (103), intensity (109), specifier (110), effect (111), prohibition (113) all absent — no origin, degree, narrowing, outcome or fence is set down. Surrounded in the passage by moaning, anguish, terror and trembling, the mercy here is the interior turned upward in dependence: not a quality it holds but a favour it has none of by right and must beg.

**pity** — Psa 72:13  ·  `H2347:pity#1`

_Story_

In this song about the ideal king, his greatness is measured not by his power but by his tenderness: he has pity on the weak and the needy. This pity is not a fleeting feeling; it is bound up with actually rescuing them and saving their lives — the mercy inside him is what drives the saving he does. It is a settled part of who he is as king. The text does not say where the feeling comes from, how intense it is, or track any separate result; it simply shows that real royal greatness bends toward the lowest and most helpless. The inside of good power, here, is compassion aimed at those who have no strength to claim it.

_Reading_

In the psalm of the ideal king, pity is the inward face of just rule: 'he has pity (chus) on the weak and the needy' (101 sense). It is classed as status (102 type) — a settled quality of the king's person, kingship marked by pity rather than a single passing act. The bearer is the king (105); the operation is to have pity or compassion (106). Two spanned pairs give the reading its shape: the target is the weak and needy (107, spanned), and the coupling binds the pity to saving their lives (112, spanned) — so the compassion is not a mere feeling but is welded to rescue, the inward mercy behind the king's saving acts. The discovery (114) names it as the tenderness of the just king, the compassion that spares and cares for the weak, kingship marked by pity, not merely power. It is a characteristic (115), located within as an interior state (116 internal:ib-state). The absences: no seat (104 none) — the pity is not organ-lodged but a state of the ruler; no manner (108 none) — the tenderness is not otherwise qualified; and source (103), intensity (109), specifier (110), effect (111), prohibition (113) all absent — no traced origin, no degree, no narrowing, no separately recorded result, no fence. The finding is that power in this psalm is read from the inside out: the king's greatness is his pity, and the target is deliberately the lowest — the weak, the needy, the helpless — so that the mark of the interior is tenderness bent toward those with no claim of strength.

**pity** — Psa 102:14  ·  `H2603:pity#1`

_Story_

In this reading, pity is aimed at a ruined city. God's servants feel tenderness even for Zion's dust — they hold her broken stones dear and grieve over the rubble. It is a genuine leaning of the heart, not just an action, and it is knit together with that love for her stones. The text does not say where the feeling begins, how strong it is, or what it produces; it simply shows hearts moved by ruin. What is striking is that this compassion is stirred not by a suffering person but by a broken place — the caring heart can ache over wreckage and rubble, loving what is shattered simply because it matters to them.

_Reading_

Here pity turns toward a ruined place: God's servants 'have pity (chanan) on her dust' — tender regard for the very rubble of Zion (101 sense). It is classed as disposition (102 type), a leaning of the heart rather than a bare deed. The bearer is the servants of God (105); the operation is to have pity or affection (106); the target is Zion's dust (107). Uniquely for this word here, the recorded coupling field carries the phrase internal:ib-state (112) and the locus field carries 'paired with holding her stones dear' (116) — the two are cross-lodged, but together they say the same thing: the pity is an interior state, welded to the parallel line where the servants hold her very stones dear. The discovery (114) reads it as the tender regard of the servants for ruined Zion, love for her very dust. It is a characteristic (115). The absences frame the tenderness: no seat (104 none) — not organ-lodged; no manner (108 none) — uncoloured; source (103), intensity (109), specifier (110), effect (111), prohibition (113) all absent — no origin, degree, narrowing, result or fence. The finding worth marking is the target: pity here bends not toward a person but toward a place in ruins, even its dust and stones — the compassionate interior can be provoked by loss and rubble, loving what is broken because it is dear.

*Why this reading differs:* The two H2603:pity readings use the same verb (chanan) but run in opposite directions. Here (102:14) pity is present, active and warm — a disposition borne by God's servants toward ruined Zion, welded to holding her stones dear (an ascent). At 109:12 the same word appears only as a wished absence — 'nor any to pity his fatherless children' — with bearer 'no one', pity invoked precisely so that it be withheld (a descent). The difference in bearer (God's servants vs no one) and polarity (present tenderness vs curse-withheld mercy) is itself the finding: chanan can name both the heart that pities rubble and the heart that wills pity denied.

**shows compassion** — Psa 103:13  ·  `H7355:showscompassion#1`

_Story_

This reading looks at the human picture used to explain God's mercy: a father's tenderness toward his children. The point of the verse is God's compassion, but what is actually captured here is the father's — the deep, settled softness of a parent's heart for his own, set right alongside the way the Lord cares for those who revere him. The text does not say where this fatherly feeling comes from, how strong it runs, or what it leads to; it simply holds it up. And that is the striking thing: the most tender thing we know in ordinary life, a parent bending over a child, is taken as the yardstick that makes God's mercy understandable.

_Reading_

This reading fixes not on God's mercy directly but on the human image chosen to picture it: 'as a father shows compassion (racham) to his children' (101 sense). It is classed as disposition (102 type) — the settled tenderness of a parent's heart. The bearer named is a father (105) — the human father, whose pity is the analogy; the operation is to show compassion (106); the target is to his children (107). The recorded coupling field holds internal:ib-state (112) and the locus field holds 'paired with God's compassion on those who fear him' (116) — again cross-lodged, but together they mark it as an interior state set in parallel to the LORD's own compassion. The discovery (114) reads it precisely: the tender pity of a human father, the analogy for God's mercy. It is a characteristic (115). The inner-being lens matters here — though the verse's aim is God's mercy, the datum captured is the human father's compassion, the earthly interior scripture treats as the readable pattern of the divine. The absences: no seat (104 none) — the father's compassion is not organ-lodged; no manner (108 none) — uncoloured; source (103), intensity (109), specifier (110), effect (111), prohibition (113) all absent — no origin, degree, narrowing, separate result or fence. The finding is that the deepest human tenderness — a father over his children — is taken as the very measure by which God's compassion is made intelligible, the inner life of the parent standing as the known term in the comparison.

**pity** — Psa 109:12  ·  `H2603:pity#2`

_Story_

In this bitter psalm of cursing, pity shows up only so that it can be refused. The psalmist wishes that no one — not a single person — would ever show kindness to his enemy's orphaned children. The tender feeling is named on purpose, precisely to be shut off, tied to the kindness he also wants withheld. This is the harsh side of the human heart shown without excuse: not softness here but a wish for payback, that a merciless man should himself find no mercy for his own. The text gives no reason, no measure, no outcome — just the bare will that pity be denied. It shows that even the word for tenderness can be swung like a weapon.

_Reading_

In the great imprecatory psalm, pity appears only to be wished away: 'nor any to pity (chanan) his fatherless children' (101 sense). It is classed as disposition (102 type), the same tender leaning — but here named precisely so that it be denied. The bearer is 'no one' (105): the reading's spine is an absence deliberately willed, pity invoked as a thing that must find no one to carry it. The operation is pity (106); the target is the enemy's fatherless children (107). The recorded coupling holds internal:ib-state (112) and the locus holds 'paired with the kindness withheld' (116) — cross-lodged, but together binding this withheld pity to the withheld kindness of the same verse. The discovery (114) states it plainly: the wish that the pitiless find no pity for his own. This is the descent read at full weight (directive 5): the psalmist's interior here is not compassionate but retributive, and the data must be reported without softening — mercy is summoned as the exact thing to be refused to a man's children. It is still classed a characteristic (115). The absences sharpen it: no seat (104 none); no manner (108 none); source (103), intensity (109), specifier (110), effect (111), prohibition (113) all absent — the curse names no origin, degree, narrowing, result or fence, only the bare will that no pity come. The finding is that within this family the very word for tenderness can be turned as a weapon — the interior wishing that the merciless reap mercilessness, pity real enough to be pointedly denied.

*Why this reading differs:* This second H2603:pity reading inverts the first (102:14). Same verb chanan, same disposition-type, but the bearer collapses from God's servants to 'no one', and the polarity flips from present, warm regard for ruined Zion to a curse that pity be withheld from an enemy's children. The variation is the finding: the identical inner word runs as ascent (compassion given) at 102:14 and as descent (compassion willed absent) at 109:12 — how the tender interior can be turned into an instrument of retribution.

**kindness** — Psa 109:12  ·  `H2617:kindnes#1`

_Story_

Right next to the wish that no one pity his enemy's children, the psalmist adds: let no one show him kindness either. This is the strongest word for loyal, faithful love — and it appears here only so it can be shut off. He wants nobody at all to extend it. The refused kindness and the refused pity go together as one dark wish. It is worth noticing the difference between them: kindness here is the loyal, reaching hand of steady love, while the pity beside it is the softened heart — and he wants both, hand and heart, to reach his enemy from no one. No reason is given, no measure, no result — just the bare desire that kindness find no one willing to give it. Even the noblest form of love can be named inside a curse.

_Reading_

Beside the withheld pity of the same verse stands withheld kindness: 'Let there be none to extend kindness (chesed) to him' (101 sense). It is classed as disposition (102 type) — the settled loyal-love — but, like its neighbour, summoned only to be refused. The bearer is 'no one' (105): kindness is named as a thing the psalmist wills that nobody supply. The operation is to extend kindness (106); the target is the enemy (107). The recorded coupling holds internal:ib-state (112) and the locus holds 'paired with none to pity his children' (116) — cross-lodged, welding this refused chesed to the refused pity so that the two form a single imprecation. The discovery (114) names it: the wish that the merciless man find no mercy. This is chesed — the weightiest Hebrew word for steadfast covenant kindness — turned into the very thing the interior wants denied, and the data must stand uncomforted (directive 6). It is a characteristic (115). Preserve the distinction from the paired pity (directive 8): chesed here is loyal, extended kindness (the reaching hand of loyalty), pity is chanan (the softened heart) — the psalmist wills that both, hand and heart alike, come to his enemy from no one. The absences: no seat (104 none); no manner (108 none); source (103), intensity (109), specifier (110), effect (111), prohibition (113) all absent — only the naked wish that kindness find no bearer. The finding is that even steadfast covenant-kindness can be named inside a curse, invoked as the good the interior wants pointedly refused.

*Why this reading differs:* First of three H2617:kindnes readings, and the harshest. Here chesed is willed absent (bearer 'no one') toward an enemy — kindness named inside a curse. It differs from #2 (109:16), where the enemy himself is the one who failed to show kindness (bearer 'the enemy'; a diagnosis of his crime rather than a wish against him), and sharply from #3 (141:5), where kindness is received, not withheld — a rebuke welcomed as care. The arc across the three runs curse-withheld → accused-failure → embraced-correction; the shifting bearer (no one → the enemy → the psalmist) is the finding.

**kindness** — Psa 109:16  ·  `H2617:kindnes#2`

_Story_

A little later the psalm names exactly what made the enemy guilty: he never remembered to be kind. Here the failure is his own — not something wished on him, but the real fault in him.
