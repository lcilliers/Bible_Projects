**repent** — Psa 7:12  ·  `H7725:repent#1`

_Story_

This psalm pictures a righteous man appealing to a just God while a wicked enemy schemes against him. At its centre stands a warning about the wrongdoer's inner life: if a man does not repent. Repentance shows up here only as something the wicked man refuses to do. It is a matter of the will - not a feeling that overtakes him and not a single act he performs, but a turning he simply declines. The passage does not say what makes him this way; it just reports the closed door. His refusal is not aimed at anything beyond itself - it is entrenchment for its own sake, an interior locked shut. And it is a settled trait, not a passing mood. Everything about the judgment being readied over him - the sharpened sword, the bent bow - hangs on this one unmoving inner fact: he will not turn. This is repentance shown from its dark side: not the turn taken, but the turn withheld.

*Why this reading differs:* The item 'repent' (H7725) carries two distinct readings in this family and the difference is the finding. The one is the turn refused, the other the turn made-but-not-kept; set together they mark the two failure-shapes of repentance - never begun, and begun without root.

**turned** — Psa 30:11  ·  `H2015:turn#1`

_Story_

This is a song of rescue. The psalmist had gone down to the edge of death and had mourned in sackcloth, and now he looks back at what happened to him: you turned my mourning into dancing. The turning here is not something he did but something done to him - his whole inner mood was flipped over, grief swapped for joy. The image is of changing clothes: the rough sackcloth of grief stripped off and gladness put on in its place. Mourning and dancing are held together as the two ends of a single motion, the before and the after. The passage does not linger on how it happened or measure it; it simply reports the reversal itself as the heart of the matter. And it is not a passing lift of spirits but the settled new condition of a rescued man. In a group of words mostly about people turning back to God, this one names the opposite direction - God turning a person's inner world right around.

**fallen away** — Psa 53:3  ·  `H5472:fallenaway#1`

_Story_

This psalm takes God's own vantage point: he looks down from heaven over the whole human race to see if anyone understands, anyone truly seeks him. The verse gives the verdict - they have all fallen away. The word means turning back, drawing off, deserting, and here it is not one bad man but everyone, the entire race wheeling away from God at once. What makes it so heavy is the completeness of it: all together, without a single exception, not one left standing. And turning away is not a clean act - in the same breath they become corrupt; to desert God is to rot. The passage names no reason and traces no cause; it simply states the fact. This is the settled condition of humanity as the psalm sees it. In a group of words mostly about people turning back toward God, this one shows the opposite at its widest reach: everyone turned away, and God's search for even one who seeks him coming back empty.

**repent** — Psa 78:34  ·  `H7725:repent#2`

_Story_

This psalm retells Israel's history in the wilderness - fed, then struck down when they kept sinning. The verse catches a moment of turning: when God began killing among them, they repented and sought God earnestly. This is repentance as an actual movement - the people wheeling back toward the God they had abandoned. Their turning and their seeking are the same motion; to turn back is to search for him in earnest. And it is aimed squarely at God himself. But the passage is honest about what kind of turning it was. Only a couple of lines later it admits they flattered him with their mouths and lied to him with their tongues - the turn was real but shallow, made under the pressure of the blow and dropped as soon as the pressure eased. So this is repentance caught in the middle: not flatly refused, but not held either. It began when it hurt and faded when the hurt did.

**turned away** — Psa 78:57  ·  `H5472:turnedaway#1`

_Story_

After a long recital of God's rescues - leading them out of Egypt, bringing them into the land, driving out nations before them - the psalm turns to how the people answered: they turned away. The word means drawing off, deserting, and here it is the whole people wheeling back from the very God who had just settled them. Turning away and betrayal are one and the same motion; the desertion showed itself as treachery. And the passage marks something particular about it - they turned away like their fathers. This apostasy runs in the family line, the sons repeating the defection of the generation before them, handed down like an inherited flaw. The verse even likens them to a warped bow that cannot shoot straight. This is the same turning-away-from-God seen elsewhere, but named here as something bred in the bone, passed from fathers to sons.

**turn aside** — Psa 119:102  ·  `H5493:turnasid#1`

_Story_

Surrounded by enemies who set traps for him, the psalmist states his own steady inner course: I do not turn aside from your rules. The turning here is swerving off the path, and he names it only to say he refuses it. This is not a single act but a settled way of being - a constancy, a holding straight when everything around him would pull him off. What he refuses to veer from is God's word, and he gives the reason: for you have taught me. His steadiness is not sheer willpower but rests on the fact that God himself has been his teacher. It is one thread in the psalm's larger pattern of either turning away or holding fast. Here it is the good kind of not-turning: not the hardened refusal to repent, but the faithful refusal to drift - a person taught by God who simply will not swerve from what he has learned.

**awake** — Psa 139:18  ·  `H6974:awak#1`

_Story_

This is not something he does; it is a condition he simply finds himself in. Sleep is a kind of gap, a nightly break in awareness, and the wonder is that when his mind comes back on in the morning, God is still there - the connection held straight through the dark. Waking and being-still-with-God are one and the same discovery: to come to is to realize he never left the presence. It is a settled feature of his inner life, an abiding with-God-ness that even unconsciousness cannot cut.
