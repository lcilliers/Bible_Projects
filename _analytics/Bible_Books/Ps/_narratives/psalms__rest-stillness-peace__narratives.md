Psa 32:11 Be glad in the Lord , and rejoice , O righteous , and shout for joy , all you upright in heart !

**silent** — Psa 32:3  ·  `H2790:silent#1`

_Story_

David remembers a time when he stayed silent about his sin, and that silence did him no good. Refusing to own what he had done, he kept it locked inside — and instead of bringing calm, the concealment ate at him, until his very bones felt worn out and he groaned all day long. The quiet he chose was not the peaceful kind; it was the heavy hush of a man hiding, and it wore his body down from the inside. Nothing outside him caused it and it settled in no single part of him — it was simply the self, refusing to speak, quietly destroying itself. There is no rule here and no measure of how bad it grew; there is only the plain picture of what unconfessed silence does to a person.

_Reading_

The sense (101) is caught in the very phrase 'kept silent, my bones wasted' — silence here is not repose but a suppression that costs the body. Its type (102) is a state, not a discrete act: a sustained condition the self holds. The operation (106) traces the full movement: when the self kept silent about its sin, its bones wasted away through its groaning all day long — concealment corroding the body from within. The bearer (105) is the psalmist himself (inferred), so the silence is self-inflicted, not imposed; the target (107) is 'concealment's-cost', meaning the silence is aimed at nothing outside itself — it turns inward and its whole payload lands on the self. The coupling (112) — 'silence-wastes' — binds the interior act of not-speaking to physical decay: the two move as one, so that stillness of the mouth becomes rot of the frame. The discovery note (114) states the finding plainly: 'the operation is the self-destruction of the unconfessed: the interior, refusing to speak, rots itself.' Now the absences, which are evidence in their own right. Source (103) is not recorded (present:false): the passage does not name what generated this silence — it simply is the self's chosen concealment, ungrounded in a prior cause the reader can cite. Seat (104) is explicitly 'none': this operation is not lodged in a named organ or faculty; it is not heart-seated or bone-seated as a location, even though it manifests in the bones. Manner (108) is 'none': the text does not colour how the silence is held — no adverb of style, only the bare fact and its cost. Intensity (109), specifier (110), effect (111), and prohibition (113) are all unrecorded (present:false): the reader marked no measured degree, no narrowing qualifier, no separately-tracked outcome-flag beyond the operation itself, and no command against it — this is description of a lived ruin, not a rule. Role (115) is 'characteristic': this silence is read as a genuine trait of the inner being, not a mere qualifier of something else. Locus (116) is 'internal:ib-state': the whole event sits inside the human interior as a state of that interior. Read against the inner-being lens, this is the dark movement of the family: 'rest/stillness' inverted — a silence that does not settle the self but wastes it, because it is the muteness of guilt withheld rather than peace received. It stands deliberately unfiltered as the family's negative pole.

**mute** — Psa 39:2  ·  `H0481:mute#1`

_Story_

The psalmist had promised himself he would keep his mouth shut, guarding his tongue like a horse held by a bridle, so long as the wicked were watching. So he went mute and said nothing — but holding it all in did him no good at all. Far from calming him, the bottled-up words only made his inner turmoil worse, until the pressure built like a fire he could no longer contain. This was a silence he imposed on himself as an act of self-control, yet it aimed at nothing but its own failure: the harder he clamped down, the more his distress swelled. His forced quiet brought no peace; it only tightened the knot inside him.

_Reading_

The sense (101), 'I was mute, and my distress grew worse', already couples the muteness to a backfire. Its type (102) is a state — a held condition of enforced wordlessness. The operation (106) gives the full arc: the self was mute and silent, held its peace to no avail, and its distress only grew worse — the failure of forced silence. The bearer (105) is the psalmist (inferred): this is a deliberate self-restraint (v1's resolve to guard the mouth with a muzzle) carried into v2. The target (107) is 'silence's-failure': the whole operation is oriented not toward some external good but toward its own collapse — the restraint aimed at self-control lands on the self as intensified pain. The coupling (112), 'mute-distress-worse', welds the act of clamping the mouth to the swelling of inner distress: the more the words are held, the worse the interior becomes. The discovery note (114) names it: 'the backfiring of restraint: bottling the words did not ease the interior but inflamed it.' The absences: source (103) is unrecorded (present:false) — the text does not ground the muteness in a stated cause beyond the self's own vow. Seat (104) is 'none': the muteness is not organ-located, not lodged in a faculty. Manner (108) is 'none': no styling of how the silence was kept beyond its bare futility ('to no avail'). Intensity (109), specifier (110), effect (111), prohibition (113) all present:false: no graded degree, no narrowing qualifier, no separate outcome-flag, no command — the reader records a failed strategy, not a precept. Role (115) is 'characteristic'; locus (116) is 'internal:ib-state' — the event is a state of the human interior. In the inner-being frame this is the second face of the family's dark pole: stillness attempted as self-discipline that does not yield rest but pressure, the muzzled self growing hot until (v3) 'the fire burned' and the words break out. It is silence as containment that fails.

*Why this reading differs:* Both mute readings sit in Psa 39, but they differ in what the silence IS. In v2 (#1) the muteness is a self-imposed restraint that BACKFIRES — type 'state', target 'silence's-failure', coupling 'mute-distress-worse': containment that inflames the interior. In v9 (#2) the muteness is willed SUBMISSION — type shifts to 'volition', target 'submissive-silence', coupling 'mute-you-did-it': the same outward wordlessness now accepts affliction as God's own act. The difference is the finding: the same characteristic moves from failed self-control (ascent of distress) to yielded acceptance (settled quiet), a shift in both source-of-motive and effect on the self.

**mute** — Psa 39:9  ·  `H0481:mute#2`

_Story_

Later in the same psalm the psalmist is silent again, but everything about it has changed. Now he says, 'I am mute; I do not open my mouth, for it is you who have done it.' This time the quiet is not a strategy he forces on himself and it does not build up pressure inside him. It is a deliberate choice to stop arguing and accept what is happening as coming from God's own hand. He keeps his mouth closed not to bottle up his pain but because he has recognised who is behind his affliction and has decided not to protest. Where his earlier silence tightened the knot, this one loosens it — a settled quiet that comes from consent rather than from clenching.

_Reading_

The sense (101) is 'I am mute, for you have done it' — the muteness is now tied to a reason outside the self's own strategy. Crucially the type (102) shifts to 'volition': this is not a mere held state (as in v2) but an act of the will — a chosen not-speaking. The operation (106): the self is mute and does not open its mouth, because it is God who has done it — silent submission to God's dealing. The bearer (105) is the psalmist (inferred). The target (107) is 'submissive-silence': the operation is oriented toward acceptance itself; the self aims its wordlessness at yielding, not at controlling. The coupling (112), 'mute-you-did-it', binds the closed mouth to the recognition that God is the author of the affliction — the silence is held BECAUSE the hand behind the stroke is acknowledged. The discovery note (114) draws the distinction explicitly: 'distinct from v1's tongue-guard: this silence is submission, the interior accepting the affliction as God's own act and not protesting.' The absences: source (103) present:false — while the motive (God's doing) is named in the coupling, no separate source-row is recorded. Seat (104) 'none': not organ-located. Manner (108) 'none': the text does not colour the style of the submission. Intensity (109), specifier (110), effect (111), prohibition (113) all present:false: no measured degree, no qualifier, no separate outcome-flag, no command. Role (115) 'characteristic'; locus (116) 'internal:ib-state'. In the inner-being frame this is the family's constructive stillness reached through the storm: the same mouth that in v2 was muzzled to no avail is here shut in peace, because the self has stopped protesting and rests in the fact that the affliction is God's own. It is quiet as consent rather than quiet as containment — the interior at rest not by escaping the trouble but by accepting its source.

*Why this reading differs:* See H0481:mute#1. This v9 reading differs from the v2 reading in TYPE (volition here vs state there), in TARGET ('submissive-silence' vs 'silence's-failure') and in COUPLING ('mute-you-did-it', acceptance of God's act, vs 'mute-distress-worse', inflamed distress). The outward act — a mute mouth — is identical; the inner-being movement is opposite: v2 is failed containment, v9 is willed submission. That reversal within one psalm is itself the finding: the same characteristic can carry either a rising torment or a resting acceptance depending on whether it clamps down on the self or yields the self.

## Psa 55:6

> Psa 55:6 And I say, “Oh, that I had wings like a dove! I would fly away and be at rest;

**rest** — Psa 55:6  ·  `H7931:rest#1`

_Story_

In the middle of terror and betrayal in the city, the psalmist cries out a wish: 'Oh, that I had wings like a dove! I would fly away and be at rest.' What he longs for is not just to run away but to come to rest — to settle down in some peaceful place far from the violence around him. He does not have this rest; he only aches for it. The verse gives him no one to grant it and no moment where he actually arrives; it leaves him mid-longing, straining toward a calm he cannot yet reach. It is the picture of a heart worn out by strife, dreaming of a quiet dwelling — the peace it wants held just out of reach.

_Reading_

The sense (101) is 'be at rest / settle' (shakan — 'and be at rest'), the repose of a settled dwelling. Its type (102) is 'status': rest here is a condition-of-being longed for, not an action performed. The bearer (105) is the psalmist 'longing' (inferred) — and that qualifier matters: the rest is not possessed but craved. The operation (106) is 'long to rest / dwell in peace' (inferred): the movement is desire reaching toward a settledness the self does not yet have. The coupling (112) is a span-resolved pair — 'the goal of the longed-for flight' — binding this rest to the wish 'Oh, that I had wings like a dove! I would fly away': the rest is the destination of an imagined escape from the violent city. The discovery note (114) reads it as 'the ache for peace, for a settled place away from strife... not mere escape but rest, the wilderness preferable to the treacherous city.' The absences are pointed here. Source (103) present:false: the text does not name what would grant this rest. Seat (104) 'none': the rest is not organ-located. Target (107) is explicitly 'none': the longing has no object it acts upon — it is a reaching without a landing-place, which is itself the finding: the self yearns for rest but the verse gives it no attainment, no one to give it. Manner (108) 'none': no styling of the longing. Intensity (109), specifier (110), effect (111), prohibition (113) all present:false: no measured degree, no qualifier, no realised outcome, no command — and the missing effect is telling, because the rest is only wished, never reached within the verse. Role (115) 'characteristic'; locus (116) 'internal:ib-state'. In the inner-being frame this is rest as unfulfilled longing: the tormented interior, surrounded by strife and betrayal, straining toward a settled dwelling it cannot yet enter — the family's central good held out as desire rather than possession. The empty target and empty effect mark it precisely as ache, not arrival.

**safety** — Psa 55:18  ·  `H7965:safety#1`

_Story_

Surrounded by enemies and cut to the heart by a friend's betrayal, the psalmist declares that God 'redeems my soul in safety from the battle that I wage.' The word for safety here means wholeness and peace — the secure, unharmed state into which God carries his inner self after the fighting. This peace is not something he strains toward or works up; it is simply the settled condition he is brought into once he is rescued. It does nothing and reaches for nothing further; it is the place of arrival, calm itself. And the psalm makes its point by contrast: while the betrayer's heart is full of war, hidden behind words smooth as butter, the rescued soul is held in perfect peace.

_Reading_

The sense (101) is 'safety / peace' (shalom — 'in safety'), the wholeness into which the rescued self is brought. Its type (102) is 'status': a condition of secured wellbeing, not an act. The bearer (105) is 'the psalmist (his soul)' (inferred) — the peace is predicated of the inner self, the nephesh redeemed. The coupling (112) is a span-resolved pair — 'the peace in which the soul is redeemed' — binding this shalom to the redeeming act of v18, 'He redeems my soul in safety from the battle that I wage': the peace is the sphere the soul is brought INTO out of conflict. The discovery note (114) reads it as 'the wholeness/peace of the rescued self... the unharmed security into which God brings the soul out of battle — peace as the fruit of redemption, the opposite of the war in the betrayer's heart (v21).' Now the striking cluster of absences. Operation (106) is explicitly 'none': no process is recorded FOR the peace itself — the peace does not act; it is a resulting condition, and the reader marks that it does no work of its own. Target (107) is 'none': the safety is aimed at nothing; it is terminal, a place of arrival rather than a movement toward something. Seat (104) 'none': not organ-located. Source (103) present:false: no source-row, though the coupling ties it to redemption. Manner (108) 'none': no styling. Intensity (109), specifier (110), effect (111), prohibition (113) all present:false: no measured degree, no qualifier, no separate outcome-flag, no command. The empty operation and empty target together are the finding: unlike the longing of Psa 55:6, this shalom is not reached-for but received — it simply IS the settled state the redeemed soul occupies, requiring no further motion. Role (115) 'characteristic'; locus (116) 'internal:ib-state'. In the inner-being frame this is the family's positive pole realised: peace as the achieved condition of a soul brought whole out of the battle — and the psalm sharpens it by contrast, for the same passage shows 'war was in his heart' (v21) in the betrayer. The interior at peace stands directly against the interior at war.

## Psa 76:7-12

> Psa 76:7 But you , you are to be feared ! Who can stand before you when once your anger is roused ? Psa 76:8 From the heavens you uttered judgment ; the earth feared and was still , Psa 76:9 when God arose to establish judgment , to save all the humble of the earth . **still** — Psa 76:8  ·  `H8252:still#1`

_Story_

When God speaks his verdict from heaven, the whole earth goes quiet — but it is not the quiet of peace. 'The earth feared and was still.' This is the hush of terror: creation and everyone in it falling silent because God has risen up to judge, and no one can stand before him when his anger is roused. Here the stillness is not something the psalmist works up in his own soul; it is the reaction of the world, struck dumb with dread. Fear does not always shout — sometimes it shows itself as this awed, frozen silence before a God too great to answer back. It is the quiet that falls when the Judge stands up.

_Reading_

The sense (101) is 'be still / quiet' (shaqat — 'and was still'). Its type (102) is 'response': this stillness is not a self-generated state but a reaction — the earth answering something done to it. The bearer (105) is 'the earth / its inhabitants' (inferred), which sets this reading apart from the family's other members: the still one is not the psalmist's own soul but the created order and those in it. The operation (106) is 'fall silent / be quieted' — a hush that comes over the bearer. The target (107) is a span-pair, 'before God's judgment', binding the stillness to v8's 'From the heavens you uttered judgment; the earth feared and was still': the silence is directed Godward, in the face of his verdict. The manner (108) is recorded — 'hushed by dread' (inferred): here, uniquely in this family, the text colours HOW the stillness falls — not as calm but as the muteness of terror. The coupling (112) is a span-pair, 'paired with the earth's fear', welding the stillness to the fear of v8: dread and silence are one movement, fear expressed AS quiet rather than as clamour. The discovery note (114): 'the hush that follows the divine verdict... the silence of awe, the earth stilled when God rises to judge — dread expressed not as clamour but as quiet.' The absences: source (103) present:false — no source-row, though the trigger (God's judgment) is carried by the target. Seat (104) 'none': not organ-located. Intensity (109), specifier (110), effect (111), prohibition (113) all present:false: no measured degree, no qualifier, no separate outcome-flag, no command. Role (115) 'characteristic'. Locus (116) is the family's exception: 'external:god' — this stillness is located in the God-ward arena, a response before the divine judge, not simply an internal ib-state. In the inner-being frame this reading stretches the family outward: stillness here is the interior's (the earth's inhabitants') reaction of awed silence before God's rising judgment — quiet as the shape that dread takes when the Judge speaks. It is neither the failed silence of Psa 39:2 nor the received peace of Psa 55:18, but a third mode: silence compelled from outside by fear.

**rest** — Psa 94:13  ·  `H8252:rest#1`

_Story_

In a psalm crying out against oppressors who think God does not see, there is a quiet promise: God gives rest to the man he disciplines and teaches. This rest is not something the man wins for himself; it is given to him, a steadying calm 'from days of trouble.' Notice it does not remove the trouble — it holds him through it, keeping him settled while the hard days run their course. And it comes with a sharp companion thought: the very stretch of time in which the faithful man is given rest is the time in which a pit is being dug for the wicked. The same span of days that quiets the one who trusts God prepares the downfall of the one who mocks him.

_Reading_

The sense (101) is 'rest' (shaqat) — the same root that in Psa 76:8 rendered the earth's dread-silence, here turned to a granted repose. Its type (102) is 'state': a settled condition the self is brought into. The bearer (105) is 'the disciplined man' (inferred) — 'Blessed is the man whom you discipline, O Lord' (v12) — so the rest belongs to the one God is correcting, not to the untroubled. The operation (106) is 'be given rest': a passive of gift; the self does not seize this rest but receives it. The target (107) is 'from days of trouble' (inferred), binding the rest to v13's 'to give him rest from days of trouble': the repose is deliverance FROM affliction — rest as relief in the midst of ongoing trouble, not the absence of trouble. The coupling (112) is recorded as 'internal:ib-state' (inferred, span-linked), marking the rest as an interior condition welded to the disciplined self. The discovery note (114): 'the quiet God grants the disciplined while judgment ripens for the wicked' — and it names the sharp pairing that the locus makes explicit. Locus (116) here carries an unusual value, 'paired with the pit dug for the wicked': the disciplined man's rest is set directly against v13's second clause, 'until a pit is dug for the wicked' — the same span of time gives the righteous rest and prepares the wicked's ruin. The absences: source (103) present:false — no source-row, though God is plainly the giver. Seat (104) 'none': not organ-located. Manner (108) 'none': no styling of the rest. Intensity (109), specifier (110), effect (111), prohibition (113) all present:false: no measured degree, no qualifier, no separate outcome-flag, no command. Role (115) 'characteristic'. In the inner-being frame this is rest as gift-in-the-midst: the interior of the disciplined man given quiet not by escaping trouble (contrast Psa 55:6's longed-for flight) but by God's holding him through it, while the same stretch of days that steadies him seals the wicked's fall. Read without valence bias, the reading holds two interiors at once — the rested righteous and the doomed wicked — as one paired movement.

## Psa 120:6-7

> Psa 120:6 Too long have I had my dwelling among those who hate peace . Psa 120:7 I am for peace , but when I speak , they are for war !

**peace** — Psa 120:7  ·  `H7965:peac#1`

_Story_

The psalmist has lived too long among people who hate peace, and he sums up the clash in one line: 'I am for peace, but when I speak, they are for war.' His peace is not a mood that comes and goes but a settled way of being — he is, at his core, for peace, and he stays that way even when it is met with hostility. He turns that peaceable heart toward the very people who want to fight him, and they answer his every word with more war. The verse gives him no truce and no win; it simply shows a man whose inner bent toward peace holds firm against a world set on conflict. His peace is real, but it stands alone, unshaken and unrewarded.

_Reading_

The sense (101) is 'peace' (shalom). Its type (102) is 'disposition' — and this is the reading's distinctive mark within the family: peace here is neither a longed-for status (Psa 55:6) nor a received condition (Psa 55:18) but a settled bent of the self, a standing orientation. The bearer (105) is the psalmist (inferred): 'I am for peace.' The operation (106) is 'be for peace': the self's active leaning toward peace as its posture. The target (107) is 'toward the hostile' (inferred), binding the peaceable disposition to v7's contrast, 'but when I speak, they are for war': the peace is directed at those who want war — it faces hostility and does not convert to it. The coupling (112), 'internal:ib-state' (inferred, span-linked), marks the peace as an interior condition of the self. The discovery note (114): 'the psalmist's peaceable heart, met with the foes' appetite for war.' Locus (116) carries the value 'paired with their bent for war': the psalmist's peace is set against the enemies' war-appetite as a deliberate contrast — his interior disposition defined by opposition to theirs. The absences: source (103) present:false — no source-row for the peace. Seat (104) 'none': not organ-located. Manner (108) 'none': no styling. Intensity (109), specifier (110), effect (111), prohibition (113) all present:false: no measured degree, no qualifier, no separate outcome-flag, no command. Role (115) 'characteristic'. In the inner-being frame this is peace as steadfast disposition under provocation: the self that remains oriented to peace even while surrounded by, and answered with, war. The empty effect is quietly important — the verse records no result, no peace achieved between the parties; the peace stays a one-sided inner posture that hostility does not dislodge. Read without valence bias, the passage holds two dispositions in flat opposition — a self for peace, a world for war — and gives the peaceable self no victory, only its unbroken bent.

## Psa 131:1-3

> A Song of Ascents . Of David . Psa 131:1 O Lord , my heart is not lifted up ; my eyes are not raised too high; I do not occupy myself with things too great and too marvelous for me . Psa 131:3 O Israel , hope in the Lord from this time forth and forevermore .

**quieted** — Psa 131:2  ·  `H1826:quiet#1`

_Story_

In this short psalm David describes deliberately calming himself down. 'I have calmed and quieted my soul,' he says, 'like a weaned child with its mother.' This is something he does, not something that just happens to him — he takes his own restless, striving inner self and hushes it. It grows out of the humility of the opening line, where he refuses to reach for things too high and too great for him; letting go of that grasping, he settles his soul into stillness. The picture is of a child no longer fretting for its mother's milk, content and quiet — and that is what he makes of his own inner life. It is peace won by the self's own gentle work of letting go.

_Reading_

The sense (101) is 'quiet' (damam). Its type (102) is 'action' — not a state that befalls the self but a deed the self performs: 'I have... quieted my soul.' The bearer (105) is the psalmist (inferred), and here the bearer is also the agent AND the object: the self quiets the self. The operation (106) is 'quiet the soul': the interior work of hushing one's own inner clamour. The target (107) is 'into stillness' (inferred), binding the operation to the result-state of v2, 'like a weaned child... is my soul within me': the quieting drives toward a settled, weaned calm. The coupling (112), 'internal:ib-state' (inferred, span-linked), marks it as wholly an interior condition. The discovery note (114): 'the hushing of the inner clamour, the self brought to silence.' The absences: source (103) present:false — no source-row for the quieting, though v1 supplies the moral setting (a heart not lifted up, not reaching for things too great). Seat (104) 'none': not organ-located — the soul is quieted but not a named organ. Manner (108) 'none': no styling of how the quieting is done. Intensity (109), specifier (110), effect (111), prohibition (113) all present:false: no measured degree, no qualifier, no separate outcome-flag beyond the operation, no command. Role (115) 'characteristic'. Locus (116) carries the value 'paired with calming the soul' — the quieting is explicitly bound to its twin verb in the same verse (H7737:calm, shavah), the two forming one self-stilling act. In the inner-being frame this is the family's positive pole reached by the self's OWN work: quiet not imposed by dread (Psa 76:8) nor merely received (Psa 55:18) but achieved — the interior deliberately hushing its own noise and ambition until it rests like a weaned child. It stands as the constructive mirror of Psa 32:3's silence: there silence rotted the self; here quiet settles it, the difference lying in what the self does with its interior.

**calmed** — Psa 131:2  ·  `H7737:calm#1`

_Story_

Right alongside quieting his soul, David also says he has 'calmed' it — and the two words together describe one gentle work he does on himself. If quieting names the stillness he arrives at, calming names what he does with the restless craving underneath: he settles the hungry, grasping part of himself that wants things too high for it. Having decided in the opening line not to occupy himself with matters too great and marvellous, he calms that appetite down. The two acts belong together — he calms the wanting and quiets the noise — until his inner self rests as contentedly as a weaned child with its mother. It is the picture of a person actively bringing their own striving heart to peace.

_Reading_

The sense (101) is 'calm' (shavah). Its type (102) is 'action' — like its twin (H1826:quiet) in the same verse, a deed the self performs, not a condition received. The bearer (105) is the psalmist (inferred), again both agent and object: the self calms the self. The operation (106) is 'calm the soul': the active work of settling the restless interior. The target (107) is 'from craving' (inferred) — and this is where it is distinguished from its twin: whereas the paired verb aims 'into stillness', this one is read as calming the soul AWAY from craving, quieting the appetite of a self that (v1) refuses to reach for things too great. The coupling (112), 'internal:ib-state' (inferred, span-linked), marks it as an interior condition. The discovery note (114): 'the operation of self-stilling: the active work of settling the restless self.' The absences: source (103) present:false — no source-row. Seat (104) 'none': not organ-located. Manner (108) 'none': no styling of how the calming is done. Intensity (109), specifier (110), effect (111), prohibition (113) all present:false: no measured degree, no qualifier, no separate outcome-flag, no command. Role (115) 'characteristic'. Locus (116) carries 'paired with quieting the soul' — explicitly bound to its twin verb, the two together forming the single self-stilling act of v2. In the inner-being frame this is the same constructive movement as its twin but caught at a slightly different angle: 'calm' addresses the SOURCE of the unrest — the craving, the reaching appetite — while 'quiet' names the RESULT — stillness. Preserved as distinct (directive 8), the pair shows the self-stilling of Psa 131 as a two-beat operation: first the appetite is calmed, then the soul falls quiet, and the interior settles like a weaned child.

*Why this reading differs:* This 'calm' (shavah) and its verse-mate 'quiet' (damam) share bearer, verse, action-type and self-stilling operation, so they are NOT collapsed: the difference is the finding. 'Calm' is read with target 'from craving' — addressing the SOURCE of unrest, the reaching appetite; 'quiet' is read with target 'into stillness' — naming the RESULT, the settled hush. Together they render Psa 131:2's self-stilling as a two-beat movement (calm the craving, then the soul falls quiet), each verb catching a different phase of the one act.
