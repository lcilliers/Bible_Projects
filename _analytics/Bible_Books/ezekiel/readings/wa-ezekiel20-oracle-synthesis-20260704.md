## EZE-name-sake-loathe · Ezekiel 20:1–49 · [D, multi] — God acting for his name's sake; the self-loathing that the memory of forgiven sin produces

**The reading.** God recounts Israel's rebellion from Egypt through the wilderness through the generations, and a refrain tolls: at each point of deserved destruction, "**I acted for the sake of my name, that it should not be profaned in the sight of the nations**" (vv.9,14,22). His forbearance is grounded not in their worthiness but in his own name. There is a sober note — "**I gave them statutes that were not good**" (v.25) — the handing-over to ruinous consequences. Yet the movement ends in restoration and its interior fruit: "**there you shall remember your ways and all your deeds with which you have defiled yourselves, and you shall loathe yourselves for all the evils that you have committed**" (v.43); "**you shall know that I am the LORD, when I deal with you for my name's sake, not according to your evil ways**" (v.44). The finding is **God's forbearance and salvation grounded in *his own name* (not the people's merit) — producing, when grace is received, a *self-loathing* at the memory of forgiven sin** *(stated)*. The operation is **the relocation of the ground of salvation *outside the self*: God spares and restores "**for my name's sake… not according to your evil ways**" — so the interior owes its deliverance entirely to God's regard for his own glory, not to any goodness of its own; and the *fruit* of such unmerited grace is *not license but self-abhorrence* — "**you shall loathe yourselves**" — the humbling recognition, in the light of undeserved mercy, of what one has been** *(inferred: the refrain "**for the sake of my name**" (vv.9,14,22,44) is the theological spine — the finding is that God's forbearance is grounded in *his own name and glory*, not in the people's merit: they deserved destruction at every stage, and what stayed his hand was his regard for his *name* "in the sight of the nations"; the inner-being implication is *radical humility* — the interior can claim *no credit* for its own preservation; it lives entirely by God's commitment to himself; and the striking fruit (v.43) is that this unmerited grace produces *self-loathing* — "**you shall LOATHE YOURSELVES for all the evils**": the finding is that grace rightly received does *not* inflate the self ("God spared me, so I must be worth sparing") but *humbles* it — the memory of one's sins, seen in the light of a mercy one did *not* earn (given "not according to your evil ways", v.44), produces *self-abhorrence*, a holy shame (as 16:63, "ashamed… when I atone for you"); the finding is the paradox of grace — being freely forgiven makes one *think less* of oneself, not more; the deeper the awareness that salvation was "for his name's sake, not mine", the deeper the humbled loathing of the sin that such mercy covered)*.
