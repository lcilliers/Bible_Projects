**eat up** — Psa 53:4  ·  `H0398:eatup#1`

_Story_

The psalm opens with the fool who says in his heart there is no God, and then it shows what such men do: they eat up God's people as easily as they eat their daily bread. That picture is the heart of it. Human beings are treated as food - consumed, used up, devoured - and the horror is how ordinary it is to them. There is no rage described, no agony of conscience, just appetite: they harm people as thoughtlessly as a man tears off a piece of bread at a meal. The passage does not tell us what drives them; it simply reports the deed. And it ties the cruelty tightly to their godlessness - the very men who feed on God's people are the men who never call on God. The two go together: to treat people as prey and to refuse God are one and the same movement. This is not a single cruel act but the settled character of men who have decided there is no one above them to answer to.

_Reading_

The sense (101) is akal - 'who eat up my people as they eat bread': devouring, the consuming of persons as though they were food. Type (102) is action: a doing, an active predation, not a felt state or a standing status. Source (103) is absent: the reading books no interior spring behind the devouring - no dread, no desire, no thought is named as its origin; it is reported as bare deed, its motive left unaccounted. Seat (104) resolves to none: the cruelty is not organ-located, not lodged in a named inner faculty - it is the persons' action, not the working of an interior organ. Bearer (105) is the evildoers (inferred, to_span 279919): the interior read here is that of 'those who work evil', the fools of v1 who say there is no God. Operation (106), the core event, is 'eat up / devour' - the consuming of human beings. Target (107) is God's people (inferred, to_span 279919): the devouring is aimed at the LORD's own, the covenant people made prey. Manner (108) is 'as they eat bread' (span-resolved pair, from_span 279919 to_span 279921): the HOW is the sharpest stroke - the people are consumed casually, as routinely and unthinkingly as a man eats his daily loaf; cruelty made ordinary, appetite without horror. Intensity (109), specifier (110), and effect (111) are all absent: no degree of the devouring is scored, nothing narrows it further, and no downstream outcome is booked - the reading holds the act itself, not its measure or its result. Coupling (112) is 'paired with not calling upon God' (span-resolved pair, from_span 279919 to_span 279924): the devouring is welded to godlessness - the same men who eat up God's people 'do not call upon God'; the cruelty and the refusal of God are one movement, the horizontal predation inseparable from the vertical refusal. Prohibition (113) is absent: no forbidding is marked here, though the whole psalm indicts these men. Discovery (114) names it: predatory cruelty made routine - devouring God's people as casually as one eats a meal, oppression without conscience, joined to a refusal to call on God. Role (115) is characteristic: a standing trait of the evildoers' interior, not a single lapse. Locus (116) is external:person - the operation is directed outward at other persons; it is cruelty aimed at human beings, not an inward state turned on the self. The full process: from no named source and no organ-seat, the godless evildoers devour the LORD's people as casually as they eat bread, appetite without conscience, the predation welded to their refusal to call on God - a routine, external cruelty that treats persons as food.

**ruthless men** — Psa 54:3  ·  `H6184:ruthlessmen#1`

_Story_

Here the danger is named not as a deed but as a kind of man. Ruthless men are after the psalmist's life - and the word points to their character before it points to any single act. They are simply cruel; hardness is what they are. The passage does not explain where such hardness comes from. But it does name the one thing that lets it run loose: they do not set God before themselves. That is the whole engine of their cruelty - not that they are angry or wronged, but that God is nowhere in their sight, and so nothing holds them back. With no fear of God to restrain them, their ruthlessness shows itself in the one thing they want: to take his life. It is the same picture as the fool who says there is no God - once God is out of view, a person is free to be as merciless as he pleases.

_Reading_

The sense (101) is arits - 'ruthless men seek my life': the violent, terror-inspiring man, cruelty as a kind of person rather than a single deed. Type (102) is status: not an action booked here nor a passing feeling but a standing condition - this is what these men ARE, ruthless by character. Source (103) is absent: no interior origin is traced - the reading does not name what breeds the ruthlessness, reporting the condition rather than its spring. Seat (104) resolves to none: the ruthlessness is not organ-located, not lodged in a named faculty - it is the men's character, held by the whole person, not seated in an inner organ. Bearer (105) is the ruthless enemies (inferred, to_span 279984): the interior read is that of the aggressors who have risen against the psalmist, the 'strangers' of the verse. Operation (106) is none: strikingly, no event is booked - the reading records no act the ruthlessness performs here; it is presented as status, a settled cruelty, not caught in a particular deed. Target (107) is none likewise: no object is filed for an operation that is not itself booked - the menace is named as character before it is traced to a victim. Manner (108) carries a real value: 'they do not set God before themselves' (inferred, to_span 279984) - the HOW of their ruthlessness is godlessness; the mode in which they are ruthless is precisely that God is not before their eyes, the restraint of the fear of God simply absent. Intensity (109), specifier (110), and effect (111) are all absent: no degree is scored, nothing narrows it, no outcome booked - the reading holds the bare status. Coupling (112) is 'paired with seeking the psalmist's life' (span-resolved pair, from_span 279984 to_span 279985): though no operation is booked, the ruthlessness is welded to the hunting of his life - the character and the lethal aim move together, the status showing itself in the pursuit of a life. Prohibition (113) is absent. Discovery (114) names it: the violent, God-ignoring aggressors - arits, the ruthless whose godlessness (like the fool of Ps 53) frees them to hunt the psalmist's life without restraint. Role (115) is characteristic: a standing trait, cruelty as identity. Locus (116) is external:person - the ruthlessness is directed outward at another; it is menace aimed at a human life. The full process: from no named source and no organ-seat, and without a single deed being booked, men are named ruthless by character - their cruelty a status whose very mode is that God is not before them, welded to their seeking of the psalmist's life; the absence of the fear of God is what leaves the ruthlessness unrestrained, an outward menace to a human being.

*Why this reading differs:* The same term arits recurs at Psa 86:14 (reading H6184:ruthlessmen#2), and the two readings differ tellingly. Here at Ps 54:3 the ruthlessness is pure status with no operation booked - cruelty named as character before any deed - and its mode is godlessness ('they do not set God before themselves'), located as external:person. At Ps 86:14 the same status breaks into a definite operation, 'seek my life', its godlessness shifted from manner to the aim ('without regard for God'), coupled with insolence and located as an internal:ib-state. Both name men ruthless by character with God out of view; the difference is that one holds the ruthlessness as bare identity turned outward, the other catches it in the act of hunting a life as an inner disposition - one word for the merciless read now as standing status, now as status erupting into deed.

**violence** — Psa 55:9  ·  `H2555:violenc#1`

_Story_

The psalmist looks out over the city and what he sees is not one crime but a whole atmosphere: violence and strife everywhere, filling the streets day and night. Violence here is less a single act than a climate - it is simply what the place is like, the settled character of the people who live in it. He does not trace where it began or point to one attacker; he describes a town soaked in cruelty and quarrelling, the two running together as the twin diseases of the place. And though it fills a city, it is really a picture of what is inside the wicked - their inner ugliness spread out until it becomes the very air everyone breathes. This is cruelty not as an event but as a condition, a whole community shaped by the hardness of the hearts within it.

_Reading_

The sense (101) is chamas - 'I see violence... in the city': violent wrong, cruelty as the pervading condition of a place. Type (102) is status: not a single act booked here but a standing state - violence as the settled climate of the city. Source (103) is absent: no interior origin is traced for the violence; it is reported as an atmosphere, its spring in individual hearts left unnamed. Seat (104) resolves to none: the violence is not organ-located, not lodged in a named faculty - it saturates the city rather than sitting in one interior organ. Bearer (105) is the city's wicked (inferred, to_span 280206): the interior behind the violence is that of the wicked who fill the city, those who 'go around it on its walls' day and night. Operation (106) is none: no discrete event is booked - the violence is not caught in a single deed but presented as the condition of the whole place, pervasive rather than punctual. Target (107) is none: with no operation booked, no object is filed - the violence is named as ambient wrong before it is aimed at a victim. Manner (108) is none: no mode is marked - the reading gives the bare fact of pervasive violence, uncoloured. Intensity (109), specifier (110), and effect (111) are all absent: no degree is scored, nothing narrows it, no outcome booked - violence is held as sheer state. Coupling (112) is 'paired with strife filling the city' (span-resolved pair, from_span 280206 to_span 280207): the violence is welded to strife - the two named together as the twin sicknesses the psalmist sees, cruelty and contention filling the city as one. Prohibition (113) is absent. All three are status and (bar Ps 58's outward reach) internally located, but they move from ambient climate, to inflicted deed, to worn identity.

**strife** — Psa 55:9  ·  `H7379:strif#1`

_Story_

Alongside the violence, the psalmist sees strife - endless quarrelling and division running through the whole city. Like the violence, it is not one dispute but a condition, the settled state of a people at odds with each other. But there is something here that the violence beside it did not have: the psalmist names where he wants the strife to lead. He prays that God would confuse their tongues, deliberately deepening their divisions - a deliberate echo of Babel, calling down on the city the very chaos of speech that once scattered mankind. So the contention and the violence are twins, two names for one sickness in the town; and the psalmist's answer is to ask God to turn the wicked's own division into their judgment, letting them fracture until they can no longer stand together. Underneath it all, the quarrelling is simply what the wicked are, their inner discord spread out until it becomes the character of the whole place.

_Reading_

**attack** — Psa 56:2  ·  `H3898:attack#1`

_Story_

The psalmist is surrounded. Many enemies are making war on him - not a single rival but a crowd, trampling him all day long and pressing the attack. What marks their assault is pride: they come at him from above, with the contempt of people who think themselves his betters, cruelty carried out with a sneer. The trampling and the warring are the same thing seen twice - the foes who tread him underfoot are the ones waging open battle against him. The passage does not say what set them off; it simply shows the arrogant force of the many against the one. And it lets their pride stand out for a reason: it is exactly this swaggering human hostility that the psalmist's trust cuts down to size when he asks what mere flesh can really do to him. Their arrogance is loud, but against a trusting man it turns out to be only flesh.

**strife** — Psa 56:6  ·  `H1481:strif#1`

_Story_

The psalmist's enemies do not just hate him at a distance - they organise. They band together, stirring up trouble as a group, and then they lie in wait: watching his every step, tracking his movements, biding their time for his life. This is not random hostility that flares and fades; it is deliberate, coordinated malice, people pooling their ill will and lying in ambush. The plotting and the stalking are one motion - they scheme together precisely so they can watch and wait and strike. The passage does not tell us what first turned them against him; it simply shows the patient, gathered cruelty of foes who have made his destruction a shared project. It is the ugliness of malice that has become organised, a hunt run by many against one.

_Reading_

The sense (101) is gur - 'they stir up strife, they lurk': to gather, to band together and stir up contention, cruelty as collusion. Type (102) is action: a doing, the active massing and scheming of the foes, not a static status. Source (103) is absent: no interior origin is booked - the reading does not name what motivates the banding, reporting the deed alone. Seat (104) resolves to none: the colluding is not organ-located, not lodged in a named faculty - it is the enemies' action, not the working of an inner organ. Bearer (105) is the enemies (inferred, to_span 280298): the interior read is that of the foes who 'all day long injure my cause' and whose 'thoughts are against me for evil'. Operation (106), the core event, is 'band together / stir up' - the enemies massing and rousing contention as one. Target (107) is 'against the psalmist' (inferred, to_span 280298): the collusion is aimed at him, malice organised toward one man. Manner (108) is none: no mode is marked - the bare fact of the banding, uncoloured. Intensity (109), specifier (110), and effect (111) are all absent: no degree scored, nothing narrowing it, no outcome booked. Coupling (112) is 'paired with watching his steps' (span-resolved pair, from_span 280298 to_span 280300): the stirring-up is welded to surveillance - they band together AND 'watch my steps, as they have waited for my life'; the conspiracy and the stalking move together, plotting joined to lying-in-wait. Prohibition (113) is absent. Discovery (114) names it: the enemies conspiring - gur, the collusion of the foes, massing and lying in wait; organised malice, not random hostility. Role (115) is characteristic: a standing trait of the enemies' interior, scheming as their settled bent. The full process: from no named source and no organ-seat, the enemies band together and stir up strife against one man, their conspiracy welded to a patient watching of his steps as they wait for his life - organised, deliberate malice rather than a random flare, an outward cruelty aimed at a human being.

*Why this reading differs:* The same gur ('stir up strife / band together') recurs at Ps 59:3 (reading H1481:strif#2). Both are action, both external:person, both the massing of foes aimed at the psalmist. Here at Ps 56:6 the banding is welded to surveillance ('they watch my steps'), the conspiracy joined to a patient stalking, and the bearer is 'the enemies'. At Ps 59:3 it is welded instead to the doers themselves ('the fierce men') and framed as unprovoked ('for no transgression of mine'). The shared substance is organised, deliberate malice; the difference is that one foregrounds the stalking that the plotting serves, the other the sheer groundlessness of the massing.

**violence** — Psa 58:2  ·  `H2555:violenc#2`

_Story_

This psalm turns on rulers who were supposed to hand down justice and instead hand out harm. The line is stark: in their hearts they plan wrongs, and their hands deal out violence across the land. The key is that the two halves belong together - what they secretly plot inside, they then dispense outside; the violence of their hands is simply their inner corruption made visible. It is not one cruel verdict but their whole way of operating, injustice as a settled habit. And its reach is wide: not a single victim but the earth itself becomes the field where their cruelty is spread. The passage shows wrongdoing travelling from the inside out - a corrupt heart that does not stay hidden but works its way into the hands and out into the world, until the people who should have protected the land are the ones filling it with violence.

_Reading_

The sense (101) is chamas - 'your hands deal out violence on earth': violent wrong, cruelty issuing from the hand. _Reading_

The sense (101) is kalah - 'They had almost made an end of me on earth': to be finished, all but destroyed - here the near-annihilation of one persecuted man. Type (102) is state: not a deed but a condition - the state of being brought to the very brink of destruction. Source (103) is absent as a booked row, though the passage names the agents plainly: it is the insolent persecutors (vv 85-86) who nearly end him - the reading files no separate source, letting the enemies stand in the passage. Seat (104) resolves to none: the near-ending is not organ-located, not lodged in a named faculty - it is the whole person's condition. Bearer (105) is the psalmist (inferred, to_span 272139): the interior read is that of the servant persecuted with falsehood, dug about with pitfalls. Operation (106) is 'be almost destroyed': the event is the near-finishing itself - brought to the edge, not over it. Target (107) is none: no object is filed - the near-destruction is the psalmist's own suffered state, not aimed onward at another. Manner (108) is none: no mode is marked - the bare fact of the near-ending. Intensity (109), specifier (110), and effect (111) are all absent: no degree beyond 'almost' is scored, nothing narrows it, no outcome booked. Coupling (112) is 'paired within its char-arc across the psalm' (inferred, to_span 272139): the reading welds this near-ending to the wider movement of Psalm 119 - the recurring pressure of the insolent against a servant who will not let go of the word. Prohibition (113) is absent. Discovery (114) names it, and here is the turn: the near-destruction endured 'but I have not forsaken your precepts' - the being-almost-ended met not with collapse but with fidelity. Role (115) is characteristic: a standing condition of the persecuted servant. Locus (116) is internal:ib-state - the near-ending is read as an inner-being state, the brink felt within by a man who holds his ground. The full process: brought by his persecutors to the very edge of destruction, the psalmist is 'almost made an end of' - a state suffered, not a deed done; and unlike the perishing of Ps 90, this near-ending is met with resolve - held within as the brink of ruin, welded to the psalm's long arc of pressure, yet answered by a servant who, all but finished, still does not forsake the word.

*Why this reading differs:* The same kalah is read at Ps 90:7 (H3615:end#1) and Ps 90:9 (H3615:end#2), where the bearer is mankind - consumed by God's anger, then simply running out 'like a sigh'. Here at Ps 119:87 the bearer narrows to one persecuted man, and the agent is neither God's wrath nor mere transience but human enemies who have 'almost made an end' of him. Most tellingly, the response reverses: where Ps 90 meets the ending with dismay and resignation, this reading meets it with fidelity ('I have not forsaken your precepts'). One word for coming-to-an-end spans divine judgment, natural brevity, and human persecution resisted - the last of these turning the brink of ruin into an occasion of loyalty.
