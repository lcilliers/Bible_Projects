## Psa 2:1-2

> Psa 2:1 Why do the nations rage and the peoples plot in vain ? Psa 2:2 The kings of the earth set themselves, and the rulers take counsel together , against the Lord and against his Anointed , saying,

**rage** — Psa 2:1  ·  `H7283:rage#1`

_Story_

In the opening of this psalm whole nations are pictured boiling over with fury against God and his chosen king. It is not yet a scheme, just raw heat — a crowd seething with rage. The poem never says what set them off; the anger simply rises from inside them, as if from nowhere. It is not pinned to any one part of a person, nor given any particular shape or measured strength — it is simply a shared, churning uproar. And the verse leaves it there, at full boil, without yet telling us what it leads to. What we see is a whole people's inner life burning with revolt, with God as the one it burns against.

_Reading_

The characteristic here is 'rage' typed as an affect (D102 type=affect) — not yet a plan but the raw heat of revolt. Its sense (D101) is 'nations rage': the massed peoples seethe. The bearer (D105) is the nations themselves; the operation (D106) is a collective seething in tumultuous uproar against the LORD and his anointed — fury as a corporate interior state. The target (D107) registers as 'tumult', i.e. the fury pours out into uproar rather than toward a discrete object it means to change, and the coupling (D112) binds the state to 'rage' itself. Crucially the locus (D116) is internal:ib-state — an interior condition of the peoples — and the role (D115) is a genuine characteristic, not a mere qualifier. The discovery note (D114) reads v1 as the churning uproar of the massed peoples, emotion before scheme. Now the silences, which are evidence. No source (D103 present:false) — the text does not say what kindles the rage; it is presented as arising, uncaused, from within the collective. No seat (D104=none) — the fury is not lodged in a named organ (heart, bowels); it is diffuse, a whole-body corporate heat, not organ-located. No manner (D108=none), no intensity (D109 absent), no specifier (D110 absent): the rage is given bare, without described texture or measured degree. No effect (D111 absent) — the verse holds the emotion at its boiling point, before consequence. No prohibition (D113 absent) — it is described, not forbidden. In the inner-being frame God and his anointed are the arena the fury is directed against, but the reading stays on the human interior — a shared, sourceless, seatless, boundless heat of rebellion.

## Psa 4:1-5

> To the choirmaster : with stringed instruments . A Psalm of David . Psa 4:1 Answer me when I call , O God of my righteousness ! You have given me relief when I was in distress . Be gracious to me and hear my prayer ! Psa 4:2 O men , how long shall my honor be turned into shame ? How long will you love vain words and seek after lies ? Selah Psa 4:3 But know that the Lord has set apart the godly for himself; the Lord hears when I call to him . Psa 4:4 Be angry , and do not sin ; ponder in your own hearts on your beds , and be silent . Selah Psa 4:5 Offer right sacrifices , and put your trust in the Lord . Psa 4:6 There are many who say, “Who will show us some good? Lift up the light of your face upon us, O Lord!”

**angry** — Psa 4:4  ·  `H7264:angry#1`

_Story_

This verse does something surprising with anger — it does not condemn it. It tells the listener: go ahead and be angry, but do not let it tip over into wrongdoing. The work here is self-mastery: to feel the heat and hold it, neither smothering it nor letting it run wild. The poem never says what provoked the anger, because that is not the point; it never locates the feeling in one part of a person or measures how fierce it is. What matters is the fence around it. And notice — the anger itself is never called sin; only letting it break loose is. It is a picture of a person governing a strong emotion from within.

_Reading_

Here anger is typed not as an affect that simply happens but as volition (D102 type=volition) — something the hearer is summoned to govern. The sense (D101) is the command 'be angry and do not sin.' The bearer (D105) is the hearer addressed; the operation (D106) is anger permitted yet fenced: the passion is neither denied nor indulged but disciplined — felt without being let spill into sin. Tellingly the target (D107) is 'disciplined-anger' itself — the object of the work is the emotion, turned back on itself to be mastered; the coupling (D112) binds 'angry' to 'not-sin', the two held in a single tether. The locus (D116) is internal:ib-state and the role (D115) a characteristic: a real interior movement, not a label. The discovery (D114) names it the governing of a strong emotion — restraint short of wrongdoing, not suppression. The silences sharpen this. No source (D103 present:false) — the provocation is not named, because the point is not what stirs the anger but what one does with it. No seat (D104=none) — not lodged in a bodily organ (the next clause moves to 'ponder in your hearts', but the anger itself is not seated). No manner (D108=none), no intensity (D109 absent), no specifier (D110 absent): the anger is given without texture or degree, because the command concerns its boundary, not its heat. No effect (D111 absent): the outcome is left open — the verse legislates the handling, not the result. Strikingly, no prohibition on the anger itself (D113 absent) — anger is not forbidden; only its spilling into sin is fenced. Within the inner-being frame this is the rare disciplined pole of the family: the human interior told it may burn, but under restraint.

**Fret** — Psa 37:1  ·  `H2734:fret#1`

_Story_

This psalm opens with a warning against a very ordinary temptation: stewing over people who do wrong and seem to get away with it. Do not fret, it says, and do not envy them. The task is inward — to refuse the slow burn of resentment that the success of the wicked stirs up. The poem does not spell out exactly what sparks the feeling, or where in a person it sits, or how hot it gets; it simply calls the person to put it out. It is anger's quieter cousin, a gnawing agitation, and here the command is plain: don't let it take hold.

_Reading_

'Fret' enters the family as volition (D102 type=volition), a movement the hearer is charged to refuse. Its sense (D101) is the double command 'fret not, be not envious of evildoers.' The bearer (D105) is the hearer; the operation (D106) is the self told not to burn over the wicked — refusing the corrosive envy stirred by their prosperity. The target (D107) is 'refusing-envy' — again the object is the inner heat itself, to be quelled; the coupling (D112) ties the state to its own negation, 'fret-not'. Locus (D116) internal:ib-state, role (D115) characteristic. The discovery (D114) reads it as the disciplining of a specific inner unrest: the interior refuses to burn with envy at the wicked's success. This is anger's near-neighbour — resentful agitation — met here by prohibition-in-the-form-of-command. The silences: no source named (D103 present:false), though the surrounding passage supplies the occasion (the evildoers' prosperity); the row itself leaves the origin unstated. No seat (D104=none) — not organ-lodged. No manner (D108=none), no intensity (D109 absent), no specifier (D110 absent): the fret is given bare, its boundary rather than its texture in view. No effect (D111 absent) — though the wider psalm warns fretting 'tends only to evil' (v8), this anchor row records no traced outcome. No prohibition row (D113 absent) even though the sense is itself a prohibition — the forbidding is carried inside the operation, not flagged separately. In the inner-being frame the wicked are the occasion in the arena, but the reading holds on the hearer's interior, called to refuse a smouldering, envious agitation.

## Psa 55:1-5

> To the choirmaster : with stringed instruments . A Maskil of David . Psa 55:1 Give ear to my prayer , O God , and hide not yourself from my plea for mercy ! Psa 55:2 Attend to me, and answer me; I am restless in my complaint and I moan , Psa 55:3 because of the noise of the enemy , because of the oppression of the wicked . For they drop trouble upon me, and in anger they bear a grudge against me . Psa 55:4 My heart is in anguish within me; the terrors of death have fallen upon me . Psa 55:5 Fear and trembling come upon me, and horror overwhelms me.

**anger** — Psa 55:3  ·  `H0639:anger#1`

_Story_

In this lament the poet's enemies are described as holding a grudge against him 'in anger.' What is striking is that the anger here does not do anything — it doesn't strike or shout; it simply sits in them as a settled hostility. It has cooled from a flare-up into a fixed, nursed resentment, tied directly to the grudge they keep against him. The poem never says what first set it off, or where it lives inside them, or how fiercely it burns; it just names it as a hot dislike that has hardened into something permanent, aimed squarely at him.

_Reading_

Here anger (Hebrew aph) is typed as a status (D102 type=status) — not an act but a settled condition. Its sense (D101): 'in anger they bear a grudge.' The bearer (D105) is the enemies; the target (D107) is the psalmist, against whom the anger is held. Notably the operation (D106=none): this is the striking silence — the anger here does nothing, performs no verb; it is a state resting in the enemies rather than a movement breaking out. That absence is the finding: aph has settled, hardened from a flaring into a fixed hostility. The coupling (D112) is an explicit span-pair — anger bound to 'the grudge they cherish' — so the reading is anger fused to a nursed resentment. Locus (D116) internal:ib-state; role (D115) characteristic. The discovery (D114) names it the enemies' hot hostility, aph as the burning resentment out of which they cherish enmity — anger settled into grudge. The other silences: no source (D103 present:false) — what first kindled it is unstated; it is simply borne. No seat (D104=none) — not organ-lodged, though aph literally evokes the flaring nostril, the row records no bodily seat. No manner (D108=none), no intensity (D109 absent), no specifier (D110 absent), no effect (D111 absent), no prohibition (D113 absent): the anger is given as a bare, settled, targeted state without described texture, degree, or traced consequence. In the inner-being frame the psalmist is the object in the arena, but the reading is on the enemies' interior — a hot resentment that has cooled into a permanent grudge.

*Why this reading differs:* One of two readings of aph (H0639:anger). At Psa 55:3 aph is a status with operation:none — anger frozen into a borne grudge against the psalmist; at Psa 124:3 the same word is a state whose operation is 'have anger ignite', fury flaring to swallow its victim alive. The difference is state-vs-event and settled-vs-kindling: here anger is a cold, nursed condition; there it is a hot, igniting motion. Same word, opposite temperature of movement — evidence that aph spans both the smouldering grudge and the sudden blaze.

## Psa 58:1-5

**venom** — Psa 58:4  ·  `H2534:venom#1`

_Story_

Here the wicked are said to carry venom inside them, like the poison of a snake. The picture is not of them biting someone in this moment, but of poison simply being in them — malice as part of who they are. And it is worse than a snake's, the poem adds: they are like a deaf adder that refuses to hear the snake-charmer, so the venom can never be coaxed out or calmed. Where this hatred came from is not said — it is treated as inborn — and it is not pinned to any one place in them, or shown doing its damage yet. What we are left with is a portrait of an inner life that is toxic and stubbornly closed, poison that will not be charmed away.

_Reading_

chemah — literally heat/venom/wrath — is read here as a status (D102 type=status), a poisonous condition the wicked possess rather than an act they perform. The sense (D101) is 'venom / poison-wrath': they have venom. The bearer (D105) is the wicked. As at Psa 55, both operation (D106=none) and target (D107=none) are absent, and that double silence is the point: this venom is not shown striking anyone in particular — it is a constitutional toxicity, malice held in the inner being as a property, not yet a deed. What the row does carry is manner (D108): 'like the venom of a serpent, a deaf adder' — the one described texture, a simile of reptilian poison. The coupling (D112) is an explicit span-pair linking the venom to the wilful refusal to hear (v5): the malice is welded to incorrigibility — a deaf adder that will not be charmed. The discovery (D114) makes this explicit: malice as deadly poison, worse for being uncharmable — venom joined to stubborn deafness. Locus (D116) internal:ib-state; role (D115) characteristic. Remaining silences: no source (D103 present:false) — the venom is innate, un-originated in the text (v3 says estranged 'from the womb'); no seat (D104=none) — not organ-lodged despite chemah's heat-imagery; no intensity (D109 absent), no specifier (D110 absent), no effect (D111 absent) — the poison's outworking is not traced here; no prohibition (D113 absent). In the inner-being frame there is no human victim named as target — the reading rests wholly on the wicked's interior: a settled, serpent-like, un-charmable poison of the soul.

## Psa 59:1-5

**fierce men** — Psa 59:3  ·  `H5794:fiercemen#1`

_Story_

In this cry for rescue the poet is surrounded by fierce, powerful men who stir up trouble against him — and, he insists, he has done nothing to deserve it. The poem calls them fierce almost as a plain fact about who they are, rather than describing a particular thing they feel or do; it does not say what drives them, or trace their fierceness to any inner spark, or measure it. Really this is a description of dangerous people more than of a feeling inside them — the odd one out in this group, pointing outward at the enemies themselves. What holds it together is the strife they raise: you know their fierceness by the conflict they create around an innocent man.

_Reading_

'Fierce men' (Hebrew az, strong/fierce) is typed as a status (D102 type=status) — a quality of the enemies rather than a discrete act. The sense (D101): fierce, strong men who stir up strife. The bearer (D105) is the fierce enemies. Here the silences pile up: operation (D106=none), target (D107=none), and manner (D108=none) — three empty dimensions. This is the thinnest reading: the fierceness is asserted as a trait, but the row records no operation it performs, no object it strikes, no described manner. What anchors it is the coupling (D112), an explicit span-pair binding the fierce men to 'their stirring up strife' — their fierceness is known by the discord they raise. The discovery (D114) fills this out: mighty, ruthless foes who band against the psalmist though he has done no wrong (v3-4) — fierceness as unprovoked aggression. Most distinctive is the locus (D116): external:person — alone in this family, this reading is placed outside the inner-being interior, on the persons of the enemies rather than as an inner state. That single flag is a real finding: 'fierce men' reads less as an interior emotion than as a description of hostile agents, hovering at the family's edge (its cluster is 'Strength', not Anger). Remaining silences: no source (D103 present:false) — their fierceness is not accounted for; no seat (D104=none); no intensity (D109 absent), no specifier (D110 absent), no effect (D111 absent), no prohibition (D113 absent). In the inner-being frame this is the boundary case — the arena's aggressors named as such, their interior barely opened, their fierceness read as the outward strength of hostile men.

## Psa 76:7-12

> Psa 76:7 But you , you are to be feared ! Who can stand before you when once your anger is roused ? Psa 76:8 From the heavens you uttered judgment ; the earth feared and was still , Psa 76:9 when God arose to establish judgment , to save all the humble of the earth . Psa 76:11 Make your vows to the Lord your God and perform them; let all around him bring gifts to him who is to be feared , Psa 76:12 who cuts off the spirit of princes , who is to be feared by the kings of the earth .

**wrath** — Psa 76:10  ·  `H2534:wrath#1`

_Story_

This verse makes a bold claim: even human rage ends up praising God. The furious anger of people, which means to do harm, is turned around so that it serves God's glory instead — it never quite reaches the target it aimed at, because it is redirected into praise. Whatever fury is left over that cannot be used this way, the poem says, God simply holds back, like a belt cinched tight. It does not explain what makes people rage, or where the anger sits, or how intense it is; the whole interest is in its being overruled. It is the one place here where this hot, dangerous feeling is neither forbidden nor let loose, but quietly put to work.

_Reading_

chemah appears again, now typed as a state (D102 type=state), 'the wrath of man.' The bearer (D105) is man / humanity — this is human fury in general. What sets this reading apart is the operation (D106), the one anchor where wrath's motion is not 'none' but a span-linked event: 'turned to praise God.' The wrath does not reach its own intended object; instead it is redirected — its outworking is that even hostile rage is made to serve God's glory (manner, D108). The target (D107=none) is itself telling: the wrath's intended victim is not registered, because the point is that its trajectory is hijacked — it lands not where it aimed but on praise. The coupling (D112) is an explicit span-pair binding the wrath to 'the praise it is made to render.' The discovery (D114) draws it out: human fury, even in rebellion, God turns to his own honour; chemah overruled becomes an instrument of praise, with a residue God simply restrains (the 'remnant of wrath', v10b). Locus (D116) internal:ib-state; role (D115) characteristic. The silences: no source (D103 present:false) — what kindles man's wrath is unstated, taken as given; no seat (D104=none); no intensity (D109 absent), no specifier (D110 absent), no effect flagged separately (D111 absent) though the operation itself carries the outcome; no prohibition (D113 absent). In the inner-being frame God is the arena that overrules, but the reading holds on the human interior: wrath as a hot state whose native motion is thwarted and turned — the rare case where the family's dark heat is neither condemned nor indulged but conscripted, and its surplus leashed.

**provoked** — Psa 78:41  ·  `H8428:provok#1`

_Story_

Here the people are described as provoking God — actually wounding him — again and again in the wilderness. This is anger seen from the other side: not a feeling boiling up in them, but something they do to another, hurting the Holy One of Israel by the limits they keep placing on him. It goes hand in hand with their constant testing of him. The poem does not dwell on what was churning inside them, or where the impulse sat, or how it felt; it fixes on the act and its victim. It is the inner life shown through the damage it inflicts, aimed outward at God.

_Reading_

With tavah the family shifts from a felt state to an action (D102 type=action): to provoke, and specifically to pain or wound. The sense (D101) is 'provoke / pain.' The bearer (D105) is 'they' (Israel in the wilderness); the operation (D106) is to provoke / wound; the target (D107) is the Holy One of Israel. Unlike the settled-status readings, here the movement is fully traced: an agent performs an act of provocation upon an object. The coupling (D112) pairs it with 'the testing' — the provoking is bound up with their repeated testing of God (v41). The discovery (D114) reads it as the wounding of the Holy One by the limits they set on him. The locus (D116) is external:god — the movement is directed outward, at God, rather than resting as an inner state; this is provocation-as-deed, its centre of gravity in the arena. Role (D115) characteristic. The silences: no source (D103 present:false) — the inner spring of the provocation (unbelief, hardness) is not named in this row; no seat (D104=none) — not organ-lodged; no manner (D108=none) — the how of the provoking is unstated; no intensity (D109 absent), no specifier (D110 absent), no effect (D111 absent) — the outcome (God's restrained anger, v38) is not traced in this anchor; no prohibition (D113 absent). In the inner-being frame this is one of the 'provoke' cluster where the human interior is read chiefly through what it does to God: the inner being expressed as an act that wounds, its emotional root left implicit and its locus thrown outward onto its divine object.

This is the inner life read through a chosen provocation aimed outward.

**provoked** — Psa 106:29  ·  `H3707:provok#1`

_Story_

At Peor the people provoke God to anger through their corrupt worship, and this time the poem ties the provocation straight to what follows: a deadly plague breaks out, halted only when Phinehas steps in. As with the other scenes of provoking, the interest is not in a feeling swelling inside them but in the act aimed at God and the judgement it brings down. Their deeds are the means by which they rouse him. The poem does not describe an inner mood, or where it sat, or how fierce it was; it binds the provocation to its terrible result. It is the inner life read through a deed that calls down disaster.

**angry** — Psa 112:10  ·  `H3707:angry#1`

_Story_

At the very end of this psalm about the blessed, godly person, we glimpse the wicked man watching — and he is furious. Seeing the good that comes to the one who fears God, his envy curdles into rage. His anger shows itself in his body: he grinds his teeth. But then it turns on him and undoes him — he 'melts away', and everything he wanted comes to nothing. The poem does not spell out where the anger sits or how hot it burns; it shows the sequence: he sees, he seethes, he gnashes, he dissolves. It is resentment at someone else's blessing that ends up destroying the resenter.

**anger** — Psa 124:3  ·  `H0639:anger#2`

_Story_

This short song looks back on a narrow escape: if God had not been on their side, the poet says, their enemies' anger — once it flared up — would have swallowed them alive. Here the anger is caught in the moment of catching fire, blazing up to devour them like a flood. Unlike the settled grudge pictured elsewhere, this is anger igniting, reaching hungrily for its victim. The poem does not say what lit it, or where it sits, or how hot it grew; it ties the flaring fury straight to the near-disaster it almost caused. What saved them was not that the anger cooled, but that God stood with them against it.

_Reading_

Bearer (D105) the enemies; operation (D106) 'have anger ignite' — an event of kindling, fury flaring up; target (D107) 'to devour the people' — the anger reaches toward swallowing its victim alive. The coupling (D112) is flagged 'internal:ib-state' — the igniting anger coupled to the enemies' inner condition. The discovery (D114) reads v3: when their anger was kindled against us — hostile fury igniting, wrath flaring to the point of swallowing its victim alive. The locus row (D116) carries 'paired with the swallowing alive' — the anger bound to its devouring outcome, the near-catastrophe from which only the LORD's being 'on our side' delivered (v1-2). Role (D115) characteristic. The silences: no source (D103 present:false) — what kindled it is unstated; no seat (D104=none); no manner (D108=none); no intensity (D109 absent) — though 'kindled' and 'swallow alive' imply great heat, no degree is flagged; no specifier (D110 absent), no separate effect (D111 absent) though the devouring appears in the locus flag; no prohibition (D113 absent). *Why this reading differs:* The second of two aph readings. At Psa 55:3 aph is a status with operation:none — anger frozen into a nursed grudge against the psalmist; here at Psa 124:3 aph is a state whose operation is 'have anger ignite', a kindling fury reaching to swallow its victim alive. The difference is settled-vs-igniting and static-vs-devouring: there anger has cooled into a borne resentment; here it flares as an active blaze. The same word thus spans both temperatures — smouldering grudge and sudden, consuming fire — and that range is itself the finding about how aph operates.
