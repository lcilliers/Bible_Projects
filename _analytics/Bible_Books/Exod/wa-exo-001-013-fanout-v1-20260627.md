**Spiritual and Psychological Forms of Slavery in Scripture**

Beyond physical bondage, Scripture describes slavery as a spiritual and psychological condition—a loss of freedom rooted in one’s allegiance. Anyone who practices sin becomes enslaved to sin (John 8:34), a bondage that operates through internal compulsion rather than external chains. This slavery manifests when people present themselves as obedient to sin, which leads to death, rather than to obedience, which leads to righteousness (Rom 6:16–23).

The Apostle Paul illustrates this internal conflict vividly. He describes being “sold under sin,” unable to do what he wants but instead doing what he hates (Rom 7:14–25)—a condition where one’s will is held captive by competing powers. He experiences “another law waging war against the law of my mind and making me captive to the law of sin” (Rom 7:14–25), revealing that slavery operates at the level of competing desires and authorities.

Scripture identifies several distinct forms of spiritual slavery:

* **Slavery to sin and passions**: Believers were once “slaves to various passions and pleasures” (Titus 3:3), and those who promise freedom while enslaved to corruption demonstrate that “whatever overcomes a person, to that he is enslaved” (2 Pet 2:19).
* **Slavery to fear**: Some are “subject to lifelong slavery” through fear of death (Heb 2:14–15), while believers receive not “the spirit of slavery to fall back into fear” (Rom 8:15).
* **Slavery to worldly principles**: Believers were once “enslaved to the elementary principles of the world” and “enslaved to those that by nature are not gods” (Gal 4:3–9).
* **Slavery to legalism**: Legalism itself constitutes a kind of slavery[1].

Paradoxically, Scripture also presents a redemptive slavery: Those freed from sin have “become slaves of righteousness” (Rom 6:16–23), a willing submission that produces sanctification rather than death. Believers freed from sin and enslaved to God experience “the fruit of holiness” with eternal life as the result, whereas slaves of sin receive death as their compensation[2].

## 1. The same Hebrew word (the precise lexical answer)

The term carrying "ruthlessly" in Exodus 1:13 is **פֶּרֶךְ (*perek*, Strong's H6531)** — harshness, rigour, severe/crushing labour. It's a rare word, appearing only **six times** in the Hebrew Bible:

- **Exodus 1:13** — the Egyptians work Israel *bə-farek* (with rigour)
- **Exodus 1:14** — repeated in the very next verse, framing the whole oppression
- **Leviticus 25:43** — "you shall not rule over him *ruthlessly*"
- **Leviticus 25:46** — the same prohibition, brother over brother
- **Leviticus 25:53** — again, of a hired/bondservant
- **Ezekiel 34:4** — the shepherds of Israel ruled the flock *with harshness*

The theological shape here is striking and probably the most interesting part of your question: **the three Leviticus uses deliberately forbid Israel from doing to one another exactly what Egypt did to them.** The word that names Egypt's cruelty in Exodus becomes the word for the cruelty Israel is *commanded not to imitate*. Ezekiel 34:4 then turns the indictment inward — Israel's own leaders are charged with *perek*, the Egyptian sin.

Worth noting for your own method: the ESV renders *perek* as "ruthlessly" in Exodus and Leviticus but as "**harshness**" in Ezekiel 34:4. Same lemma, different English string — so the English word won't reliably track the Hebrew if you search by translation.

## What the text actually says (Exod 1:13–14)

The passage uses a cluster of loaded words:

- **עבד (ʿavad)** — "made them serve/work as slaves" (v.13, hiphil). This is *the* core service-and-slavery root. It matters enormously, because it is the same verb used for serving/worshipping God. The whole Exodus turns on it: Pharaoh's repeated demand is reversed by YHWH's "let my people go **that they may serve (ʿavad) me**" (Exod 4:23; 7:16; 8:1; 9:1; 10:3). Scripture never frames the question as *whether* Israel will serve, but *whom*. Bondage to Pharaoh is the counterfeit of service to God.
- **בְּפָרֶךְ (be-pharekh)** — "with rigour / ruthlessly" (vv.13–14). This same rare word reappears in Lev 25:43, 46, 53, where Israel is *forbidden* to rule a fellow Israelite "with rigour (perek)." So the law deliberately names Egypt's defining cruelty and prohibits it among the redeemed.
- **מרר (marar)** — "made bitter" (v.14), the root behind the Passover *maror* (bitter herbs). The bitterness is liturgically remembered, not just narrated.
- **עֲבֹדָה קָשָׁה (ʿavodah qashah)** — "hard service." Note *ʿavodah* again doubles as the word for cultic *worship*.
- The labour is both construction (**mortar and brick**, for the store-cities of v.11) and agricultural ("all kinds of field work") — total economic conscription. The taskmasters of v.11 are *sarei missim*, overseers of **mas** (corvée/forced state labour).

## Why slavery happens, in the text's own terms

Two answers operate at once. At the narrative level, the cause is **Pharaoh's fear**: Israel "multiplied and grew strong," and Pharaoh fears they will side with enemies (1:9–10). Slavery here is a security policy born of fear of the other — and it escalates from labour (v.11) to crushing labour (vv.13–14) to infanticide (vv.16, 22). Scripture depicts enslavement as the downward logic of fear-driven control.

At the theological level, the bondage is the foretold fulfilment of Gen 15:13–14, where God tells Abraham his offspring will be "servants" and "afflicted" in a land not theirs for 400 years — *but* "I will bring judgment on the nation they serve." So the text frames the slavery as permitted and foreknown, with judgment and redemption already promised against it.

## To what end

Three ends emerge:

1. **Redemption and identity.** The slavery is the dark ground against which the Exodus becomes Israel's defining act of self-understanding. "I am the LORD your God who brought you out of the house of slavery" prefaces the Decalogue (Exod 20:2).

2. **An ethical memory.** "You were slaves in Egypt" becomes the recurring *motive clause* for compassion toward the vulnerable — the sojourner, the bonded servant, the poor (Exod 22:21; 23:9; Lev 19:34; Deut 5:15; 15:15; 16:12; 24:18, 22). The experience is meant to be metabolised into law that limits the very thing Israel suffered.

3. **Reoriented service.** Per the *ʿavad* wordplay, the goal of deliverance is not autonomy but covenant worship — service to God replacing service to Pharaoh.

## Does scripture give insight into "what slavery is"?

Yes, but it does so by *distinguishing registers* rather than treating "slavery" as one flat thing. This is where I'd be most careful, and where readings diverge — I'll separate what the text states from what is interpreted.

**The text distinguishes at least:**

- **Egyptian-style state/chattel bondage** — the paradigm of *evil*. The whole narrative is its condemnation.
- **Hebrew debt-servitude** (Exod 21:2–6; Deut 15:12–18; Lev 25) — time-limited (release in the seventh year), with protections and a release-gift; closer to bonded labour discharging debt or poverty than to chattel ownership.
- **Foreign/captive slavery** (Lev 25:44–46) — permitted and more permanent. This is the hardest text and the one interpreters wrestle with most.

**Two limiting principles the text builds in:**

- **Man-stealing is a capital crime** (Exod 21:16; Deut 24:7), and the NT echoes it: 1 Tim 1:10 lists *andrapodistai* (slave-traders / men-stealers) among the lawless. On a plain reading this condemns the kidnap-and-sell model that defined the trans-Atlantic trade — which is one reason many argue biblical servitude and modern race-based chattel slavery are *categorically different institutions*, not the same thing regulated.
- **Belonging to God relativises ownership by humans.** Lev 25:42, 55: Israelites "are my servants, whom I brought out of Egypt; they shall not be sold as slaves." Slavery to God sets a ceiling on slavery to any human master.

**New Testament register.** Paul both works within the institution and undercuts it: he tells slaves to gain freedom if they can and "do not become slaves of men" (1 Cor 7:21–23); he sends Onesimus back to be received "no longer as a slave but as a beloved brother" (Phlm 16); "neither slave nor free" in Christ (Gal 3:28); and theologically he recasts *all* believers as freed from slavery to sin into slavery to righteousness/Christ (Rom 6). The household codes (Eph 6; Col 3; 1 Pet 2) address masters and slaves directly.

## Where the perspectives genuinely diverge (flagging, per your request)

This is contested ground, and I don't want to smuggle one view in as "the" biblical position:

- **Trajectory / redemptive-movement reading** (e.g. the broadly abolitionist and "redemptive-hermeneutic" school, associated with figures like William Webb): the texts *regulate and humanise* an existing institution while planting principles — imago Dei, the Exodus memory, man-stealing prohibition, "no longer a slave but a brother" — that point beyond slavery toward its abolition. The codes are accommodation-with-subversion.
- **Plain-permission reading**: the texts permit and regulate slavery (including the difficult Lev 25:44–46) without commanding abolition; this is read either as honest acknowledgment that the Bible reflects its ancient context, or — historically — was misused by 18th–19th c. pro-slavery apologists citing these same passages.
- **Categorical-distinction reading**: "slavery" in the English Bible flattens several different *ʿeved*/*doulos* realities; the prophetic-and-Exodus condemnation of oppressive bondage and the regulated debt-servitude are different things, and conflating them with modern chattel slavery is a translation-driven error.

One self-critical thread worth noting, since it bears on your question of "what slavery is": Israel, redeemed *from* forced labour (*mas*), later *imposes* corvée under Solomon (1 Kings 5:13–14; 9:15), and that very *mas* becomes the grievance that splits the kingdom (1 Kings 12). Scripture turns the Egypt-critique back on Israel itself — which suggests the Bible treats coercive forced labour as a recurring temptation of *power*, not merely a foreign Egyptian vice.

The Bible doesn't lay out a single systematic "theory of cruelty," but it names several roots fairly explicitly, and they cluster in an interesting way. Let me separate what the text states directly from what is synthesis on my part, since that distinction matters for your kind of work.

## Start with Exodus 1 itself — the motive is given

Before going wide, it's worth noting that the *perek* of 1:13 isn't unexplained. The narrative supplies a causal chain in the preceding verses:

- 1:8 — a new king "who did not know Joseph" (severed relationship / lost memory of covenant kindness)
- 1:9 — he perceives Israel as "too many and too mighty" (perceived threat)
- 1:10 — "Come, let us deal shrewdly with them... lest they multiply, and if war breaks out, join our enemies" (**fear of the other + self-preservation + political calculation**)
- 1:11–14 — *therefore* taskmasters and crushing labour

So in the originating text, ruthlessness springs from **fear of a perceived threat to power and security, joined to a refusal to honour an existing relationship**. That's a tight, defensible reading of the passage's own logic.

## The recurring causes the Bible names

**1. The heart / inner being as the wellspring (the most fundamental answer).**
Scripture locates the source *inside* the person, not in circumstance. Genesis 6:5 — every intention of the heart "only evil continually"; Jeremiah 17:9 — the heart "deceitful... and desperately sick"; and Jesus most explicitly in Mark 7:21–23 — "from within, out of the heart... come evil thoughts, murder..." This is the closest thing to a stated origin: cruelty flows *out of* the inner being rather than being imposed from without. (Given your WA work, note this is an explicit movement — the heart as antecedent/source, the act as effect.)

**2. Absence of the fear of God (the most repeated explicit causal link).**
This thread is unusually direct — the text actually states the connection:
- Genesis 20:11 — Abraham reasons, "There is no fear of God at all in this place, and they will kill me." (No fear of God → expectation of violence.)
- Deuteronomy 25:18 — Amalek struck the faint and weary stragglers, "and he did not fear God." (Ruthlessness toward the weak *explicitly* tied to not fearing God.)
- Psalm 36:1 — "there is no fear of God before his eyes," introducing a portrait of the wicked; quoted at Romans 3:18 as a summary of human corruption.

This is the strongest *stated* causal pattern: the fear of God functions as the restraint, and its absence removes the brake.

**3. Fierce anger / wrath.**
Two texts make this link explicitly:
- Genesis 49:7 — of Simeon and Levi (the Shechem massacre): "Cursed be their anger, for it is fierce, and their wrath, for it is cruel."
- Proverbs 27:4 — "Wrath is cruel, anger is overwhelming."

**4. Greed / covetousness.**
- 1 Timothy 6:10 — love of money "a root of all kinds of evils."
- Micah 2:1–2 — they "covet fields and seize them," oppressing households; the Naboth's-vineyard episode (1 Kings 21) is the narrative case study — coveting leads to judicial murder.

**5. Pride / arrogance / self-sufficiency.**
The "ruthless" (*arits*) of Isaiah are typically the arrogant (e.g. Isaiah 13:11 pairs "the arrogant" and "the ruthless"). Habakkuk 1:7, 11 portrays a nation whose "justice and dignity go forth from themselves" and "whose own might is their god" — cruelty as the fruit of self-deification, might-makes-right.

## The synthesis the New Testament offers

Romans 1:28–31 ties several threads together: when people suppress the truth about God, "God gave them up to a debased mind," and the resulting vice list includes "heartless, ruthless" (Greek *aneleēmōn*). Here ruthlessness is framed as a *symptom* of being handed over once God is pushed out of view — which loops back to the "no fear of God" thread above. ## 8. The FULL inner-being window (all aspects — researcher direction, 2026-06-27)
The first pass stopped at *ruthlessness*. Reading Exo 1:13 with its tight neighbour 1:14, the morphology — especially the **causative stems** — exposes a *coupled causal chain* of several inner-being aspects, not one:

- **Driver [needs-corroboration]** — *dread* (the Egyptians' fear of Israel, v.12) → the cruelty. The actor's inner state as antecedent.
- **Inner state / manner [observation]** — *ruthlessness* (`perek`, *be-perek* "with harshness", `HNcmsa`+prep) — the cruelty qualifying the act.
- **Operation: ENSLAVEMENT [grounded]** — `abad` in the **HIPHIL** (`HVhw3mp` = causative): the Egyptians **cause** Israel to serve. *Enslavement is the actor producing a state* — the causative stem is the proof. It expresses, in the enslaver, the will to subjugate.
- **Coupling: ruthlessness ⟷ enslavement [grounded]** — *be-perek* (manner) grammatically modifies the Hiphil `abad`: ruthlessness **is the manner of** the causative enslavement. One coupled movement (ruthless-enslavement), not two facts. Both instantiate *power-over-the-weak*.
- **Produced state [grounded]** — v.14: `marar` in the **PIEL** (`HVpw3mp` = factitive/causative): the enslavement **causes** their lives to be *bitter*. The affective fruit in the enslaved — bitterness, caused.
- **Realised state [observation]** — v.14: `abad` **Qal** (`HVqp3cp`): Israel *serves* — the enslaved condition realised.
- **Experiencers / patients [observation]** — *the people of Israel*: acted upon; they enter the enslaved + embittered state.

**The full window:** *dread (driver) → ruthlessness (manner) **coupled-to** enslavement (Hiphil = causative operation) → bitterness (Piel = produced affective state) → serving (Qal = realised state)*, borne by Israel (patients). A coupled causal chain, every link morphologically grounded.
