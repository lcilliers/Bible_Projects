## Purpose

This document is the cluster science review for M55. It is a pre-analytical asset, produced before the cluster's Session B analysis, standing as reference for the D9.1.1/D9.2.1/D11.2.1/D12.1.1 catalogue prompts. M55 is a large and internally diverse cluster (61 glosses) that absorbed the former M27 (Evil, Wickedness and Abomination); its vocabulary spans physical/relational destruction and ruin, idolatry, error and culpable wrongdoing, and a small residual death-adjacent vocabulary (the Rephaim/"shade" term). The document sections this diversity by phenomenon rather than forcing a single frame across it.

## Science sections

### Section 1 — Destruction, ruin, and devastation

**Primary field:** Trauma and disaster psychology / behavioural science

**Glosses covered:**
destruction (apōleia), destruction (kathairesis), destruction (rēgma), devastation (erēmōsis), harmful (blaberos), ruin (katastrofē), ruin (suntrimma), to destroy/lodge (kataluō), to ruin (kataskaptō), be desolate: appalled (sha.mem), destruction (o.ved), destruction (a.vad.don), destruction (av.dan), destruction (ov.dan), destruction (che.vel), destruction (cha.loph), destruction (mash.chit), destruction (mash.chet), destruction (qe.tev), destruction (qo.tev), destruction (tav.lit), devastation (me.sham.mah), devastation (shet), devastation (sho.ah), devastation (she.ma.mah), ruin (kid), ruin (mid.cheh), ruin (map.pa.lah), ruin (map.pe.lah), ruin (av.vah), ruin (i), ruin (she.iy.yah), ruin (te.vu.sah), to ruin (la.vat), to ruin (sha.dad), to ruin (sha.chat), to silence: destroyed (da.mam)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
This is by far the largest single register in the cluster — 36 distinct terms clustered around physical and relational ruin, a density that itself reflects the biblical corpus's extensive vocabulary for catastrophic loss (war, famine, exile, the collapse of households and nations). Disaster and trauma psychology (Herman, *Trauma and Recovery*, 1992; van der Kolk, *The Body Keeps the Score*, 2014) documents the psychological aftermath of large-scale destructive events — loss of safety, assumptive-world shattering (Janoff-Bulman's shattered-assumptions theory, 1992), and complicated grief where the loss is total and communal rather than individual. Community-level disaster research (Erikson, *Everything in Its Path*, 1976, on the Buffalo Creek flood) documents that destruction of a whole community's physical and social fabric produces a distinct form of collective trauma beyond the sum of individual losses — directly relevant to a vocabulary largely produced in contexts of national conquest, exile, and city-destruction. Desolation/appalment (sha.mem — to be desolate, appalled, devastated) names both the state of a devastated place and the corresponding human emotional state, a dual usage the trauma literature's concept of "landscape trauma" or "solastalgia" (Albrecht, 2005 — distress caused by environmental destruction of one's home place) partially illuminates.

**Emergence — what causes destruction and ruin, and the psychological state of devastation, to arise:**
Physical destruction arises from external forces (military conquest, natural disaster, economic collapse) or internal collapse (structural failure, moral/social breakdown leading to a community's unravelling). The corresponding psychological devastation arises when the destruction is total enough, or affects a sufficiently central attachment (home, land, family, nation, temple), to shatter the assumptive world — the basic beliefs about safety, meaning, and continuity that ordinarily structure experience (Janoff-Bulman). Sha.mem's dual usage (of both place and person) reflects a documented psychological reality: destroyed places produce a correspondingly desolate inner state in those who witness or inhabit them.

**Reduction and absence — what causes recovery from ruin and devastation:**
Trauma and disaster recovery research identifies restored safety, social support and communal rebuilding, meaning-making (the capacity to integrate the loss into a coherent, forward-looking narrative), and — critically for community-level destruction — the physical rebuilding of place itself as the primary pathways to recovery. Recovery is markedly slower and less complete where destruction is total, where it is inflicted deliberately by other humans (versus natural disaster), and where the pre-existing social fabric that would normally support recovery has itself been destroyed.

**Location — where destruction/devastation is seated:**
Primarily an event in the external world (physical, social, or national ruin) with a documented correlate psychological state in those affected — desolation, appalment, the shattering of basic assumptions about safety and continuity. It is not, in itself, an inner-being characteristic in the way most of the programme's clusters name one; its relevance to the programme is chiefly through the devastated inner state that destruction produces, and through moral vocabulary (destruction as consequence of wrongdoing) that shades into Sections 2–3 below.

**External influences:**
*Circumstances* are definitionally primary — destruction and ruin are, by the nature of this vocabulary, externally caused events rather than internal dispositions. *Social and political:* Societies subject to repeated invasion, conquest, or catastrophic loss (the historical experience underlying much of this vocabulary's biblical usage) develop cultural forms — lament, communal memory practices — specifically adapted to processing collective devastation. *Hereditary/developmental:* Prior experience of loss and destruction shapes both vulnerability to and resilience under subsequent devastating events (stress-inoculation effects can cut either way depending on whether prior coping was adaptive or overwhelmed).

**Coverage assessment:** Good for the psychological aftermath of destruction (trauma and disaster psychology); the destruction/ruin events themselves are more the subject-matter of history and political science than psychology proper, which the review notes rather than forces into a psychological frame it does not fit.

### Section 2 — Idolatry and disordered ultimate attachment

**Primary field:** Psychology of religion / addiction and behavioural science

**Glosses covered:**
idol (eidōlon), idolater (eidōlolatrēs), idolatrous (kateidōlos), idolatry (eidōlolatreia), sacrificed to idols (eidōlothutos), idol (a.tsav), idol (e.lil), idol (se.mel), idol (pa.sil), idol (pe.sel)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Psychology of religion does not typically treat idolatry as a category in its own right, but two adjacent literatures bear directly on it. Tillich's concept of "ultimate concern" (*Dynamics of Faith*, 1957), taken up in the psychology of meaning and values, identifies the human tendency to organise life around a supreme value or object of devotion — and treats misdirected ultimate concern (investing final, unconditional weight in a finite object — wealth, nation, self, pleasure) as a structural human vulnerability rather than a fringe pathology. Addiction medicine's reward-substitution model (Volkow and colleagues on dopaminergic reward learning) documents how the brain's reward and salience systems can become organised around a substitute object that comes to command attention, sacrifice, and loyalty disproportionate to its actual capacity to deliver the good sought — a functional, though secularised, parallel to the biblical critique of idols as unable to deliver what devotion to them seeks. Kirkpatrick's attachment theory of religion (*Attachment, Evolution, and the Psychology of Religion*, 2005) treats the object of ultimate religious attachment as functioning psychologically like any other attachment figure — raising the empirical question of what happens, psychologically, when that attachment figure is an object incapable of the responsiveness a genuine attachment relationship requires.

**Emergence — what causes idolatry to arise:**
On the ultimate-concern and attachment-substitution models, idolatry arises when the human need for a secure, ultimate object of trust and devotion — a need the attachment and meaning literatures both treat as basic rather than optional — is directed toward an object that cannot bear that weight. This can occur through cultural transmission (inherited devotional practice), through the reward-substitution pathway (a lesser good becoming disproportionately salient through repeated reinforcement), or through crisis (turning to a controllable, tangible object when the true object of ultimate trust feels absent or unreliable).

**Reduction and absence:** The literature on ultimate concern and reward substitution suggests that redirected devotion to a substitute object diminishes primarily through the substitute's own repeated failure to deliver what was sought (a documented feature of addiction cycles — escalating investment without proportionate return) combined with the availability of a trustworthy alternative object of ultimate devotion.

**Location:** Motivational and characterological at the deepest level — idolatry as this literature construes it is not a discrete emotion or belief but an organisation of the whole person's devotion, attention, and sacrifice around a given object; it recruits the same reward and attachment neurocircuitry documented for other forms of devotion (see M50/M51) redirected toward an object incapable of reciprocating.

**External influences:** *Cultural:* Idolatrous practice in the biblical world was overwhelmingly a matter of inherited communal religion rather than individual choice — cultural transmission is the primary vector. *Circumstances:* Periods of felt abandonment or crisis are documented (in both the biblical narrative and general psychology-of-religion research) as occasions when substitute objects of devotion gain particular appeal.

**Coverage assessment:** Thin-to-moderate, and indirect. No science literature treats "idolatry" as its own category; the ultimate-concern and addiction-reward-substitution frameworks provide genuine but partial illumination of the underlying human dynamic, while the specific ancient religious practices this vocabulary names (image-making, cultic sacrifice) are primarily the subject of anthropology and history of religion rather than psychology.

### Section 3 — Error, transgression, and culpable wrongdoing

**Primary field:** Moral and legal psychology / criminology

**Glosses covered:**
crime (adikēma), crime (radiourgēma), error (agnoēma), error (planē), liable for (enochos), ungodlinessness (asebeia), crime (cha.vu.la), error (me.shu.gah), error (she.gi.ah), error (to.ho.lah), error (to.ah), revolt (sa.rah)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Moral and legal psychology distinguishes culpability levels by mental state — the mens rea framework distinguishes intentional wrongdoing from negligent, reckless, or genuinely mistaken action, a distinction that this section's vocabulary partially mirrors: error/agnoēma (a wrong done in ignorance) and planē (a wandering, a going-astray, closer to deception or drift than deliberate violation) sit differently on the culpability spectrum than crime/adikēma (a definite wrong deliberately committed) and asebeia (irreverence, active ungodliness). Fischer and Ravizza's work on moral responsibility (*Responsibility and Control*, 1998) formalises the philosophical distinctions underlying this spectrum; criminology's routine-activity and rational-choice theories (Cohen and Felson, 1979; Cornish and Clarke, 1986) address the situational conditions under which culpable wrongdoing (as opposed to error) occurs. Liability (enochos — held liable, answerable) names the legal-relational consequence that follows once an act is judged culpable, a category well developed in both criminal-justice psychology and, in the biblical material's own frame, cultic/covenantal liability.

**Emergence — what causes error versus deliberate wrongdoing to arise:**
Error (agnoēma, she.gi.ah — an unwitting sin) arises from genuine ignorance, oversight, or unintentional deviation — its psychological profile is closer to ordinary human fallibility than to moral failure proper. Deliberate crime and revolt (sa.rah) arise through the same pathways addressed under M55's absorbed M27 territory and the broader moral-disengagement literature (Bandura): rationalisation, diffusion of responsibility, and the progressive erosion of restraint through repeated small violations. Asebeia (active irreverence/ungodliness) names a settled orientation rather than a discrete act — closer to a character disposition than to any single transgression.

**Reduction and absence — what causes wrongdoing/error to be addressed or reduced:**
For error specifically, remedy typically requires only correction and, in many legal and cultic systems (including the biblical sacrificial system for unwitting sin), a proportionate and less severe response than for deliberate wrongdoing — a distinction criminal-justice psychology also preserves (sentencing that weighs intent). Deliberate wrongdoing is reduced through the standard moral-psychology mechanisms: accountability structures, restored moral self-monitoring, and — per Section 1's trauma material — communities strong enough to hold members accountable without themselves collapsing under repeated violation.

**Location — where culpability is seated:**
Cognitive (what the person knew or believed), volitional (what the person intended), and characterological (asebeia as settled disposition) — culpability psychology is inherently concerned with locating an act along exactly these dimensions, which is why the mens rea framework maps as well as it does onto this section's error/crime distinction.

**External influences:** *Circumstances:* Poverty, social chaos, and weak accountability structures are documented criminogenic factors (routine-activity theory) that increase the incidence of deliberate wrongdoing; genuine error rates are less circumstantially driven and more a function of information availability and cognitive load. *Cultural/legal:* What counts as a culpable "crime" versus an excusable "error" is partly culturally and legally constructed, though core categories (intentional harm versus honest mistake) show cross-cultural consistency.

**Coverage assessment:** Good. Moral responsibility, legal psychology, and criminology provide well-developed and directly applicable frameworks for distinguishing this section's error and crime vocabulary.

### Section 4 — The Rephaim and death-adjacent language

**Primary field:** Thanatology / anthropology of death (thin coverage)

**Glosses covered:**
shade (re.pha.im)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Re.pha.im names the shades of the dead in Sheol — a category belonging to the biblical material's own conception of the afterlife rather than to any documented human psychological phenomenon. Thanatology (the scientific study of death, dying, and bereavement — Kübler-Ross onward) addresses the living's psychological experience of death and loss but has no descriptive purchase on claims about the ontological status of the dead themselves.

**Coverage assessment:** Effectively outside science's reach; see outside-science inventory.

## Outside-science inventory

- **shade (re.pha.im)** — the biblical conception of departed shades in Sheol is a claim about the state of the dead, not a describable psychological phenomenon in the living; science has no descriptive purchase on it.
- **idolatry as covenant infidelity** — beyond the disordered-attachment dynamic addressed in Section 2, idolatry in the biblical material is centrally a covenantal offence (infidelity to Yahweh specifically, in violation of a specific relationship) rather than merely a generic psychological misdirection of devotion; the covenantal and theological weight of "other gods before me" exceeds what ultimate-concern psychology reaches.
- **destruction/devastation as divine judgment** — where ruin and devastation in this cluster's vocabulary are presented as God's own judicial action against sin (rather than as a neutral natural or historical event), the theological claim of divine agency in judgment is outside science's descriptive scope; disaster and trauma psychology addresses the human experience of devastation, not its claimed ultimate cause or moral meaning.
- **ungodliness (asebeia) as a settled orientation against God** — as with M16's foolishness, this names a fundamental posture of irreverence toward the Creator rather than a cognitive or behavioural deficit addressable by moral psychology's ordinary categories.

**Observation:** M55 is the largest and most heterogeneous cluster in this batch. Its science coverage is correspondingly uneven: strong for the psychological aftermath of destruction (Section 1) and for the culpability distinctions in Section 3, thin and indirect for idolatry (Section 2), and essentially absent for the Rephaim vocabulary (Section 4). The unifying theological thread across all four sections — that destruction, idolatry, and culpable wrongdoing are bound together in the biblical material as consequence, cause, and expression of a single departure from right relationship with God — is not a thread the secular literature draws, since it treats each phenomenon within its own separate field.

## D9.1.1 / D9.2.1 / D11.2.1 / D12.1.1 prompt reference notes

**D9.1.1 — Physiological/neural substrate:** The devastation/appalment state documented in Section 1 shows the hallmark physiology of severe, often complicated grief and trauma (HPA-axis dysregulation, hypervigilance or numbing). Idolatry-as-disordered-attachment (Section 2) recruits ordinary reward and attachment circuitry (dopaminergic salience, oxytocin-mediated bonding) directed at a non-reciprocating object. No distinct substrate is documented for error/culpability judgments (Section 3) beyond the general moral-cognition network (ventromedial prefrontal cortex, temporoparietal junction) involved in intent attribution generally.

**D9.2.1 — Generational transmission:** Idolatrous practice (Section 2) is, per the ancient evidence, overwhelmingly transmitted generationally through inherited communal religion — a pattern the biblical material's own repeated concern with "the sins of the fathers" in respect of idolatry independently attests. Collective destruction and devastation (Section 1) also transmits generationally through communal memory and, where trauma is severe and unresolved, through documented epigenetic and psychosocial mechanisms of intergenerational trauma transmission (Yehuda's research on descendants of Holocaust survivors is the most studied example of this general mechanism). Error and culpable wrongdoing (Section 3) show weaker direct generational transmission, mediated mainly through modelling and moral formation rather than a distinct heritable or cultural-inheritance pathway specific to this category.

**D11.2.1 — Innate endowment vs. acquired:** None of this cluster's core phenomena are innate in any direct sense; destruction/devastation are events, not dispositions; idolatrous attachment is an acquired misdirection of an innate attachment/devotion capacity (the capacity itself is innate per attachment theory, its object is acquired); culpable wrongdoing versus honest error is substantially a matter of acquired knowledge, formation, and circumstance rather than innate difference, though general impulse-control and moral-reasoning capacity (which shape susceptibility to wrongdoing) do have a heritable component.

**D12.1.1 — Social/behavioural manifestation:** Destruction and devastation manifest observably at the level of place, community, and institution before they manifest as an individual psychological state. Idolatry manifests behaviourally as disproportionate time, resource, and ultimate loyalty directed toward a finite object. Error and crime manifest, respectively, as unintentional deviation discoverable on examination and as deliberate, culpable violation typically accompanied by concealment or rationalisation — a behavioural distinction Session B may find useful in testing which register a given occurrence of this cluster's error/crime vocabulary belongs to.
