## Science sections

### Section 1 — Remembering: recall, encoding, and retrieval

**Primary field:** Cognitive psychology — memory research

**Glosses covered:**
to remember (H2142 zakhar, G3403 mnaomai, G3415 mimnēskomai, G3421 mnēmoneuō), to return: recall (H7725N shuv)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Tulving's episodic and semantic memory distinction (*Elements of Episodic Memory*, 1983) is foundational: episodic memory is autobiographical, contextually located, and personally experienced; semantic memory is factual, context-free, and impersonal. Remembrance (zakhar) in the biblical material typically involves episodic memory — remembering specific events in history with their personal and covenantal significance, not merely abstract facts about them. Schacter's *Seven Sins of Memory* (2001) describes the failure modes of human memory — transience, absent-mindedness, blocking, misattribution, suggestibility, bias, and persistence — providing a framework for understanding why faithful remembering is treated in the biblical material as something requiring deliberate cultivation rather than assumed to occur automatically. Bartlett's constructive memory research (*Remembering*, 1932) established that memory is not reproductive (a recording simply played back) but reconstructive — each act of remembering is an active construction shaped by current knowledge, expectation, and emotional state, which is directly relevant to why biblical remembrance is consistently framed as a present, active engagement with the past rather than passive replay.

**Emergence — what causes remembering to arise:**
Remembering arises through retrieval cues — contextual, emotional, or semantic triggers that activate stored memory traces. Emotionally significant events are remembered more vividly and durably than neutral ones (flashbulb memory research, Brown and Kulik, 1977) — directly relevant to why the biblical material consistently anchors covenantal remembrance to events of high emotional and existential significance (deliverance, covenant-making, judgment) rather than to routine occurrences. Zakhar's causative form and the broader reminding vocabulary (which this cluster's own H7725N recall-sense partially overlaps, and which is treated in its own right in M82) name an external triggering of memory — a relational act of activating another's retrieval process.

**Reduction and absence — what causes remembering to diminish:**
The Ebbinghaus forgetting curve documents that forgetting is the default trajectory of most memories — without rehearsal, retrieval, or genuine emotional significance, memories fade predictably over time (see Section 2 for the forgetting vocabulary's own treatment). Stress interacts with memory in a dual way: acute stress can enhance memory for central, salient details of an event while impairing peripheral detail; chronic stress impairs hippocampal function more broadly and can disrupt memory consolidation over time — a nuance worth carrying into Session B where the verse evidence involves traumatic or highly stressful remembered events.

**Location — where memory is seated:**
Memory is distributed across multiple brain systems rather than localised to a single "memory centre" — episodic memory recruits the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex, emotional memory recruits the amygdala, and procedural memory recruits the basal ganglia and cerebellum. Experientially, memory is felt as a return — the past brought into the present, with varying vividness, emotional charge, and narrative coherence depending on which system and retrieval pathway is engaged.

**External influences:**
*Social:* Collective memory (Halbwachs's foundational concept) — the shared memory sustained by a community — shapes what individuals remember and how; Israel's liturgical practices (Passover, Sabbath, and comparable rites) are, on this account, cognitively sophisticated social-memory-sustaining mechanisms rather than merely symbolic gestures. *Developmental:* Childhood amnesia means explicit episodic memories typically begin forming around age three to four, though earlier implicit memories continue to shape emotional and relational patterns beneath conscious recall. *Circumstances:* Stress both enhances and impairs memory depending on acuteness and chronicity, as noted above.

**Coverage assessment:** Good. Cognitive memory research is one of psychology's most developed fields and directly relevant to this section's vocabulary.

### Section 2 — Forgetting: decay, suppression, and forgetfulness as a character trait

**Primary field:** Cognitive psychology / personality psychology

**Glosses covered:**
to forget (G1585 eklanthanomai, G1950 epilanthanomai, H5382 nashah, H7876 shalah, H7911 shakach), forgetfulness (G1953 epilēsmonē, G3024 lēthē, H5388 nashah), forgetful (H7913 shikchah — descriptive/adjectival)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
The Ebbinghaus forgetting curve (1885, replicated extensively since) remains the foundational quantitative description of memory decay absent rehearsal — most new information is lost rapidly within the first hours and days unless actively reinforced. Active or motivated forgetting (the deliberate, though only partially effective, suppression of a painful or unwanted memory) is a genuine but more limited phenomenon than folk psychology often assumes — motivated forgetting studies (building on the "think/no-think" experimental paradigm, Anderson and Green, *Nature*, 2001) show partial, effortful suppression is possible but rarely produces the complete erasure popularly imagined. Traumatic memories occupy a distinctive and paradoxical position in this literature: they are frequently both more vivid (intrusive, involuntary re-experiencing, as documented extensively in PTSD research) and more fragmented or narratively incomplete than ordinary autobiographical memory — a combination directly relevant to reading biblical laments and narrative that describe both haunting, unwanted remembering and gaps or silences around traumatic material. The specific classification of "forgetful" (H7913) as a descriptive character trait, rather than merely a momentary lapse, is analytically notable — it treats forgetfulness as something that can characterise a person's settled disposition (paralleling this batch's observation, in M75, that hardness of heart is likewise treated dispositionally rather than episodically).

**Emergence — what causes forgetting to arise:**
Forgetting arises passively through the natural decay of unrehearsed memory traces (the default, Ebbinghaus trajectory) or, in some cases, through active/motivated suppression of painful material, though the latter is less reliable and less complete than commonly assumed. Habitual forgetfulness as a character trait (H7913's descriptive sense) likely reflects a combination of attentional/encoding deficits (information was never well-encoded in the first place, distinct from true forgetting of well-encoded material) and motivated inattention — not caring enough about the object to encode it durably, which the biblical material's moral use of "forgetting God's law/works" treats as itself morally significant rather than a neutral cognitive limitation.

**Reduction and absence — what causes forgetting to diminish (i.e., what sustains remembering against decay):**
Active rehearsal, retrieval practice, and ritualised repetition (Israel's liturgical calendar functioning, on the collective-memory account above, as a systematic anti-forgetting technology) are the primary known countermeasures to the default decay trajectory. Emotional significance, as noted in Section 1, independently strengthens retention without deliberate rehearsal.

**Location — where forgetting is seated:**
Neurologically, forgetting reflects synaptic decay and interference in the same distributed memory systems discussed in Section 1, rather than a separate "forgetting centre." As a character trait, forgetfulness (H7913) is better located at the level of habitual attention and valuation — what a person consistently fails to attend to and rehearse — than at the level of a discrete memory deficit.

**External influences:**
*Developmental:* Encoding and retrieval strategies (and the habits of attention that support durable memory) are substantially learned and improve with deliberate practice across childhood and adulthood. *Social:* Communities that ritualise and rehearse shared memory (see Section 1's collective-memory note) measurably counteract individual forgetting; communities that do not provide such structures leave individual memory to its default decay trajectory. *Circumstances:* Trauma and chronic stress both impair ordinary memory consolidation (Section 1) and can produce the specific fragmented/intrusive pattern noted above.

**Coverage assessment:** Good. The forgetting literature is as well-developed as the remembering literature and provides direct, substantial coverage, including a genuine (if partial) point of contact for forgetfulness treated as a settled character trait rather than a momentary lapse.

## Outside-science inventory

- **God's remembering (zakhar applied to God — remembering Noah, Abraham, Rachel, his covenant)** — divine "remembering" as an anthropomorphic theological category naming covenantal faithfulness made active, not literal cognitive retrieval (implying no prior divine forgetfulness), is outside science's descriptive scope.
- **forgetting as covenant unfaithfulness** — Israel "forgetting" God or his works, treated throughout the canon as a moral-covenantal failure rather than a neutral cognitive lapse, carries theological weight (idolatry's root often diagnosed as forgetting) that the character-trait treatment in Section 2 can describe the mechanism of but not the covenantal seriousness of.
- **remembrance as liturgical/sacramental participation** — anamnēsis in the Lord's Supper as the church's participation in Christ's redemptive act through memorial (a category more fully treated under M82's reminder/report content) exceeds ordinary social-memory-sustaining-practice research.

## Catalogue prompt reference notes (D9.1.1 / D9.2.1 / D11.2.1 / D12.1.1)

**D9.1.1 — Physiological/neural substrate:** Among the best-characterised substrates in the entire programme — hippocampal and prefrontal circuitry for episodic memory, amygdala involvement for emotionally significant memory, synaptic consolidation and decay processes underlying both remembering and forgetting, and documented HPA-axis/stress interaction effects on memory consolidation.

**D9.2.1 — Generational transmission:** Strong and distinctive transmission pathway via collective/ritual memory (Halbwachs) rather than direct heredity — communities transmit what is remembered and how across generations through liturgical and narrative practice, a cultural-transmission mechanism the biblical corpus itself relies on extensively (repeated command to "remember and tell your children").

**D11.2.1 — Innate endowment vs. acquired:** The basic neural capacity for memory formation and retrieval is innate; what is remembered, how durably, and through what rehearsal practices, is substantially acquired/cultivated — the distinction between raw memory capacity (innate) and cultivated remembrance as a practice (acquired, and morally significant in the biblical material) is analytically important for Session B.

**D12.1.1 — Social/behavioural manifestation:** Manifests behaviourally through ritual practice, spoken recitation, and commemorative action (Passover observance, memorial stones, covenant renewal) as much as through private recall — remembering in this corpus is characteristically a communal, enacted behaviour rather than a purely private cognitive event.
