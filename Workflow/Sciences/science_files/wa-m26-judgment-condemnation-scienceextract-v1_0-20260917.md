## Science sections

### Section 1 — Moral judgment, condemnation, and accountability

**Primary field:** Moral psychology / legal psychology

**Glosses covered:**
to judge (G2919-family via H1778, H8196, H8201, G2917, G2920, G6048), judgment (G2917, G2920, G6048, H1779, H8196, H8201, H4941K), to condemn (G2607, G2613, G2632), condemnation (G2633, G1349), self-condemned (G0843), accountable (G5267 hupodikos), cause/charge (G0156, G0157, H4931), causer (G0159), be conscious of (G6083), impartial (G0087)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Moral psychology's dual-process model (Haidt's social intuitionist model, 2001; Greene's dual-process moral cognition research using fMRI, 2001 onward) treats moral judgment as arising substantially from rapid, affect-laden intuition, with deliberate reasoning more often serving to justify a judgment already reached than to generate it — a significant finding for understanding how condemnation is actually formed, as against how it is formally reasoned about. Kohlberg's stages of moral development (1958 onward, later substantially revised by Gilligan and others for its cultural and gender assumptions) address the developmental trajectory by which capacity for principled, impartial judgment matures. Legal psychology (Kahneman, Sunstein, and Schkade's research on the unpredictability of punitive judgments, 2000s) documents how even trained judges and juries show substantial, systematic inconsistency in condemnation and sentencing severity, driven by factors unrelated to the offence itself (anchoring, mood, fatigue, group polarisation). Self-condemnation specifically overlaps with shame and guilt research (Tangney and Dearing, *Shame and Guilt*, 2002), which distinguishes guilt (condemnation of a specific act, generally adaptive and repair-motivating) from shame (global condemnation of the self, generally maladaptive and withdrawal-motivating).

**Emergence — what causes judgment and condemnation to arise:**
Moral judgment arises rapidly and largely intuitively upon perceiving an act as harmful, unfair, or norm-violating, with conscious reasoning typically constructed after the intuitive judgment to justify it (Haidt). Condemnation specifically requires the further step of assigning responsibility to an agent (accountability) — an act perceived as harmful but accidental or unintended is judged differently from one perceived as culpable. Self-condemnation arises when the same evaluative apparatus is turned on the self, and — per the guilt/shame distinction — its psychological character differs sharply depending on whether it is act-specific (guilt, generally functional) or global (shame, generally corrosive).

**Reduction and absence — what causes judgment and condemnation to diminish:**
Condemnation is moderated by increased information about mitigating circumstances, by perspective-taking with the accused (documented to reduce punitive severity in experimental settings), and by structural safeguards against the well-documented biases (fatigue, anchoring, in-group favouritism) that distort judgment. Self-condemnation is reduced through the guilt-to-repair pathway (acknowledging a specific wrong and making amends resolves guilt functionally) and, where it has become globalised shame, through therapeutic reframing that separates the act from the person's total worth. Impartiality (adiakritos) as an ideal is precisely the corrective the research literature aims at — judgment uncontaminated by the systematic biases the empirical record documents as pervasive even among trained adjudicators.

**Location — where judgment and condemnation are seated:**
Primarily cognitive (appraisal of the act and the agent's responsibility) with strong affective involvement (moral judgment is intuition-led and affect-laden, not a cool calculation) and a clear social/relational dimension (judgment is rendered about persons, usually by persons occupying some position of authority or standing to judge). Self-condemnation adds a reflexive dimension — the same evaluative machinery applied to the self.

**External influences:**
*Developmental:* Moral reasoning capacity matures through childhood and adolescence (Kohlberg, Gilligan); the capacity for impartial, principled judgment as against in-group-favouring judgment is a developmental achievement, not a given. *Social:* Group membership strongly biases judgment — people judge in-group members' transgressions less harshly than identical transgressions by out-group members, a robust and widely replicated finding. *Circumstances:* Judges' and evaluators' own state (fatigue, mood, recent experience) measurably and often unconsciously shapes the severity of judgment rendered, independent of the facts of the case. *Hereditary:* Individual differences in empathic capacity and in impulsivity (both relevant to how harshly one judges) show heritable components.

**Coverage assessment:** Good. Moral psychology and legal psychology together provide strong, well-developed coverage of the mechanism and biases of human judgment.

### Section 2 — Justification, vindication, and being declared righteous

**Primary field:** Moral psychology / social psychology of reputation and vindication

**Glosses covered:**
to justify (G1344 dikaioō, H6663), justification (G1347), righteous act (dikaiōma — implicit in the wider dikaios family), righteousness (H4941K judgment hall aside; tse.deq, tsad.diq reflected via judgment terms), just (G0087 adiakritos, G0703 aretē as virtue), upright/uprightness (H4931 charge — related root), to count (G3049 logizomai, H4487), to deem worthy (G5267 axioō), to value (H6186B)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Social psychology's research on reputation and vindication (Emler, *The Sociology of Reputation*) documents that the restoration of a wrongly accused or suspected person's standing — being publicly declared "in the right" — has measurable psychological benefits beyond the resolution of the practical matter at hand: it restores social trust and relational standing, not merely a factual record. The psychological experience of being vindicated after wrongful suspicion or accusation is associated with relief, restored self-concept, and reduced hypervigilance about further accusation. "To count" (logizomai) in its accounting/reckoning sense has a loose cognitive-psychology parallel in research on how people track and update credit/debit ledgers of obligation and desert in relationships (equity theory, Walster, Berscheid and Walster, 1978) — though the forensic, once-for-all declarative sense the biblical vocabulary carries (being reckoned righteous) exceeds the ongoing, provisional tracking that equity theory describes.

**Emergence — what causes justification/vindication to be experientially significant:**
Vindication matters psychologically because standing and reputation are not private facts but social ones — being regarded as guilty or suspect by a community has real relational and material consequences independent of actual guilt, so formal justification (a declaration by a legitimate authority that one is in the right) restores something that mere private innocence does not.

**Reduction and absence — what causes the sense of justification to be undermined:**
Even formal vindication can fail to resolve the psychological residue of suspicion or accusation where trust has been damaged in ways a verdict alone does not repair — documented in wrongful-conviction exoneree research, where legal vindication frequently leaves lasting psychological and social effects (stigma, disrupted identity, and doubt) that outlast the formal declaration.

**Location — where justification is seated:**
Primarily social and relational — justification is a declared status conferred by one party (with standing to declare it) upon another, and its psychological weight depends on the authority and legitimacy of the declaring party. It has a secondary internal dimension: the person's own appropriation (or failure to appropriate) the declared status into their self-concept.

**External influences:**
*Social:* The authority and credibility of the one declaring justification determines how effectively it restores standing — a verdict from a trusted, legitimate source restores reputation far more effectively than an equivalent declaration from a source without standing. *Circumstances:* Prior public accusation or suspicion is the necessary precondition that makes justification psychologically significant; where no accusation existed, justification has no comparable weight.

**Coverage assessment:** Moderate. Reputation and exoneree research provide a genuine, if partial, analogue for the psychological significance of being declared righteous; the specifically forensic, once-for-all theological content of justification before God exceeds this (see Outside-science inventory).

### Section 3 — Vengeance, retribution, and repayment

**Primary field:** Social psychology / behavioural economics of punishment

**Glosses covered:**
to avenge (G1556, H5358), vengeance (G1557, G1558, H5359, H5360), to repay (G0467, G0661, H4487 in its counting/reckoning sense as overlap), avenging (G1558)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Fehr and Gächter's research on costly punishment (*Nature*, 2002) established that humans will incur real personal cost to punish norm violators even with no prospect of future interaction or personal benefit — "altruistic punishment," which appears to function as a stabilising mechanism for cooperation in social groups. De Quervain and colleagues (*Science*, 2004) demonstrated that administering (perceived) just punishment to a norm violator activates reward-related neural circuitry (dorsal striatum), suggesting that retribution carries genuine, measurable psychological reward independent of any restorative or deterrent benefit — a finding directly relevant to why vengeance is experientially compelling even when it serves no practical purpose. Research on revenge and its psychological aftermath (Carlsmith, Wilson and Gilbert, 2008) found, counterintuitively, that people who take revenge typically feel worse afterward than those who do not, despite predicting the opposite — revenge fails to deliver the closure it promises and instead prolongs rumination on the offence.

**Emergence — what causes the impulse to vengeance to arise:**
Vengeance arises from the perception of a wrong done to oneself or one's group combined with the desire to restore a felt balance through the infliction of proportionate (or, commonly, disproportionate) cost on the offender. The neural reward response to punishing a violator (de Quervain) helps explain why the impulse is so psychologically forceful even where it produces no practical benefit and, per Carlsmith and colleagues, often no lasting satisfaction.

**Reduction and absence — what causes vengeance to diminish:**
Vengeance is reduced by the intervention of a trusted third-party system of justice that the aggrieved party believes will render proportionate judgment on their behalf — displacing the need for personal retribution (this is close to the functional logic of formal legal systems generally, and maps directly onto the biblical pattern of vengeance being reserved to God or to properly constituted authority rather than exercised privately). Forgiveness research (Enright, Worthington) documents a distinct psychological pathway by which the desire for retribution is voluntarily relinquished, with measurable benefits to the forgiver's own wellbeing independent of whether the offender is ever formally punished.

**Location — where vengeance is seated:**
Primarily emotional and motivational, with a documented neural reward component (striatal activation on administering deserved punishment). It is inherently relational — vengeance exists only in relation to a perceived offender — and has strong social embedding, since group-level "altruistic punishment" functions to enforce cooperative norms at the community level, not merely to satisfy the individual avenger.

**External influences:**
*Social and cultural:* Honour-based cultures (including much of the ancient Near Eastern context of this material) attach higher social expectation to personal or familial retribution than do cultures with strong centralised legal enforcement; where trusted third-party justice systems exist and are perceived as effective, personal vengeance-seeking measurably decreases. *Circumstances:* The severity and personal nature of the original wrong is the primary driver of the intensity of the vengeance impulse. *Developmental:* Capacity to delay or forgo retribution in favour of due process is a developmental and socially formed capacity, not present from early childhood.

**Coverage assessment:** Good. Behavioural economics and social psychology of punishment provide strong, well-developed coverage of the mechanism and neural correlates of the vengeance impulse.

## Outside-science inventory

- **Judgment as an eschatological, divine act** — the Day of the Lord, final judgment, and God as the ultimately impartial and authoritative judge are theological categories. Science addresses human judgment's mechanism and biases; it does not address a judgment rendered by God with final, unappealable authority.
- **Righteousness [God] (tse.deq) as a divine attribute** — God's own righteousness as the standard against which all judgment is measured is a theological category, not a psychological construct.
- **Justification before God (dikaioō in its Pauline forensic sense)** — being declared righteous by God on grounds other than one's own moral performance (justification by faith) is a theological claim with no analogue in reputation or exoneree research, which addresses only vindication on the grounds of established fact.
- **Vengeance reserved to God ("vengeance is mine," Romans 12:19)** — the theological claim that ultimate retribution belongs to God alone, and that this reservation is itself what frees the human party from the burden of personal vengeance, exceeds behavioural economics' account of displacement to trusted third-party systems, which addresses only human institutions.

**Observation:** M26's outside-science content concentrates at the point where the cluster's human-level phenomena (judgment, justification, vengeance) are claimed to have their true and final form in God's own action rather than in any human court, verdict, or act of retribution. The human mechanics of judging, being vindicated, and seeking or relinquishing vengeance are well covered by moral psychology and behavioural economics; their theological terminus is not.

## D9.1.1 / D9.2.1 / D11.2.1 / D12.1.1 prompt reference notes

**D9.1.1 — Physiological/neural substrate:** Documented for vengeance specifically (striatal reward-circuitry activation on administering deserved punishment, de Quervain et al.) and for moral judgment generally (Greene's dual-process fMRI work showing distinct neural signatures for intuitive versus deliberative moral judgment). Justification/vindication has a thinner direct neural literature — its substrate is mostly inferred from stress-reduction and relief markers following restored standing, rather than dedicated neuroimaging.

**D9.2.1 — Generational transmission:** Limited direct heritability data for judgment, condemnation, or vengeance as such. What is transmitted intergenerationally is substantially cultural — honour/retribution norms, in-group judgment bias, and models of trusted (or distrusted) third-party justice are passed through family and community formation rather than biological inheritance. Individual differences in empathic capacity and impulsivity, both relevant inputs to how harshly one judges or how readily one seeks vengeance, do show heritable components.

**D11.2.1 — Innate endowment vs. acquired:** Mixed, and this is one of the more theoretically interesting sections on this question. The rapid, intuitive, affect-laden character of moral judgment (Haidt) and the neural reward of administering punishment (de Quervain) both suggest a genuinely innate substrate to judgment and retribution — not merely learned behaviour. But the specific content of what counts as wrong, the standards applied, and whether retribution is personally exercised or deferred to a trusted authority are all substantially acquired through moral development and cultural formation (Kohlberg's stages; cultural variation in retribution norms).

**D12.1.1 — Social/behavioural manifestation:** All three sections are inherently social and behaviourally manifest — judgment is rendered about and often by persons in social relation, justification is a socially conferred status, and vengeance is enacted between parties. None of this cluster's content describes a purely private, unexpressed inner state; the relevant science accordingly treats all three through social-psychological and behavioural-economic method.
