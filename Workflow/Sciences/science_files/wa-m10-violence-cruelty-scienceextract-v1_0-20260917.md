## Science sections

### Section 1 — Violent and destructive action

**Primary field:** Behavioural science / criminology — violence research

**Glosses covered:**
violence (H1500, H2555, H7701), to destroy (a large family: G1311, G5351, H2254B, G0853, G3645, H0007, H2255, H2717B, H4277, H5642B, H6789, H6979C, H8045, H8046), be desolate: destroyed (H8074G), fight (G0073), battle (H4421), to commit (H4560), staggering (H6330)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Violence research spans criminology and social psychology. The General Aggression Model (Anderson and Bushman, *Annual Review of Psychology*, 2002) integrates person and situation variables into a unified account of when aggressive cognition, affect, and arousal translate into violent action. Archer and Coyne's typology distinguishes direct physical violence from indirect and relational aggression (the latter is addressed in M06's Section 4). Collins's micro-sociology of violence (*Violence: A Micro-sociological Theory*, 2008) is a significant departure from trait-based accounts: it argues, from close analysis of real violent encounters (including recorded combat and assault footage), that most humans face strong situational barriers — "confrontational tension and fear" — against actually committing violence, and that successful violence typically requires specific situational conditions (an emotionally dominant attacker, a vulnerable or unresisting target, or group dynamics that dissolve individual restraint) rather than flowing straightforwardly from anger or hatred.

**Emergence — what causes violence and destructive action to arise:**
Violence arises from the convergence of person factors (trait aggression, prior exposure to violence, situational anger) and situational factors (provocation, the availability of weapons or means, group dynamics that diffuse individual responsibility, and — per Collins's situational account — the specific tactical conditions that allow confrontational tension to be overcome). Destruction more broadly (of property, structures, or social order) follows similar dynamics but does not require the direct interpersonal confrontation that violence against a person does.

**Reduction and absence — what causes violence to diminish:**
Violence reduces through the removal of situational triggers (de-escalation, separation of parties, reduction of means/weapon availability), through community and legal structures that reliably sanction it, and through the cultivation of empathy and inhibitory self-control that raises the threshold for acting on aggressive impulse. Collins's situational research implies a further reduction pathway: removing the specific tactical conditions (audience effects, power imbalance, group psychology) that make violence situationally "workable" reduces its incidence even where underlying hostility persists.

**Location — where violent action is seated:**
Behavioural and situational rather than purely characterological — while trait aggression contributes, violence research increasingly emphasises that the act itself depends heavily on the specific situation, not only on the actor's inner disposition. This has a direct bearing on how "violence" as a biblical category should be read: the vocabulary here names an action and its outcome (destruction) more than a stable inner quality, in contrast to Section 2's cruelty vocabulary, which does name a stable disposition.

**External influences:**
*Developmental:* Childhood exposure to violence (as victim or witness) is among the strongest predictors of adult violent behaviour. *Social:* Group and crowd contexts measurably increase the likelihood of violence beyond what any individual member would commit alone (deindividuation research — Zimbardo). *Circumstances:* War, civil breakdown, scarcity, and the availability of weapons are the primary circumstantial enablers; much of the biblical material's violence and destruction vocabulary is set in exactly such contexts (conquest, siege, national conflict).

**Coverage assessment:** Good. Violence and aggression research is extensive across criminology, social psychology, and micro-sociology.

### Section 2 — Cruelty and ruthlessness as character disposition

**Primary field:** Clinical and personality psychology

**Glosses covered:**
cruel (H0393, H0394, H0395), ruthless (a.rits, H6184), sinful (G0268)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Baumeister's analysis of the perpetrator-victim gap (*Evil: Inside Human Violence and Cruelty*, 1997) identifies a consistent asymmetry: perpetrators of cruelty typically minimise, rationalise, or fail to register the severity of the harm they cause, while victims experience it as maximally significant — cruelty is sustained partly by this systematic misperception. Dark triad research (psychopathy, Machiavellianism, narcissism — Paulhus and Williams, 2002) addresses the personality cluster most associated with cold, instrumental cruelty unmoved by others' suffering, distinct from hot, reactive aggression. Bandura's moral disengagement mechanisms (*Moral Disengagement*, 2016) — euphemistic labelling, diffusion of responsibility, dehumanisation of the victim, distortion of consequences — describe the specific cognitive operations that allow ordinarily moral people to act cruelly while retaining a positive self-image.

**Emergence — what causes cruelty and ruthlessness to arise:**
Cruelty arises when the suffering of another is perceived as deserved, satisfying, or merely instrumental to a goal, and when the normal inhibitions against causing harm are disengaged through one or more of Bandura's mechanisms. Ruthlessness specifically names a standing disposition toward pursuing one's ends without regard for the cost to others, rather than a single cruel act — it is closer to a character trait than an emotional episode. Dehumanisation of the target is frequently a co-condition for both.

**Reduction and absence — what causes cruelty to diminish:**
Cruelty is reduced by the restoration of the perpetrator's recognition of the victim's full humanity (the reverse of dehumanisation), by accountability structures that remove the social cover moral disengagement requires, and by direct exposure to the actual severity of the harm caused (closing the perpetrator-victim perception gap Baumeister documents). Genuine remorse — distinct from mere regret at consequences to oneself — is the clinical marker of a cruelty pattern beginning to be reversed.

**Location — where cruelty and ruthlessness are seated:**
Characterological — a stable disposition rather than a situational response, distinguishing this section from Section 1's more situationally-contingent violence. It has an affective component that can go either way: affective coldness (psychopathic-pattern cruelty, unmoved by the victim's suffering) or, in reactive cruelty, an affective satisfaction in the suffering caused.

**External influences:**
*Developmental and parenting:* Harsh, punitive, and abusive parenting is among the strongest predictors of cruel and ruthless character development in adulthood; childhood experience of being a victim of cruelty is itself associated with an elevated risk of later perpetrating it (the "cycle of abuse" finding, though not universal). *Hereditary:* Genetic variants associated with the MAOA gene interact with childhood abuse to influence aggression reactivity. *Cultural:* Cultures and institutional contexts that reward ruthless pursuit of advantage (certain competitive economic or political environments) normalise and reinforce the trait.

**Coverage assessment:** Good. Cruelty, dark-triad personality traits, and moral disengagement are all well-studied.

### Section 3 — Wounding and physical injury

**Primary field:** Behavioural science / forensic and trauma research

**Glosses covered:**
to wound (G5135, H4272, H6481, H8428), to suffer injury (H5142), injury (H6094), to crush (G2352, G3039, H1792, H1794, H1795, H1854, H2115, H3807, H7533, H7602B)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Trauma research (van der Kolk, *The Body Keeps the Score*, 2014) documents the lasting physiological and psychological consequences of physical injury inflicted by another person, which differ measurably from injury sustained accidentally — interpersonal violence produces distinctively elevated rates of PTSD compared to equivalent accidental injury, because the deliberate agency of another person adds a betrayal and safety-world-breaking dimension that accident does not. Forensic and medical trauma research documents the mechanisms and severity gradient of crushing and wounding injuries specifically.

**Emergence — what causes wounding to arise:**
Wounding arises as the direct physical consequence of violent action (Section 1) or cruelty (Section 2) directed at a person's body. Crushing specifically names a severe, often disabling or lethal category of injury involving compressive force, frequently used in the biblical material figuratively for the psychological experience of being overwhelmed or broken (a metaphor with a genuine somatic basis, given crushing's real physiological severity).

**Reduction and absence — what causes wounding to cease or heal:**
Physical wounding ceases when the violent action stops; healing follows normal physiological recovery processes, though psychological sequelae (per van der Kolk) frequently persist well beyond physical recovery, particularly when the injury was deliberately inflicted by another person rather than accidental.

**Location — where wounding is seated:**
Explicitly physical/somatic at the point of injury, with well-documented psychological sequelae (trauma, hypervigilance, altered trust) that extend well beyond the body itself, especially where the wounding was interpersonal rather than accidental.

**External influences:**
*Circumstances:* War, assault, and armed conflict are the primary contexts. *Social:* The presence of social support after wounding is among the strongest predictors of psychological (not merely physical) recovery.

**Coverage assessment:** Moderate. Direct physical trauma is medically well understood; the psychological sequelae of deliberately-inflicted (versus accidental) wounding are well documented in trauma research.

### Section 4 — Strife and interpersonal conflict

**Primary field:** Social psychology — conflict research

**Glosses covered:**
strife (H4066, H4090, H4683, H4695, H7379, H8409)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Conflict escalation research (Pruitt and Rubin, *Social Conflict: Escalation, Stalemate, and Settlement*) documents the characteristic pattern by which limited disputes escalate into sustained strife: tactics intensify, the number of issues in contention grows, parties shift from specific grievances to general character attacks, and the conflict becomes self-sustaining independent of its original cause. Gottman's marital conflict research (applicable more broadly to sustained interpersonal strife) identifies specific communication patterns — criticism, contempt, defensiveness, stonewalling — that predict whether a conflict resolves or entrenches into chronic strife.

**Emergence — what causes strife to arise:**
Strife arises from unresolved disputes over scarce resources, status, or competing claims, and is sustained by escalation dynamics that shift the conflict's focus from the original issue to the relationship itself. Once escalated, strife frequently persists independent of any rational cost-benefit calculation about the original dispute.

**Reduction and absence — what causes strife to diminish:**
De-escalation requires deliberate intervention: third-party mediation, a decisive resolution of the underlying dispute, or a change in the relationship's power dynamics that removes the incentive to continue contesting. Left to its own dynamics, strife tends to entrench rather than resolve.

**Location — where strife is seated:**
Relational — like M06's enmity and oppression vocabulary, strife names a standing state between parties rather than a purely internal condition, though it produces internal states (frustration, anger, anxiety) in those caught up in it.

**External influences:**
*Social and structural:* Ambiguous authority structures, competition over scarce resources, and the absence of legitimate dispute-resolution mechanisms are the primary structural enablers of sustained strife.

**Coverage assessment:** Moderate. Conflict escalation research is well developed for interpersonal and organisational contexts.

## Outside-science inventory

- **destruction in its eschatological/judgment register** — where "to destroy" and related terms describe God's judgment rather than human violent action, the theological category of divine judgment exceeds criminology's descriptive account of human violence.
- **violence as covenant curse** — where violence and destruction appear as consequences of covenant violation (per Deuteronomy's curse structures), the covenantal-causal framework is a theological category outside science's scope.
- **ruthlessness and cruelty attributed to instruments of divine judgment** (e.g., foreign nations used as God's rod of discipline) — the theological structure by which human cruelty can be simultaneously morally culpable and providentially used has no science analogue.

**Observation:** M10's outside-science content sits primarily at the judgment/covenant-curse end. The human phenomena of violence, cruelty, wounding, and strife are all well covered by science, particularly by the more recent situational (Collins) and moral-disengagement (Bandura) literatures, which are more analytically useful for this cluster than older purely trait-based accounts of "violent personality."

## D9/D11/D12 prompt reference notes

> These four catalogue questions (`D9.1.1`, `D9.2.1`, `D11.2.1`, `D12.1.1`) replace the retired `T7.3` prompt as of the 2026-09-17 catalogue realignment (`catalogue_version='v2-dimension-realign-20260917'`). Each is answered here from this document's science sections as the pre-analytical data input; the verse-evidence side of each question is completed at the characteristic-level answer stage, not here.

**D9.1.1 — Physiological/Neural Substrate:** Well documented for the arousal side — violent action (Section 1) engages the same amygdala/sympathetic threat-and-aggression circuitry addressed in M02's anger review. Cruelty (Section 2) is notable for the opposite pattern in its coldest (psychopathic-pattern) form: reduced amygdala reactivity and reduced autonomic response to others' distress cues. Wounding (Section 3) has an obvious direct physical substrate plus a well-documented trauma-physiology sequela (HPA axis dysregulation, per van der Kolk).

**D9.2.1 — Generational Transmission:** Strong. Childhood exposure to violence (as victim or witness) is among the most robust predictors of adult violent and cruel behaviour in the entire literature; the MAOA-gene-by-childhood-abuse interaction is one of the best-replicated gene-by-environment findings in aggression research. Strife patterns (Section 4) are also documented to be modelled and transmitted within families and communities.

**D11.2.1 — Innate Endowment:** Mixed. The general capacity for aggression and the situational triggers that provoke it appear to have an evolved, cross-species basis (general aggression research), but Collins's situational research is significant here: it suggests actual violence is not a simple expression of an innate drive but requires specific enabling conditions to overcome strong default inhibition — bearing directly on D11.1's created-design/fallen-condition question, since it suggests humans are not straightforwardly "wired for violence" but require particular conditions (dehumanisation, group dynamics, power imbalance) to overcome an inhibitory default.

**D12.1.1 — Social/Behavioural Manifestation:** Overwhelmingly competitive/adversarial. Every section of this cluster names action taken against another party's body, property, or standing. The one partial exception is Section 4's finding that strife, once escalated, can become self-sustaining independent of any ongoing rational competitive motive — worth testing against biblical strife vocabulary for cases where conflict persists beyond its original cause.
