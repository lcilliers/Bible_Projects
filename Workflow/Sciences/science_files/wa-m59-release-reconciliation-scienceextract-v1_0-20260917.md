## Science sections

### Section 1 — Leaving and departure

**Primary field:** Developmental psychology — separation and attachment-rupture research

**Glosses covered:**
to leave (anachōreō), to leave (apoleipō), to leave (apotassō), to leave (apochōreō), to leave (afistēmi), to leave (metairō), to leave (hupoleipō), to leave (na.tash), to leave: neglect (a.zav)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Bowlby's separation research (*Attachment and Loss, Vol. 2: Separation*, 1973) documents the characteristic sequence of protest, despair, and detachment that follows separation from an attachment figure — a well-established psychological pattern regardless of whether the separation is voluntary or imposed. The verbs in this section span a considerable range: some (apotassō — to take leave of, to say farewell to) name an ordered, mutually-acknowledged departure; others (afistēmi — to withdraw, fall away) carry a more decisive, often relationally-rupturing sense; the Hebrew a.zav specifically (to leave, forsake, neglect) carries a strong abandonment valence distinct from an ordinary parting. Levinson's transition psychology (*The Seasons of a Man's Life*, 1978) and life-course research on voluntary departures (leaving home, leaving employment, leaving a community) document that even chosen, well-prepared departures carry a measurable adjustment cost, while unilateral or abandoning departures (a.zav's register) produce a qualitatively more severe response in those left behind, closely paralleling attachment-rupture and abandonment research in clinical psychology.

**Emergence — what causes leaving/departure to occur:**
Departure can arise from ordinary life-course transition (a planned, mutually understood parting — apotassō), from withdrawal under pressure or disagreement (afistēmi — a falling-away or distancing, often used of apostasy or defection), or from active abandonment/neglect of an ongoing obligation or relationship (a.zav, na.tash). The psychological and relational consequences differ sharply across these three registers even though a single English gloss ("to leave") can cover all of them — a distinction worth testing carefully in the biblical pass, since the cluster currently does not differentiate them structurally.

**Reduction and absence — what causes departure not to occur, or its effects to be mitigated:**
Secure attachment and adequately prepared, mutually acknowledged transitions (versus abrupt or unilateral departure) substantially reduce the psychological cost of leaving for those affected. Abandonment's distinctive harm (documented extensively in developmental psychology on parental abandonment and in adult attachment research on unilateral relationship exit) is mitigated by the availability of alternative attachment figures and by the abandoned party's own prior attachment security.

**Location — where the response to leaving is seated:**
Primarily relational and emotional in the person left behind (grief, protest, and — where the departure is abandonment — a documented and distinct injury to basic trust and security); for the one who leaves, the location depends on the register — volitional and often morally weighted for afistēmi/a.zav-type departure, more neutrally transitional for apotassō-type leave-taking.

**External influences:**
*Developmental:* Early experience of stable, non-abandoning relationships builds resilience to later, non-abandoning departures (ordinary transitions) while leaving a person more vulnerable, not less, to the specific harm of abandonment-type departure when it does occur, because it violates an established expectation of constancy. *Cultural/social:* Norms around leave-taking (formal farewell rituals, expected notice, communal acknowledgment of departure) shape how disruptive a given departure is experienced as being.

**Coverage assessment:** Good for the general psychology of separation and departure; the specific differentiation this section's own vocabulary implies (ordinary parting versus withdrawal versus abandonment) is well supported by the literature but not yet reflected in how the cluster's terms have been grouped.

### Section 2 — Release, letting go, and permission

**Primary field:** Behavioural and organisational psychology — obligation-release and letting-go research

**Glosses covered:**
to release (apallassō), to release: leave (afiēmi), to release: permit (afiēmi), to release: release (apoluō), to release (sha.mat)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
This section's vocabulary spans release from an obligation, formal permission-granting, and the specific Hebrew sha.mat (to let drop, release — the term underlying the sabbatical-year debt release). Zeigarnik's classic finding on unfinished tasks (the Zeigarnik effect, 1927) and subsequent goal-disengagement research (Wrosch and colleagues, *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin*, 2003) document that unresolved obligations and unreleased goals occupy ongoing cognitive and emotional resources, and that the capacity to deliberately disengage from an unattainable or no-longer-appropriate goal or obligation is a documented, measurable contributor to wellbeing — "letting go" as a psychologically real and beneficial process, not merely a figure of speech. Behavioural economics research on debt and default (Mullainathan and Shafir, *Scarcity*, 2013) documents the disproportionate cognitive and psychological burden of unreleased financial obligation on the indebted — directly relevant to sha.mat's institutional debt-release usage.

**Emergence — what causes release/letting go to occur:**
Deliberate release of an obligation or claim requires the releasing party's recognition that continued holding serves no further good purpose (or that a higher value — mercy, restored relationship, structural fairness — outweighs continued enforcement), combined with the standing or authority to grant the release. Sha.mat's institutional register (a periodic, structurally mandated release rather than a case-by-case act of individual grace) is psychologically and sociologically distinct from ad hoc permission-granting — goal-disengagement research suggests that scheduled, structurally guaranteed release may be easier to sustain over time than release that depends on repeated individual decision.

**Reduction and absence — what prevents release:**
Goal-disengagement research identifies loss aversion (the asymmetric psychological weight of a loss versus an equivalent gain — Kahneman and Tversky's prospect theory) and sunk-cost reasoning as the primary obstacles to releasing a claim, obligation, or unattainable goal, even where release would be beneficial to the releasing party as well as the one released.

**Location:** Volitional and relational — release is an act of the will exercised toward another (or toward one's own unattainable goal), with documented wellbeing consequences for the releaser as well as relational consequences for the one released.

**External influences:** *Structural:* Institutionally mandated release mechanisms (analogous to sha.mat's sabbatical debt-release) remove the burden of repeated individual decision and are documented in behavioural economics to produce more consistent release than systems relying on case-by-case discretion. *Circumstances:* Power imbalance shapes who has the standing to grant release and who must depend on it being granted.

**Coverage assessment:** Moderate. Goal-disengagement and obligation psychology provide a genuine, if indirect, frame; the section is small and its institutional/legal register (sha.mat) sits closer to economic and legal history than to psychology proper.

### Section 3 — Deprivation

**Primary field:** Clinical and social psychology — loss and scarcity research (thin coverage, single gloss)

**Glosses covered:**
deprivation (apobolē)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Apobolē (a casting away, a loss) names the state of having been deprived rather than an act of releasing or leaving. Loss and scarcity research (Mullainathan and Shafir, cited above) documents the cognitive and psychological effects of deprivation — a narrowing of attentional "bandwidth" onto the scarce resource, at real cost to other cognitive function.

**Coverage assessment:** Thin. A single gloss with only indirect science engagement; a short honest treatment is preferred here over padding.

## D9.1.1 / D9.2.1 / D11.2.1 / D12.1.1 prompt reference notes

**D9.1.1 — Physiological/neural substrate:** Separation and abandonment-type leaving (Section 1) activate the same attachment-rupture and social-pain circuitry documented elsewhere in this batch (M50, M53). Deliberate release/letting-go (Section 2) is associated with reduced rumination-related default-mode-network activity once successfully achieved, per goal-disengagement neuroscience. Deprivation (Section 3) is associated with measurable reductions in fluid cognitive capacity under scarcity conditions (Mullainathan and Shafir).

**D9.2.1 — Generational transmission:** Abandonment-type leaving (a.zav, na.tash) shows documented intergenerational transmission via attachment style — children of abandoning parents show elevated rates of both experiencing and enacting relational abandonment in their own adult relationships. Institutional release mechanisms (sha.mat) transmit generationally through law and communal memory rather than through any psychological mechanism.

**D11.2.1 — Innate endowment vs. acquired:** Attachment sensitivity to separation and abandonment is innate (a species-typical feature of the attachment system); the specific behaviours of leaving, releasing, or depriving are acquired choices and circumstances rather than innate drives.

**D12.1.1 — Social/behavioural manifestation:** Ordinary departure manifests as an acknowledged, often mutually prepared transition; abandonment-type leaving manifests as unilateral withdrawal of presence or provision, typically without adequate acknowledgment; release manifests as a deliberate relinquishing of a claim or obligation the releaser had standing to enforce; deprivation manifests as the state of lacking a needed resource, with documented downstream effects on attention and decision quality.
