## Science sections

### Section 1 — Sincerity and undivided motive

**Primary field:** Social and moral psychology — honesty and authenticity research

**Glosses covered:**
sincerity (afelotēs), sincerity (eilikrineia), sound (haplous)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Kernis's authenticity research (*Psychological Inquiry*, 2003) identifies unbiased processing, behavioural consistency, and relational orientation toward genuineness as the core components of authenticity — a psychological construct closely matching what haplous (single-eyed, undivided, without hidden fold) and eilikrineia (clearness, as of something examined in sunlight without concealment) name: a wholeness of motive in which the inner state and outer expression are not divided against each other. Ariely's honesty research (*The (Honest) Truth About Dishonesty*, 2012) documents that most departures from sincerity are partial and self-justified rather than total — people maintain a self-concept of being basically honest while permitting small, rationalised exceptions, which throws into relief the more demanding, undivided quality this section's vocabulary names as the positive ideal.

**Emergence — what causes sincerity/undivided motive to arise:**
Sincerity arises from a strong, internally consistent moral identity (Aquino and Reed's moral-identity research) in which honesty and transparency are central to the self-concept rather than merely instrumentally useful; from the deliberate practice of aligning inner conviction with outward expression; and, in the theological context, from grounding in the character of a God who is himself without duplicity. Afelotēs (literally, without a stone — smoothness, simplicity) suggests an absence of hidden obstruction, consistent with authenticity research's emphasis on unbiased, undefended self-presentation.

**Reduction and absence — what causes sincerity to diminish:**
Insincerity is fostered by impression-management pressure (performing a self one does not actually hold), by incremental self-justified departures from full transparency (Ariely's incremental-dishonesty finding), and by social contexts that reward polished appearance over accurate self-disclosure. Authenticity research finds that chronic impression-management, even where socially successful, is associated with lower wellbeing and a felt sense of internal division.

**Location — where sincerity is seated:**
Characterological and cognitive — a whole-person quality of undivided motive rather than a discrete emotion or single behaviour; it concerns the relationship between a person's inner state and their outward expression of it.

**External influences:**
*Developmental:* Environments (family, community) that reward authentic self-disclosure over performance cultivate sincerity; environments demanding constant impression-management erode it. *Cultural:* Norms for directness versus diplomatic indirection vary substantially and shape what a given culture recognises as sincere expression.

**Coverage assessment:** Good. Authenticity and honesty research provide a solid, directly applicable framework.

### Section 2 — Straightness and moral consistency

**Primary field:** Positive and virtue psychology — integrity and character research

**Glosses covered:**
Straight (euthus), to cut straight (orthotomeō), be straight (ta.qan), straightforward (na.kho.ach), to confirm (te.qan)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Palanski and Yammarino's integrity research (*Journal of Business Ethics*, 2009) identifies integrity as consistency — between words and actions, and between espoused and enacted values — the same consistency-under-a-standard metaphor carried directly by this section's "straight/straightness" vocabulary (euthus, ta.qan, na.kho.ach), which functions as the positive counterpart to M57's "twisting/perverting" vocabulary: a straight path or straight speech is one that has not been bent from a true standard. The VIA character-strengths framework (Peterson and Seligman, *Character Strengths and Virtues*, 2004) includes honesty/authenticity among core, cross-culturally recognised character strengths. Orthotomeō (to cut a straight path, used in the New Testament of rightly handling the word of truth) specifically names accurate, undistorted transmission or interpretation of a standard — a skill-dimension of straightness distinct from mere personal consistency.

**Emergence — what causes moral/communicative straightness to develop:**
Consistency between conviction and conduct develops through habituation of honest practice over time (the Aristotelian account of character formation, empirically supported by habit-formation research), strong moral identity, and community accountability that notices and names departures from the standard. Accurate handling of truth (orthotomeō's specific sense) additionally requires competence — the skill of correctly representing a standard, not only the will to do so honestly.

**Reduction and absence — what causes straightness to be lost:**
Incremental compromise, rationalised inconsistency, and social environments that reward results regardless of the means all erode straightness over time — the same incremental mechanism documented under M57's corruption research applies here as its mirror image. Loss of straightness in the specifically interpretive sense (orthotomeō) can also occur through incompetence or carelessness rather than dishonesty — a distinct failure mode worth distinguishing in Session B.

**Location — where straightness is seated:**
Characterological (personal consistency) and, for the interpretive-handling sense, also cognitive-skill-based (accurate representation of a standard) — the cluster's vocabulary spans both a virtue (be straight) and a competence (cut/handle straight).

**External influences:** Moral-community membership, mentorship in both character and competence, and consistent accountability are the primary documented external shapers.

**Coverage assessment:** Good. Integrity and virtue-psychology research provide solid coverage; the specific interpretive-competence dimension (orthotomeō) is less directly covered by character-strengths research than by studies of expertise and accurate transmission of information.

### Section 3 — Certainty, confirmation, and epistemic assurance

**Primary field:** Cognitive and decision psychology — confidence and certainty research

**Glosses covered:**
confirmation (bebaiōsis), irrevocable (ametamelētos), to confirm (bebaioō), certain (yats.tsiv), certain (pal.mo.ni), sure (a.ma.nah), sound (ēchos), sound (fthongos), to sound a trumpet (salpizō), sound (hem.yah), sound (she.ma)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Kahneman and Tversky's work on confidence and calibration (*Judgment Under Uncertainty*, 1982) documents the frequent mismatch between subjective certainty and actual accuracy — most human confidence is imperfectly calibrated, which makes genuinely well-founded certainty (rather than mere confident feeling) a distinct and valuable epistemic state. Confirmation as a social-psychological act (bebaioō — to make firm, to guarantee) parallels commitment-device research (Ariely, again) and formal-guarantee research in contract psychology: an explicit act of confirmation measurably increases the confidence and behavioural reliance of the party receiving it, beyond what an unconfirmed statement of intent produces. Ametamelētos (without change of mind, irrevocable) names the psychological state of a decision that has genuinely passed the point of reconsideration — related to, but stronger than, Heckhausen and Gollwitzer's "crossing the Rubicon" account of the shift from deliberation to committed intention (discussed in the will/resolve literature under M64).

**Emergence — what causes certainty and confirmation to arise:**
Genuine, well-founded certainty arises from adequate evidence and a track record of reliability in the source being trusted; confirmation as an act arises when a party with standing to do so formally and explicitly guarantees a prior statement or commitment, closing off ambiguity about whether it will be honoured. The "sound" sub-vocabulary in this section is drawn from a distinct semantic field (audible sound, including the trumpet-blast of salpizō) that has been grouped with certainty/sureness terms largely because of shared English glosses ("sound" as in healthy/certain versus "sound" as in noise) — flagged explicitly in the outside-science inventory below as likely gloss-collision rather than a genuine phenomenological grouping.

**Reduction and absence — what causes certainty/confirmation to fail or be withheld:**
Certainty is undermined by inadequate evidence, by prior unreliability in the source, or by deliberate ambiguity. Withheld confirmation (a promise or claim left unconfirmed) leaves the receiving party in a documented state of lower behavioural confidence and reduced willingness to commit or rely, even where the underlying claim may in fact be true.

**Location — where certainty and confirmation are seated:**
Cognitive (the epistemic state of confidence, and its calibration against actual reliability) and social-relational (confirmation as an act performed by one party for the benefit of another's confidence and reliance).

**External influences:** *Track record:* A source's documented past reliability is the single strongest predictor of how much warranted certainty its future statements or commitments produce. *Cultural/institutional:* Formal confirmation mechanisms (oaths, contracts, seals, witnesses) are cultural technologies developed specifically to convert an unconfirmed claim into one that can be relied upon with confidence — directly relevant background for bebaioō's usage.

**Coverage assessment:** Moderate. Confidence-calibration and commitment-device research provide genuine coverage for the certainty/confirmation core of this section; the "sound" (audible) sub-vocabulary is better addressed, where it is genuinely about sound as noise rather than as certainty, by psychoacoustics (see M65 background) than by epistemic-certainty research, and its presence here likely reflects an English-gloss collision rather than a real semantic grouping.

## Outside-science inventory

- **irrevocable (ametamelētos) as a divine attribute** — where this term names God's own gifts and calling as irrevocable (Romans 11:29), the theological claim of divine constancy exceeds the psychology of strong human commitment, which describes firm resolve but not an attribute grounded in an unchanging divine nature.
- **sound (ēchos, fthongos) / to sound a trumpet (salpizō) — likely gloss-collision flag** — these audible-sound terms appear grouped into this cluster via the shared English word "sound" with the sincerity/soundness ("sound doctrine," "sound mind") terms; Session B should verify whether any of these carry genuine certainty/sincerity content in context or whether they belong more naturally with M65's speech/voice vocabulary.
- **truth as grounded in the character of God** — as with live M13, this cluster's straightness/sincerity vocabulary, where it is predicated of God or of truth as such, is ultimately grounded theologically in God's own nature rather than in social utility; science addresses honesty and sincerity as valuable because they enable cooperation and trust, not as expressions of an ontologically true God.

**Observation:** M62 has good-to-moderate science coverage across sincerity, straightness, and certainty. Its most notable structural finding is the likely gloss-collision in the "sound" sub-vocabulary (Section 3), worth checking against context in Session B rather than assuming a single phenomenon unites all of it.

## D9.1.1 / D9.2.1 / D11.2.1 / D12.1.1 prompt reference notes

**D9.1.1 — Physiological/neural substrate:** No distinct substrate is documented for sincerity or straightness beyond general moral-cognition and self-referential processing regions. Certainty/confidence has a well-documented neural correlate in confidence-signalling activity (notably in the ventromedial prefrontal cortex and anterior cingulate) that is measurably dissociable from actual accuracy — the neural basis of the calibration gap Kahneman and Tversky document behaviourally.

**D9.2.1 — Generational transmission:** Sincerity and straightness (character consistency) transmit generationally chiefly through modelling and moral formation within family and community, similar to the integrity-transmission pathway discussed under M13. No distinct generational-transmission literature exists for certainty/confirmation as such — reliability track-records are built and assessed within a generation rather than inherited across one.

**D11.2.1 — Innate endowment vs. acquired:** None of this cluster's core phenomena are innate; sincerity, straightness, and well-calibrated certainty are all acquired through formation, habituation, and — for certainty specifically — accumulated, evaluated experience with a given source's reliability.

**D12.1.1 — Social/behavioural manifestation:** Sincerity manifests as consistency between private conviction and public expression, testable by observers over time. Straightness manifests as conduct or communication that does not depart from an acknowledged standard even under pressure to do so. Confirmation manifests as an explicit, standing-backed act that measurably increases a receiving party's behavioural confidence and willingness to rely on a prior claim or commitment.
