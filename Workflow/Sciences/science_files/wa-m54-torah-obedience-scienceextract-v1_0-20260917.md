## Science sections

### Section 1 — Obedience to law and command as internalised versus compliant response

**Primary field:** Social and developmental psychology — moral internalisation research

**Glosses covered:**
obedience (hupakoē), obedient (hupēkoos), to obey (peitharcheō), to obey (hupakouō), commandment (mits.vah), instruction (to.rah), obedience (yiq.qe.hah), law (nomos)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Milgram's obedience experiments (*Obedience to Authority*, 1974) remain the most influential demonstration that legitimate authority can produce high rates of compliance even against a person's own moral instincts — but Milgram's paradigm describes obedience to an external, often impersonal authority under situational pressure, which is a different psychological phenomenon from the internalised law-keeping this cluster's vocabulary primarily names. Deci and Ryan's self-determination theory (*Intrinsic Motivation and Self-Determination in Human Behavior*, 1985) provides a better-fitting framework: it distinguishes external regulation (compliance to avoid punishment or gain reward), introjected regulation (compliance driven by internalised guilt or pride without full ownership), and integrated regulation (the norm is fully owned as an expression of one's own values) — a spectrum along which "obedience" can range from mere compliance to willing, identity-consistent observance. Kohlberg's stages of moral development (*The Philosophy of Moral Development*, 1981) similarly distinguish law-keeping motivated by fear of punishment (pre-conventional) from law-keeping motivated by internalised principle (post-conventional). Hupakoē (obedience, literally "hearing under" — to hear submissively) and to.rah (instruction, teaching — not primarily "rule" but "direction given by a teacher") both carry a relational, taught quality distinct from impersonal rule-compliance.

**Emergence — what causes law-obedience to arise:**
Obedience research (Milgram, Cialdini) identifies legitimate authority, proximity, and graduated commitment as drivers of situational compliance. Moral-internalisation research identifies a different and more durable pathway: obedience that arises from the person's own values having come to align with the command, typically produced through relationship with the law-giver (trust, respect, a sense of being taught rather than merely commanded), through repeated positive experience of the command's good effects, and through community modelling and reinforcement. To.rah's character as instruction rather than bare decree is psychologically significant here — self-determination theory finds that regulations presented with rationale and within a relationship of respect are far more readily internalised than regulations imposed as bare, unexplained authority.

**Reduction and absence — what causes disobedience or merely external compliance:**
Reactance theory (Brehm, *A Theory of Psychological Reactance*, 1966) shows that constraints perceived as arbitrary or freedom-threatening provoke resistance, including resistance to commands the person might otherwise accept — a risk specifically for law presented without explanation or relationship. Compliance remains merely external (and reverts readily once external pressure is removed) when the underlying rationale is not understood, trusted, or valued. Full internalisation fails when the authority is perceived as illegitimate, inconsistent, or acting against the follower's genuine good.

**Location — where obedience is seated:**
Ranges across the compliance spectrum from behavioural (external regulation — located in the immediate social situation and its incentives) to volitional and characterological (integrated regulation — located in the person's own settled values). Hupakoē's listening-root suggests an attentive, receptive orientation of the whole person as prior to the behavioural act of compliance.

**External influences:**
*Developmental:* Parenting style is one of the strongest predictors of which end of the internalisation spectrum a person's rule-following settles at — authoritative parenting (warm, with explained boundaries) reliably produces more internalised, willingly-owned obedience than either permissive or purely authoritarian parenting, which tend to produce either weak internalisation or merely external, resentment-laden compliance. *Hereditary:* Conscientiousness (Big Five), with moderate heritability, predicts general rule-following tendency. *Cultural:* High power-distance cultures (Hofstede) normalise a more externally-compliant obedience style; the relational, taught quality of to.rah sits in some tension with a purely power-distance framing.

**Coverage assessment:** Moderate. Self-determination theory and moral-internalisation research provide a solid general framework for the internalised/compliant distinction the cluster's vocabulary implies; the cluster itself is small (eight distinct terms) and the specifically covenantal quality of Torah-obedience — obedience as the appropriate response within an existing, trusted relationship with the law-giver — is only partially reachable by secular internalisation research, which typically studies rule-systems without a personal, covenantal law-giver behind them.

## Outside-science inventory

- **Torah (to.rah) as divine instruction and covenant charter** — Torah's status as revealed instruction from the Covenant Lord, and obedience to it as the appropriate covenantal response of a redeemed people (rather than obedience to an impersonal legal code), is a theological category. Internalisation research addresses how people come to own a norm as their own; it does not address the claim that this particular norm originates in, and expresses the character of, God.
- **commandment (mits.vah) in its full covenantal scope** — mits.vah names commands given within the specific covenant relationship established at Sinai; the theological weight of covenant obedience (as gratitude-response, as distinguishing mark of the people of God) exceeds general compliance psychology.
- **false testimony (pseudomarturia)** — flagged here as an apparent classification anomaly: this term (bearing false witness) sits more naturally with deception/integrity vocabulary than with obedience-to-law vocabulary proper; its inclusion in this cluster is worth checking against the ninth-commandment context from which it is likely drawn, for Session B review.

**Observation:** M54 has moderate, largely indirect science coverage. Its central analytical interest for the programme — the distinction between obedience as compliance and obedience as an internalised, relationally-grounded response to instruction from one who is trusted — is well served by the self-determination-theory and moral-internalisation literature even though that literature was not developed with covenant law in view.

## D9.1.1 / D9.2.1 / D11.2.1 / D12.1.1 prompt reference notes

**D9.1.1 — Physiological/neural substrate:** No cluster-specific physiological substrate is documented; obedience/compliance more generally engages prefrontal executive-control regions (inhibiting competing impulses to conform to a rule) and, where compliance is coerced or resented, the same stress-response physiology documented for constraint more broadly (see M01 Fear, Section 5, for the physiology of response to coercive external force).

**D9.2.1 — Generational transmission:** Obedience style (internalised versus merely compliant) transmits generationally chiefly through parenting style and through the community's own modelling of law-keeping — children raised in households and communities that model obedience as glad, understood response show higher rates of internalised (rather than merely compliant) adult rule-following.

**D11.2.1 — Innate endowment vs. acquired:** General rule-following tendency has a modest heritable component via conscientiousness, but the specific quality of obedience this cluster is concerned with — internalised, relationally-grounded observance of instruction versus bare external compliance — is substantially acquired through relationship, teaching, and repeated experience rather than innate.

**D12.1.1 — Social/behavioural manifestation:** Internalised obedience manifests behaviourally as consistency that persists without external monitoring or threat of sanction, and as the person's own endorsement of the command's rationale when asked; merely external compliance manifests as rule-following that is contingent on surveillance or incentive and that lapses readily once these are removed — a distinction Session B may find useful when testing which register a given verse's obedience vocabulary is drawing on.
