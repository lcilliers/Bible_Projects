## Science sections

### Section 1 — Social bonding, belonging, and community

**Primary field:** Social psychology / community psychology

**Glosses covered:**
brotherhood (G0081 adelfotēs), sister (G0079, H0269), community (H2416D), companion (H2270), fellow slave (G4790 sundoulos as background — a shared-belonging term), to share (G3330, G3348, G4829, G4909, G4934), unity (G1775 henotēs), to connect (H2836B), to agree (G1843, G4852, H2164), assembly (G1577 ekklēsia, G3831, H4721, H4744, H6116, H6951, H6952)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Baumeister and Leary's need-to-belong hypothesis (*Psychological Bulletin*, 1995) proposes belonging as a fundamental human motivation — an innate drive to form and maintain lasting, positive relationships, whose deprivation produces predictable negative psychological and physical health effects. Dunbar's social brain hypothesis (*Proceedings of the Royal Society*, 1992) addresses the cognitive constraints on sustainable social group size (the "Dunbar number," approximately 150), relevant to understanding the scale at which genuine fellowship-community, as against mere large-group membership, can be sustained. Sarason's psychological sense of community framework addresses the subjective components of belonging — membership, influence, integration, and shared emotional connection. Cacioppo's loneliness research (*Loneliness*, 2008) establishes perceived social isolation as comparably damaging to physical health as smoking roughly fifteen cigarettes a day — underscoring that community and fellowship are not merely pleasant but load-bearing for wellbeing.

**Emergence — what causes social bonding and belonging to arise:**
Social bonding arises through repeated positive interaction (Zajonc's mere-exposure effect), through shared experience — especially shared adversity, which is among the strongest known accelerants of group cohesion (Festinger) — through mutual vulnerability and disclosure (Aron's self-expansion research), and through shared identity (Tajfel and Turner's social identity theory).

**Reduction and absence — what causes belonging to diminish:**
Belonging diminishes through relational rupture and betrayal, social exclusion and ostracism (Leary's ostracism research), loss of shared identity, and sustained absence of positive interaction.

**Location — where social bonding is seated:**
Relational at its core — a property of the space between persons — with the psychological experience of belonging registered internally as felt security, warmth, and the sense of being known and valued. Neurologically, bonding engages oxytocin and dopaminergic reward systems.

**External influences:**
*Developmental:* Secure childhood attachment is the primary foundation for adult social-bonding capacity. *Cultural:* Collectivist cultures cultivate strong in-group belonging; individualist cultures cultivate more bounded, selective bonding. *Circumstances:* Shared adversity is among the most powerful community-building forces documented.

**Coverage assessment:** Good. Social psychology of belonging is well developed and directly relevant.

### Section 2 — Covenant-making and formal relational commitment

**Primary field:** Anthropology and psychology of commitment devices / promise-keeping research

**Glosses covered:**
covenant (G1242 diathēkē, H1285 berit), to make a covenant (G1303 diatithēmi, H3772H "to cut [covenant]"), to betroth (G0718, G3423, H0781), to contend sharply (G1264 — noted separately, overlap with Section 3)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
As addressed at M42 (Section 4), commitment-device research (Ariely and Wertenbroch, 2002; the broader precommitment literature) treats formalised, public commitment as substantially more binding and more reliably kept than private intention, because it raises the social and self-consistency cost of breach. Anthropology of covenant and treaty-making (Mendenhall and Baltzer's comparative work on ancient Near Eastern suzerain-vassal treaty forms, which the biblical covenant texts themselves formally echo) documents covenant-cutting as a well-attested ancient practice with a standard structure — parties, stipulations, witnesses, sanctions — designed precisely to make a relational commitment maximally durable and publicly accountable. Betrothal specifically has cross-cultural anthropological documentation as a formalising step that moves a relationship from provisional to committed and publicly recognised status, generally preceding full marriage by a period during which the commitment is regarded as already binding.

**Emergence — what causes covenant-making to arise:**
Covenant-making arises when parties seek to establish a relationship on a footing more durable and binding than ordinary goodwill or private agreement — typically where the stakes (property, protection, succession, or, in the biblical material, a relationship with lasting theological consequence) are high enough to warrant the cost of formalisation. The "cutting" language (karat berit) reflects the ancient Near Eastern practice of a ritual act (commonly involving divided animals) accompanying covenant formation, a practice with documented parallels across the ancient Near Eastern treaty corpus.

**Reduction and absence — what causes covenant commitment to diminish or fail:**
Formal commitments, per the precommitment literature, are more robust than informal ones but are not unbreakable — breach occurs where the cost of violation is perceived as lower than the benefit, where enforcement mechanisms (witnesses, sanctions) are absent or ineffective, or where one party's circumstances change dramatically enough to override the original commitment's binding force. Covenant literature (ancient Near Eastern treaty studies) documents that treaty violation was treated with extreme social and often religious seriousness precisely because covenant was designed to be maximally durable — its breach carried correspondingly high social and theological weight.

**Location — where covenant-making is seated:**
Explicitly relational and formal-behavioural — covenant exists as a structured, witnessed, and typically ritually marked act between parties, not as a private internal disposition. Its psychological weight (felt obligation, the sense of being bound) is a downstream internal effect of the external formal act, consistent with commitment-device research's general finding that formal, public commitment produces stronger felt obligation than private intention alone.

**External influences:**
*Cultural:* The specific form covenant-making takes (ritual acts, witnesses, written terms) is culturally and historically variable, though the underlying function — durable, accountable relational commitment — recurs across very different covenant/treaty traditions. *Social:* The presence of witnesses and enforceable sanctions substantially strengthens covenant durability, consistent with general commitment-device findings.

**Coverage assessment:** Moderate. Commitment-device psychology and comparative ancient Near Eastern treaty anthropology together provide genuine structural coverage of covenant-making as a human practice; the theological content of covenant with God specifically (see Outside-science inventory) exceeds this.

### Section 3 — Enmity, contention, and relational opposition

**Primary field:** Social psychology — conflict research

**Glosses covered:**
enmity (H0342 eyvah), to contend (G1864 epagōnizomai, G4866 antagōnizomai-family, H7378 riv, H8474 tacharah), to contend sharply (G1264)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Sherif's Robbers Cave experiment (*Intergroup Conflict and Cooperation*, 1961) demonstrated that intergroup conflict arises readily from competition over scarce resources and is reduced by superordinate goals requiring cooperation. Dehumanisation research (Bandura on moral disengagement; Leyens on infrahumanisation) addresses how enmity is sustained through the denial of full human status to the opposed party, making harm psychologically more permissible. The contention vocabulary here (riv, a term that spans interpersonal dispute and formal legal contest) spans a range from structured legal disagreement to relational hostility — a breadth the underlying Hebrew shares with much dispute-resolution and conflict-psychology literature, which similarly treats formal, rule-governed contention (litigation, arbitration) and unstructured relational hostility as related but distinguishable phenomena.

**Emergence — what causes enmity and contention to arise:**
Enmity arises through competition for scarce or valued resources, perceived injustice or offence triggering retributive hostility, identity or value incompatibility, and social categorisation of the other as out-group (which automatically reduces empathy and increases readiness to harm). Contention in its more formal, legal-dispute sense arises from unresolved competing claims requiring adjudication.

**Reduction and absence — what causes enmity to diminish:**
Intergroup enmity is reduced by superordinate cooperative goals, by contact under conditions of equal status (Allport's contact hypothesis), and by personalisation of the opposed party (countering dehumanisation). Interpersonal enmity is reduced through restored justice, forgiveness, and rebuilt trust.

**Location — where enmity is seated:**
A relational orientation seated in the inner being's evaluation of and settled disposition toward the other — combining negative evaluation, active hostility, and settled opposition of will — while also, in its formal-contention sense, existing as a structured social/legal process.

**External influences:**
*Social:* Group membership and social norms powerfully shape who is designated as enemy. *Cultural and historical:* Traditions of enmity can sustain hostility for generations beyond the originating conflict. *Circumstances:* Genuine harm done by the other party is a primary driver.

**Coverage assessment:** Moderate. Intergroup-conflict research provides real coverage; the theological register (enmity between humanity and God, Genesis 3:15; Romans 5:10) is outside science.

## Outside-science inventory

- **Covenant with God (as against covenant between human parties)** — the theological weight of God's covenants with Israel, Abraham, and in Christ exceeds anthropological and commitment-device accounts of covenant, which describe the human mechanics of durable formal commitment but cannot address a covenant in which one party is God himself.
- **Unity (henotēs) in its ecclesial register** — the unity of the body of Christ, grounded theologically in the unity of the Triune God (John 17), is outside social psychology's account of group cohesion, which addresses only the social mechanisms of belonging.
- **Betrothal in its covenantal-typological dimension** — marriage as an image of God's relationship with his people (Hosea; Ephesians 5) is a theological category beyond the anthropology of betrothal as a relational-formalisation practice.
- **Enmity (evah) in its Genesis 3:15 register** — the enmity between the seed of the woman and the seed of the serpent, foundational to the programme's understanding of spiritual conflict, is a theological category with no parallel in intergroup-conflict psychology, which addresses only conflict between human parties.

**Observation:** M44's outside-science content, as with M32, concentrates at the point where the cluster's genuinely covered human-level phenomena (belonging, covenant-making, enmity) involve God as a party — either as covenant-partner or as the ultimate ground of the unity or enmity in question. The horizontal, human-to-human dimensions of this cluster are well covered.

## D9.1.1 / D9.2.1 / D11.2.1 / D12.1.1 prompt reference notes

**D9.1.1 — Physiological/neural substrate:** Documented for social bonding specifically (oxytocin and dopaminergic reward-system involvement in belonging and attachment) and, separately, for the stress-response engagement characteristic of enmity and conflict (overlapping with the anger/threat substrate discussed at M02). Covenant-making as a formal act has no dedicated neural substrate of its own beyond the general commitment/promise-keeping literature, which is behavioural and social-cognitive rather than centred on a specific brain mechanism.

**D9.2.1 — Generational transmission:** Substantial for belonging capacity (attachment-mediated, transmitted across generations of caregiving pattern) and for enmity (documented intergenerational transmission of intergroup hostility through narrative, socialisation, and, in some research, epigenetic stress-pathway effects in populations with sustained historical conflict). Covenant-making itself is not a heritable trait but its social form (treaty and covenant conventions) is culturally transmitted across generations of practice.

**D11.2.1 — Innate endowment vs. acquired:** Mixed. The drive to belong (Baumeister and Leary) appears to be a fundamental, plausibly innate human motivation. The specific practices of covenant-making, betrothal, and formal dispute-contention are acquired cultural forms. Enmity's underlying mechanisms (in-group/out-group categorisation, dehumanisation under threat) have a plausible innate substrate (evolved intergroup-threat detection), while its specific objects and intensity are substantially shaped by acquired experience and culture.

**D12.1.1 — Social/behavioural manifestation:** Maximal across the whole cluster — belonging, covenant-making, and enmity are all inherently relational and behaviourally/socially enacted, never purely private inner states. This is one of the most thoroughly social clusters in the current batch.
