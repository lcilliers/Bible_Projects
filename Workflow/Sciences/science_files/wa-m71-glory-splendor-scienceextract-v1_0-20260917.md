## Science sections

### Section 1 — Honor, status, and social recognition

**Primary field:** Social psychology — status and prestige research

**Glosses covered:**
worthy (G0514 axios), to deem worthy (G0515 axioō), to honor (G5091 timaō, H1921/H1922 hadar/hadar), honor (G5092 timē, H1923 hadar, H3367 yeqar, H5082 nadiv), to honor: many (H3513I kabed)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Cheng and Tracy's dual-strategy model of status (*Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 2010, and subsequent programme) is the primary contemporary framework, distinguishing dominance (status obtained through intimidation and coercive control, cf. M72) from prestige (status freely conferred by others in recognition of genuine competence or generosity). Honour vocabulary of the kind in this section — timē, kabed (to give weight to, to honour) — maps onto the prestige route: value ascribed by others' voluntary recognition rather than seized by force. Fiske's respect and status research addresses the psychological need to be seen as worthy of regard, and the measurable wellbeing cost of chronic disrespect or disregard. Honour-shame anthropology (Pitt-Rivers, and its application to the biblical world by Malina and others) documents that many ancient Mediterranean and Near Eastern cultures organised social life around public honour and its opposite (shame, M07) far more centrally than modern individualist cultures — an important cross-cultural qualifier for reading this vocabulary.

**Emergence — what causes honour/status to arise:**
Prestige-based honour arises through demonstrated competence, generosity, or moral excellence that others voluntarily recognise and confer — it cannot be self-conferred in the way dominance-based status can be seized. Kabed's root sense ("weight," "heaviness") suggests that honour, in this vocabulary's own logic, is what a person's substance and character are found, on examination, actually to carry — an internal-then-external structure that aligns with the prestige rather than dominance route.

**Reduction and absence — what causes honour to diminish:**
Honour diminishes through exposed incompetence, moral failure, or public humiliation (the direct social-psychological opposite of prestige-conferral), and through the withdrawal of others' voluntary recognition. Because prestige (unlike dominance) depends on others' free assent, it is comparatively fragile — it can be lost through a single well-publicised failure even where genuine competence persists.

**Location — where honour is seated:**
Irreducibly relational and social — honour does not exist for an isolated individual; it is constituted in the regard of a community. Its internal correlate (self-respect, dignity) is cognitive and affective, but the term itself names a social transaction, not merely an inner state — comparable in this respect to M70's support vocabulary and M72's authority vocabulary.

**External influences:**
*Cultural:* Honour-shame cultures organise vastly more of social life around this dimension than individualist, guilt-oriented cultures (Benedict's classic anthropological distinction, refined substantially since); the biblical corpus is produced predominantly within honour-culture settings. *Developmental:* Early experience of being honoured or dishonoured by caregivers shapes baseline sensitivity to status transactions in adulthood. *Social structure:* Formal hierarchies and their legitimacy determine which routes to honour (birth, achievement, generosity, office) are culturally available.

**Coverage assessment:** Good. Status and prestige psychology is a well-developed, actively growing field with direct application.

### Section 2 — Splendor, magnificence, and aesthetic glory

**Primary field:** Psychology of awe / empirical aesthetics

**Glosses covered:**
be glorious (H0142 adar), greatness (H1420 gedulah, H1433 gedulah, H4768 rabuth), adornment (H1927 hadarah), splendor (H1935 hod, H2122 hadar-Aramaic, H3314 yephah), glorious (G2016 endoxos, H3520A kavod), palace/[implicitly royal magnificence via H2287 to celebrate]

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Keltner and Haidt's foundational awe research (2003; already established for M01 Section 4 and directly relevant here) identifies perceived vastness and the need for cognitive accommodation as awe's two core features — splendour and glory vocabulary of the "hod/hadar" type describes precisely the kind of magnificence (visual, relational, or conceptual vastness) that reliably elicits awe. Silvia's empirical aesthetics research (*Emotion*, 2005, and subsequent) addresses the specific positive-emotional and cognitive-interest response to encountering beauty and grandeur, distinguishing it from awe proper (aesthetic pleasure need not involve the vastness/accommodation structure awe requires, though the two frequently co-occur). Kavod (glory, weightiness — cognate in structure to timē's "weight" logic in Section 1) is the central Hebrew theological term this section brushes against; its aesthetic-magnificence register (visible, overwhelming splendour, as in theophany accounts) is the science-addressable edge of a term whose full theological weight exceeds this section.

**Emergence — what causes the perception of splendor/glory to arise:**
Splendour is perceived when a stimulus — visual magnificence, scale, ornamentation, radiance — exceeds ordinary categories of the beautiful or the great, producing the awe response's characteristic self-diminishment and absorbed attention. Adornment (hadarah) names the deliberate cultivation of this effect — the intentional production of splendour through ornament, dress, or architecture, a universal human practice with roots in status signalling (costly signalling theory — displaying resources or skill that would be difficult to fake) as well as pure aesthetic delight.

**Reduction and absence — what causes the sense of splendor to diminish:**
As with M01 Section 4's awe treatment, familiarity and habituation are the primary reducers — repeated exposure diminishes a stimulus's capacity to overwhelm existing categories. Splendour perceived as merely ostentatious (disconnected from genuine substance, cf. the honour/dishonour distinction in Section 1) can also collapse into its negative pole — perceived as vanity or pretension rather than genuine glory.

**Location — where splendor is seated:**
Primarily perceptual-aesthetic and emotional, with the same physiological accompaniments documented for awe (goosebumps, altered time perception, a felt sense of smallness before something vast). It is directed outward (toward the object perceived as splendid) more than inward, though the response it produces in the perceiver is genuinely internal.

**External influences:**
*Cultural:* What counts as splendid or magnificent is heavily culturally conditioned (architectural, sartorial, and ceremonial conventions vary enormously), though the underlying awe/aesthetic-pleasure capacity appears to be a human universal. *Developmental:* Exposure to grandeur (natural, artistic, ceremonial) in childhood cultivates the capacity to perceive and respond to splendour later in life (paralleling M01 Section 4's developmental note on awe).

**Coverage assessment:** Good, with substantial overlap already noted with M01 Section 4 — the analyst should expect real convergence between this section and the earlier awe treatment rather than duplication-free independence.

### Section 3 — Boasting, glorying, and self-presentation

**Primary field:** Social psychology of self-presentation / positive psychology of pride

**Glosses covered:**
to celebrate (G2165 euphrainō, H2287 chagag), to glory with (G4888 sugkauchaomai)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Tracy and Robins's authentic/hubristic pride distinction (*Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 2007) is directly applicable: authentic pride (attributed to effort and genuine achievement, associated with genuine self-esteem and prosocial behaviour) is empirically distinct from hubristic pride (attributed to innate superiority, associated with narcissism and aggression). Sugkauchaomai's specific sense — glorying together, shared boasting — points to a communal, other-inclusive form of celebratory self-report that the individual-pride literature does not fully capture; it is closer to collective celebration (Durkheim's collective effervescence, also relevant to M84's shouting vocabulary) than to solitary self-congratulation.

**Emergence — what causes celebratory glorying to arise:**
Celebration and glorying arise upon the recognition of an achieved good — a victory, deliverance, or blessing — that is significant enough to warrant public marking. Shared glorying (sugkauchaomai) specifically arises in community, where the good being marked is understood as belonging to the group rather than to any one member alone.

**Location — where this is seated:**
Affective and social — celebratory glorying is felt as expansive positive affect and expressed through communal ritual and speech; it is inherently public rather than private.

**Coverage assessment:** Thin. The authentic/hubristic pride distinction and collective-effervescence research provide partial, useful coverage.

## Outside-science inventory

- **the glory of God (kavod in its theophanic/ontological register)** — God's own glory as a manifest, self-existent attribute (not conferred by human recognition, unlike Section 1's honour) is a theological category exceeding any human status-psychology account.
- **glory as eschatological transformation** — believers glorified in the resurrection, or "glory to glory" transformation (2 Cor 3:18), is a theological category of ontological change that science does not address.
- **glory in weakness / the crucified Lord of glory** — the theological inversion in which glory is disclosed through suffering and humiliation rather than magnificence is a direct challenge to, not an extension of, the status/prestige and awe/splendour models above.

**Observation:** This cluster's outside-science content is substantial and concentrated precisely at the point where "glory" ceases to mean human-recognised honour or perceived magnificence and instead names an attribute or act of God. The programme's Session B should expect a sharp divide between the human-register sections above and the divine-glory content the verse evidence will surface.

## Catalogue prompt reference notes (D9.1.1 / D9.2.1 / D11.2.1 / D12.1.1)

**D9.1.1 — Physiological/neural substrate:** Splendour/awe perception shares the general awe-response substrate already noted for M01 (autonomic markers — goosebumps, altered time perception — alongside reward-circuitry engagement). Honour/status has a well-documented neural correlate in social reward processing (ventral striatum activation on receiving social approval; threat-related activation on status loss).

**D9.2.1 — Generational transmission:** Sensitivity to honour and status transactions is substantially shaped by family and cultural transmission (honour-culture upbringing produces markedly different status-sensitivity than individualist upbringing) rather than by direct heredity. Aesthetic responsiveness to splendour is cultivated through early exposure, similarly a transmission rather than heredity pathway.

**D11.2.1 — Innate endowment vs. acquired:** The underlying capacities (status-sensitivity, awe-responsiveness) appear to be innate, cross-culturally universal human endowments; what counts as honourable or splendid, and how intensely the capacities are expressed, is heavily acquired/culturally shaped.

**D12.1.1 — Social/behavioural manifestation:** Both honour and splendour are, by definition, publicly manifest phenomena — they exist in visible recognition, display, ceremony, and communal celebration rather than as purely private inner states, making this cluster's behavioural-manifestation dimension unusually prominent relative to more interior clusters (contrast M20 Doubt or M03 Grief's more private registers).
