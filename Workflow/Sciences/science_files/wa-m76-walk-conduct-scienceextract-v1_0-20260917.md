## Purpose

This document is the cluster science review for M76. It is produced as a pre-analytical asset for the D9.1.1 / D9.2.1 / D11.2.1 / D12.1.1 catalogue prompts. It does not constitute the biblical analysis.

The live gloss list (10 terms, queried 2026-09-17) covers two poles of a single "way of life" metaphor: sustained, directional conduct (peripateō-family "to walk in/among," nahag/halak-family "to conduct," "way: conduct" H1870G, "to follow after" G1872) and departure from the path (H5074/H5110/H7686 "to wander," H7683/H8582 "to go astray"). This cluster is unusual in the programme in that its entire vocabulary is built on a single embodied metaphor — walking as the source domain for habitual moral and relational conduct — making conceptual-metaphor research and habit psychology jointly the primary lenses.

## Science sections

### Section 1 — Habitual conduct and the formation of a "way of life"

**Primary field:** Social/cognitive psychology — habit and identity-based behaviour research

**Glosses covered:**
to walk in/among (G1704 peripateō/emperipateō), to follow after (G1872 epakoloutheō), to conduct (H2986 halak-causative, H5329 nahag), way: conduct (H1870G derek)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Lakoff and Johnson's conceptual metaphor theory (*Metaphors We Live By*, 1980; *Philosophy in the Flesh*, 1999) establishes that "life is a journey" is one of the most cross-culturally pervasive structuring metaphors in human cognition — walking, path, and destination vocabulary is not a decorative borrowing but the natural embodied schema through which humans conceptualise sustained conduct, moral direction, and life-course progress. This directly explains why derek ("way") functions in the biblical corpus as the default term for a person's habitual moral conduct, not merely their literal movement. Wood and Neal's habit research (*Psychological Review*, 2007, and the subsequent extensive habit-formation literature) documents that sustained conduct is substantially governed by automated, context-cued habit rather than by ongoing deliberate choice — a "walk" (peripateō, used pervasively in the New Testament epistles for a sustained pattern of life, e.g. "walk in the Spirit," "walk worthy") names precisely this settled, largely automatic behavioural pattern rather than a series of independent decisions. Bandura's self-concept and social-cognitive theory addresses how a person's conduct becomes consistent with an internalised identity over time — "following after" (epakoloutheō) suggests conduct patterned on an exemplar, directly relevant to modelling research on how observed conduct shapes a follower's own habitual pattern.

**Emergence — what causes a settled "walk"/way of conduct to arise:**
A sustained way of conduct arises through the consolidation of repeated behaviour into automated habit (Wood and Neal — habits form through the repeated pairing of a context cue and a response, strengthening until the behaviour requires little deliberate intention), reinforced by identity commitment (conduct consistent with who a person understands themselves to be) and by modelling (conduct patterned on an admired or authoritative exemplar, the epakoloutheō dimension). Environmental and social context is a stronger driver of sustained conduct than momentary willpower — the same context repeatedly encountered strongly predicts the same habitual response.

**Reduction and absence — what causes a settled way of conduct to change:**
Established habitual conduct is notoriously resistant to change through willpower or information alone; effective change research (context disruption — altering the cues that trigger the old habit; substituting a new consistent response in the same context; sustained repetition of the replacement pattern) is far more reliable than intention alone. Community and relational context that models and reinforces a different "way" measurably accelerates change, consistent with the biblical pattern of communal exhortation to walk differently rather than mere individual resolution.

**Location — where a "way of conduct" is seated:**
Distributed across cognitive (represented standards and self-concept), behavioural (automated habitual response), and social (context and modelling) domains rather than located in any single inner faculty — consistent with habit research's general finding that behaviour is substantially a property of person-in-context rather than person alone.

**External influences:**
*Social:* The single strongest influence on sustained conduct — communities and relationships that model and reinforce a given "way" shape individual conduct more reliably than private resolution. *Developmental:* Early-formed habitual patterns (established in childhood and adolescence) are more deeply automated and thus harder to change than adult-formed ones. *Circumstances:* Stable environmental cues support stable habitual conduct; disrupted environments (relocation, major life transition) create windows of unusual openness to conduct change, since old habit cues are absent.

**Coverage assessment:** Good. Habit psychology and conceptual metaphor theory are both mature fields with direct application to this section's vocabulary.

### Section 2 — Wandering, straying, and departure from the path

**Primary field:** Behavioural psychology — relapse and moral-drift research

**Glosses covered:**
to wander (H5074 nadad, H5110 nud, H7686 shagah), to go astray (H7683 shagag, H8582 ta'ah)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Marlatt's relapse prevention model (*Relapse Prevention*, 1985) addresses the well-documented pattern by which departure from a sustained behavioural commitment typically occurs not as a single decisive break but through a gradual sequence of small, seemingly minor lapses that erode the original commitment's hold — the "abstinence violation effect," in which an initial small lapse produces guilt and self-attribution ("I've already failed") that paradoxically makes full departure more likely rather than less. This maps closely onto the distinction within this section's own vocabulary between shagag (to go astray unintentionally, an error/inadvertence sense) and ta'ah/shagah (a more general wandering or reeling off course) — the Hebrew vocabulary itself seems to distinguish inadvertent drift from more culpable straying, a distinction with a genuine behavioural-science parallel in the difference between an unplanned lapse and a sustained pattern of departure. Sampson and Laub's life-course criminology (*Crime in the Making*, 1993, and the subsequent long-run follow-up literature) documents that life trajectories are shaped less by fixed early-life determination than by "turning points" — later-life events and relationships (marriage, stable employment, a formative relationship) that can redirect an established trajectory of wandering or drift, whether toward further departure or back toward a stable path.

**Emergence — what causes wandering/straying to arise:**
Departure from a settled way arises through: unintentional drift (small, individually low-stakes lapses accumulating without a single decisive moment of rebellion — the shagag pattern); situational disruption of the habitual context that previously supported sustained conduct; and the erosion of the internalised standard itself (a gradual loss of conviction about the way's worth, paralleling M69's note on conviction as a precondition for sustained self-control).

**Reduction and absence — what causes wandering to diminish (return to the path):**
Return from wandering is most reliably supported by turning-point events and relationships (Sampson and Laub) rather than by will alone, by prompt response to an initial lapse (relapse-prevention research shows that treating a first lapse as a single event rather than as evidence of total failure substantially reduces the likelihood of full relapse), and by restored relational/community anchoring in the original "way."

**Location — where wandering/straying is seated:**
Behavioural and cognitive — a departure from a previously maintained pattern, located in the same habit and self-concept systems discussed in Section 1, now moving in the opposite direction. The wandering vocabulary's literal sense (nadad, nud — physical wandering, homelessness, restlessness) also carries a genuine somatic/behavioural dimension worth noting: displacement and lack of a fixed dwelling are themselves documented stressors (see also M78's related burden vocabulary).

**External influences:**
*Circumstances:* Disrupted environments and the loss of previously supportive social contexts are common precipitants of drift. *Social:* Peer and community influence can either accelerate wandering (association with others already off the path) or arrest it (turning-point relationships). *Developmental:* Early-established "ways" are, as in Section 1, more resistant to permanent departure even when temporary wandering occurs, since the underlying habitual and identity structure remains available for return.

**Coverage assessment:** Moderate. Relapse-prevention and life-course criminology research each provide a genuine, if partial, point of contact; the moral-theological weight the biblical vocabulary places on straying exceeds what behavioural drift research alone captures.

## Outside-science inventory

- **walking with God / walking in the Spirit** — the theological category of conduct empowered and directed by God's own presence (Enoch "walked with God," Gen 5:24; "walk by the Spirit," Gal 5:16) names a relational-empowerment structure exceeding habit psychology's purely horizontal account.
- **the Way as a title for the Christian movement/gospel path** — derek/hodos used as a proper designation for the community and its teaching (Acts' repeated use of "the Way") is a theological-ecclesial category, not merely a metaphor for personal conduct.
- **straying from God as covenant infidelity** — going astray (ta'ah, shagah) applied to Israel's departure from covenant loyalty carries theological weight (spiritual adultery, covenant breach) beyond ordinary behavioural drift.

**Observation:** This cluster's content is almost entirely describable through the walking/journey conceptual metaphor, giving it unusually strong, well-matched science coverage on the human-conduct side; the outside-science content is concentrated specifically where the "way" being walked, or departed from, is defined as relationship with or fidelity to God rather than a generic behavioural standard.

## Catalogue prompt reference notes (D9.1.1 / D9.2.1 / D11.2.1 / D12.1.1)

**D9.1.1 — Physiological/neural substrate:** Habitual conduct (Section 1) is substantially governed by basal-ganglia-mediated procedural/habit circuitry (distinct from the prefrontal deliberate-choice systems discussed under M69), which is precisely why established habits are experienced as effortless and automatic rather than as ongoing decisions. Literal wandering/restlessness (nadad, nud) also has a documented physiological dimension in sleep and restlessness research, worth noting as a minor secondary substrate.

**D9.2.1 — Generational transmission:** Conduct patterns ("ways") are substantially transmitted across generations through modelling and shared community practice (the epakoloutheō/"following after" dynamic) rather than through heredity — children's habitual conduct patterns closely track the patterns modelled in their formative community, a well-documented social-transmission pathway.

**D11.2.1 — Innate endowment vs. acquired:** Almost entirely acquired — a "way of conduct" is, by definition, a pattern built up over time through repetition and modelling rather than an innate given; the underlying capacity for habit formation itself, however, is an innate, universal feature of human cognitive architecture.

**D12.1.1 — Social/behavioural manifestation:** This cluster is definitionally behavioural — "walk" language names sustained, observable conduct over time rather than a momentary inner state, making it one of the clearest cases in the programme of a vocabulary set whose primary referent is enacted pattern rather than felt experience, even though the metaphor's source domain (bodily walking) keeps the somatic dimension present throughout.
