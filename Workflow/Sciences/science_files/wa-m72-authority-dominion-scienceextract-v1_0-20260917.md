## Science sections

### Section 1 — Power, authority, and social dominance

**Primary field:** Social psychology / evolutionary psychology — dominance and status research

**Glosses covered:**
authority (G1849 exousia, G5247 huperochē, H2632 chosen), to have authority (G1850 exousiazō), to master (G2634 katakurieuō), to lord over (G2961 kurieuō), to domineer (G0831 authenteō, H7989 domineering, H7980/H7981 sha.lat family), lord: master (G2962H kurios), lordship (G2963 kuriotēs), to rule (G1018/G2715/H4910/H7287A/H7336/H8323A), rule (G2583/H4896/H7985), hand: power (H3027H), ruler (H8269), leader (H5387A, H7218H)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Keltner, Gruenfeld and Anderson's approach-inhibition theory of power (*Psychological Review*, 2003) proposes that high power produces an approach-oriented psychological state (action, optimism, attention to reward, reduced perspective-taking) while low power produces an inhibition-oriented state (caution, heightened attention to threat). French and Raven's five bases of social power (1959 — reward, coercive, legitimate, referent, expert) remain foundational for distinguishing legitimate authority (exousia, delegated and recognised) from raw coercive dominance (sha.lat/domineering). Milgram's obedience research demonstrated the extraordinary extent to which legitimate authority shapes behaviour even against a subject's own moral judgement. Cheng and Tracy's dominance/prestige dual-strategy model (2010, also cited in M71) draws the same distinction from the status-attainment side: dominance is status obtained and held through the capacity to inflict cost, prestige through voluntarily conferred recognition — the biblical vocabulary's split between exousia/kurios (which can be either legitimate or coercive depending on context) and the explicitly domineering terms (katakurieuō, authenteō) tracks this distinction closely.

**Emergence — what causes power and authority to arise:**
Power arises through social recognition of capacity, status, or legitimacy, or — in its dominance form — through the successful, sustained capacity to inflict cost on those who resist. Legitimate authority arises through formal structures (appointment, inheritance, election) or earned reputation for competence and reliability; the biblical vocabulary's exousia (delegated authority, "the right to") captures the legitimacy dimension distinctly from the raw-force dimension named by domineering (katakurieuō, authenteō — terms notably used in the New Testament precisely to be renounced, cf. Matt 20:25–26, as a model leaders are told not to follow).

**Reduction and absence — what causes power to diminish:**
Power diminishes through the withdrawal of recognition, the failure of the competence or reliability that supported it, legitimate challenge, or forcible displacement. Keltner's "power paradox" — that holding power measurably reduces the empathy and social attunement that often produced it in the first place — is among the more counterintuitive and well-replicated findings in this literature, and is directly relevant to why domineering forms of rule (Section named above) tend to become self-undermining over time.

**Location — where power and authority are seated:**
Power is irreducibly relational — it does not exist in an individual alone but in the relationship between an authority and those who recognise or submit to it (the same relational-location pattern noted for M70's support vocabulary and M71's honour vocabulary). Neurologically, power experience activates reward circuitry and measurably suppresses the social-cognition systems associated with perspective-taking; psychologically, it is located in the self's capacity to act on the world with reduced constraint from others' responses.

**External influences:**
*Social structures:* Authority structures are institutionally and culturally determined — what counts as legitimate power varies enormously across systems of appointment, inheritance, election, and conquest. *Developmental:* Early experiences of efficacy and social recognition shape adult power orientation; children raised in environments of arbitrary, unaccountable authority develop different expectations of legitimate rule than those raised under consistent, explained authority. *Hereditary:* Dominance-related traits (assertiveness, risk tolerance, testosterone-linked behaviour patterns) show heritable components.

**Coverage assessment:** Good. Social psychology of power and status is well-developed and directly relevant.

### Section 2 — Kingship, governance, and political power

**Primary field:** Political psychology / sociology of the state

**Glosses covered:**
kingdom (G0932 basileia, H4437/H4467/H4468 mam.la.khah/mal.khut family), palace (G0933, H0643/H1002/H1055), kingly (G0934 basileios), royal (G0937 basilikos), queen (G0938G basilissa), throne (H3678G kisse), royalty (H4438 malkuth), king (H4428G/H4430 melekh), to reign (G0936/H4427A/H7786), to reign with (G4821 sumbasileuō), hosts (G4519 sabaōth, H6635B [Lord of] Hosts), Most High [God] (H5943), dominion (H4474/H4475/H4915B/H4951), domineering (H7989), ruling (H7990)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Political psychology addresses the psychology of governance, legitimacy, and leadership; the sociology of the state (Weber's tripartite typology of authority — traditional, charismatic, legal-rational — remains foundational) addresses kingship as a social institution built on one or a blend of these legitimating grounds. Leadership research (Bass — transformational versus transactional leadership; Burns) is relevant to the governance dimension of the "reigning" vocabulary. Sabaōth/"Lord of Hosts" in its military-collective-power dimension connects to social psychology of military organisation and collective identity/cohesion research, though the term's primary content (the LORD commanding heavenly armies) is a theological title exceeding this literature's scope (see Outside-science inventory).

**Emergence — what causes royal/political power to arise:**
Political authority arises through a combination of legitimation (the recognised right to rule, by whichever of Weber's three grounds), the capacity to compel compliance, and the ongoing consent (explicit or tacit) of those governed. Kingship as an institution is a cultural universal in complex, stratified societies, typically emerging where a single figure or lineage consolidates military, economic, and symbolic-religious authority together.

**Location — where political power is seated:**
Primarily social and institutional rather than individual — political power is a property of social structures, symbols, rituals, and the ongoing consent of subjects, not something an individual holds in isolation from the community that recognises the office. The throne, palace, and royal regalia vocabulary names the material and symbolic apparatus through which this social reality is made visible and stable.

**External influences:**
*Cultural:* The form, legitimation, and limits of political power are entirely culturally and historically shaped — hereditary monarchy, charismatic leadership, and legal-rational bureaucracy each produce different psychological relationships between ruler and ruled. *Circumstances:* War, economic disruption, and social crisis are the primary conditions under which existing power structures are challenged or collapse, and under which new claimants to authority most readily arise.

**Coverage assessment:** Thin for direct psychology; the political-science and sociological dimensions are broader than the psychological. Outside-science content is proportionally large for this section, as it was for the equivalent material in the old M23 file.

### Section 3 — Domineering, mastery, and coercive control

**Primary field:** Social psychology — coercion and compliance research

**Glosses covered:**
to domineer (G0831 authenteō, H7980/H7989 sha.lat family), to master (G2634 katakurieuō), to lord over (G2961 kurieuō — negative-valence instances), domineering (H7989)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Cialdini's compliance research (*Influence*, 1984) addresses the mechanisms by which people are moved to comply with requests or demands, distinguishing persuasion from outright coercion. Seligman's learned helplessness research addresses the psychological consequences of prolonged, inescapable coercive control — a state of passivity, cognitive impairment, and emotional dysregulation that persists even after the controlling condition is removed. Patterson's coercive family-process model documents how coercive control dynamics, once established in a relationship, become self-reinforcing (each party's behaviour trains the other's escalation), a pattern with plausible application to sustained domineering leadership relationships more broadly.

**Emergence — what causes domineering coercion to arise:**
Domineering control arises when a power-holder exercises capacity over others without the consent dimension that distinguishes legitimate authority (Section 1) from coercion — the defining feature is not power's mere presence but the absence of a mutual, recognised legitimacy governing its use. Authenteō and katakurieuō are both marked, in their New Testament contexts, as departures from a positive norm (servant leadership) rather than as neutral descriptions of rule — the vocabulary itself carries an evaluative charge that the science literature's own dominance/prestige distinction independently corroborates.

**Reduction and absence — what causes domineering control to diminish:**
Domineering control reduces through the withdrawal of the coerced party's compliance (where costly resistance is possible), through external intervention, or through the power-holder's voluntary renunciation of coercive means in favour of legitimated authority — the explicit New Testament pattern (Matt 20:25–26; Mark 10:42–43) of contrasting Gentile-style domineering rule with servant leadership names exactly this reduction as a chosen, not merely circumstantial, change.

**Location — where domineering control is seated:**
Like M01 Section 5's coercion vocabulary, this section's terms primarily describe a relationship and an exercise of force rather than a purely internal state; the psychological effects on those subjected to it (fear, learned helplessness, chronic stress) are located in the same neurological and somatic systems already discussed under M01 and M73.

**External influences:**
*Social and political:* The conditions enabling domineering coercion are structural — concentrated, unaccountable power, weak reciprocal obligation, and the absence of checks that would otherwise convert raw dominance into recognised, legitimated authority.

**Coverage assessment:** Thin but genuine, mirroring the old M23 Section 5 assessment — the literature addresses the coerced party's inner response more thoroughly than the domineering party's own psychology.

## Outside-science inventory

- **almighty / Most High / Lord of Hosts** — divine omnipotence and the title naming God as commander of the heavenly armies are theological categories outside science entirely.
- **dominion in its creation-mandate register** — humanity's delegated dominion over creation (Gen 1:26–28) is a theological category of vocation and stewardship, not a human power-psychology phenomenon.
- **the kingdom of God / kingdom in its eschatological register** — basileia as the inbreaking, transforming reign of God that transcends ordinary political structures is a theological reality science does not reach.
- **Christ's kingship and lordship (kurios as confessional title)** — the confession "Jesus is Lord" names an ontological and covenantal claim, not a description of a human social-power relationship, even though the vocabulary is shared.

**Observation:** As with the old M23 file, this cluster spans a wide scientific range — from evolutionary dominance psychology to political sociology — with outside-science content concentrated heavily at the divine-title and eschatological-kingdom end. The Session B analytical challenge carried over from M23 remains live: distinguishing this cluster's inner-being content (how authority, submission, and domineering are experienced) from its extensive political/institutional content, which is not itself the programme's primary interest.

## Catalogue prompt reference notes (D9.1.1 / D9.2.1 / D11.2.1 / D12.1.1)

**D9.1.1 — Physiological/neural substrate:** Power-holding is associated with measurable neuroendocrine correlates (elevated testosterone and reduced cortisol reactivity in high-power states — the "power posture" literature, treated cautiously given replication concerns on some specific findings) and with reward-circuitry engagement; being subject to coercive domination engages the same threat/stress substrate discussed under M01 and M73 (HPA axis activation, learned-helplessness-associated changes in prefrontal-limbic regulation).

**D9.2.1 — Generational transmission:** Patterns of authority relationship are substantially transmitted through family modelling — children raised under either arbitrary/coercive or consistent/legitimated authority carry those templates into adult expectations of rule and submission. Political and institutional authority structures themselves are transmitted culturally/institutionally (dynastic succession, constitutional tradition) rather than biologically.

**D11.2.1 — Innate endowment vs. acquired:** Dominance-seeking and status-sensitivity draw on innate, cross-species social hierarchy mechanisms (well documented in primate ethology); the specific institutional forms authority takes (kingship, delegated legal authority, servant leadership) are acquired/cultural elaborations on that innate substrate, not themselves innate.

**D12.1.1 — Social/behavioural manifestation:** This cluster is almost entirely behavioural/social in its manifestation by definition — authority and dominion exist only as enacted, recognised, and responded-to relationships, visible in command, submission, ceremony, and institutional structure, paralleling M71's honour vocabulary in being inherently public rather than private.
