## Purpose

This document is the cluster science review for M73. It is produced as a pre-analytical asset for the D9.1.1 / D9.2.1 / D11.2.1 / D12.1.1 catalogue prompts. It does not constitute the biblical analysis.

The live gloss list (45 terms, queried 2026-09-17) is dominated by general illness/weakness vocabulary (asthenēs/astheneia family, chalah/choli family — "be sick," "weakness," "disease") which corresponds closely to the old M24 file's Section 1 and is reused substantially below. Beyond that general core, the cluster carries three more specific registers not present in M24's original scope: blindness (tufloō/tuflos, H5786/H5787), crippling/lameness (anapēros, kullos, H5223 nakeh), and named acute or stigmatised conditions — leprosy (H6879 tsara'), epilepsy/seizure (G4583 selēniazomai), and fever (G4445 puressō). These get their own sections below because the science literature treats disability/impairment and socially stigmatised disease as distinct phenomena from generalised illness experience.

## Science sections

### Section 1 — Illness, disease, and generalised bodily weakness

**Primary field:** Health psychology / medicine

**Glosses covered:**
be feverish (G4445), illness (G3554), be sick (G3552), weakness (G0771/G0769G/H2476/H4816), weak: weak (G0770G/G0772G/H2470A), weak: ill (G0770H/G0772H/H2470H), weak-willed woman (G1133), weak (H0536/H0537/H2523/H7504), to weaken (H0535/H2522), be sick (H1738/H2456/H4834/H5136), sick (H1739), illness (H1741/H7829), disease (H4064/H8463), be ill (H3415), be incurable (H0605), ill (G0732)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Health psychology (Ogden, *Health Psychology*, 2012) addresses the psychological dimensions of illness experience — how people appraise, cope with, and adapt to physical disease. The biopsychosocial model (Engel, *Science*, 1977) challenged purely biomedical accounts of illness by demonstrating that psychological and social factors both contribute to and shape disease onset, course, and recovery. Leventhal's illness representations model identifies the cognitive schemas (identity, cause, timeline, consequences, controllability) that shape how patients experience and manage illness — directly relevant to reading the biblical material's frequent pairing of physical sickness with questions of cause and meaning (cf. John 9's disciples asking "who sinned"). Segerstrom and Miller's meta-analysis (*Psychological Bulletin*, 2004) documents that chronic psychological stress measurably suppresses immune function via sustained HPA-axis activation, establishing a genuine (not merely metaphorical) bidirectional link between inner-being distress and bodily disease susceptibility.

**Emergence — what causes illness and weakness to arise:**
Physical illness arises through biological causes — infection, injury, genetic vulnerability, physiological deterioration — with psychological factors (chronic stress, hopelessness, social isolation) measurably influencing onset, severity, and recovery trajectory rather than merely accompanying it. Weakness as subjective experience arises from the gap between the demands a person faces and the physical resources available to meet them, a gap that can be widened either by declining resources or by rising demand.

**Reduction and absence — what causes illness/weakness to diminish:**
Recovery depends on adequate rest, nutrition, treatment, and time, with psychological factors (hope, social support, perceived efficacy) measurably accelerating or impeding it. The social environment of care has documented physiological effects on recovery trajectories independent of treatment itself.

**Location — where illness/weakness is seated:**
Primarily the body — musculoskeletal, immunological, neurological, and metabolic systems — but the experience of weakness is simultaneously affective (the feeling of being unable to function), cognitive (assessment of reduced capacity), and relational (illness changes social position, producing dependency and exposure to others' care or neglect).

**External influences:**
*Hereditary:* Disease vulnerability has significant genetic components. *Social:* Social determinants of health (poverty, healthcare access, social support, discrimination) are among the strongest predictors of illness outcomes. *Circumstances:* Nutrition, safety, and exposure to pathogens are primary drivers. *Developmental:* Adverse early experience increases chronic illness risk in adulthood via sustained stress-axis dysregulation.

**Coverage assessment:** Good. Health psychology and the biopsychosocial model provide direct, well-developed coverage.

### Section 2 — Blindness and impairment: the psychology of disability

**Primary field:** Rehabilitation psychology / disability studies

**Glosses covered:**
blind (G5185 tuflos, H5787), to blind (G5186 tufloō, H5786), crippled (G0376 anapēros, G2948 kullos, H5223 nakeh)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Wright's classic adjustment-to-disability research (*Physical Disability: A Psychosocial Approach*, 1960, subsequently much developed) established that psychological adjustment to acquired impairment is not a fixed, universal grief trajectory but varies substantially with the meaning the disability is given, the social response it receives, and the person's prior identity resources — a finding that cautions against assuming a single "stages of loss" model applies uniformly. Oliver's social model of disability (*The Politics of Disablement*, 1990) distinguishes impairment (the physical condition itself) from disability (the social and environmental barriers that convert impairment into exclusion) — a distinction with direct relevance to a biblical corpus in which blindness and lameness are repeatedly bound up with social marginalisation (begging, exclusion from full participation) as much as with the physical condition itself. Congenital versus acquired blindness produce measurably different psychological profiles (adjustment is generally, though not universally, harder for later-acquired loss, which involves the loss of an established visual self-concept rather than development without one).

**Emergence — what causes blindness/impairment to arise:**
Biologically through disease, injury, congenital condition, or age-related degeneration. The disabling social effect (as distinct from the impairment itself) arises through environmental and attitudinal barriers — physical blinding (tufloō) is also used causatively in the biblical material (an agent blinding another, whether literally or as a moral-spiritual metaphor), which the social model's impairment/disability distinction helps keep analytically separate from purely biological onset.

**Reduction and absence — what causes impairment-related distress to diminish:**
Adjustment and reduced distress follow from social inclusion, adaptive skill acquisition, and communities that do not convert impairment into exclusion — the social model's central claim is that much of the suffering historically attributed to impairment itself is properly attributable to remediable social barriers.

**Location — where this is seated:**
The biological impairment is located in the relevant sensory or motor system; but per the social model, a large share of the associated suffering is located in the social response to the impairment rather than in the impairment alone — relevant to reading the biblical corpus's consistent linkage of blindness/lameness with begging, exclusion from temple worship (cf. 2 Sam 5:8's polemical usage), and social stigma.

**External influences:**
*Social:* The single largest determinant of whether impairment becomes disabling exclusion. *Developmental:* Congenital versus acquired onset produces different adjustment pathways. *Circumstances:* Access to aids, community support, and economic resources shape outcomes substantially.

**Coverage assessment:** Good and directly relevant — disability psychology has matured considerably as a field and speaks directly to this section's vocabulary.

### Section 3 — Stigmatised and named conditions: leprosy, epilepsy, fever

**Primary field:** Sociology/psychology of illness stigma

**Glosses covered:**
be leprous (H6879), be epileptic (G4583), be numb (H6313)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Goffman's foundational stigma theory (*Stigma: Notes on the Management of Spoiled Identity*, 1963) directly addresses conditions — leprosy (tsara'at) is Goffman's own paradigm historical case — that mark the bearer as socially "spoiled," producing exclusion independent of the condition's actual severity or contagion risk. The biblical leprosy legislation (Lev 13–14) enacts precisely this social-exclusion dynamic through formal, ritualised diagnostic and quarantine procedure, making this one of the clearest points of direct contact between the biblical corpus and stigma theory of any cluster in the programme. Epilepsy has a long documented history as a stigmatised condition historically attributed to spiritual affliction rather than neurological cause (a history the Gospel accounts of "epileptic" healing sit within and partially transform); modern epilepsy psychology (Jacoby's stigma-in-epilepsy research programme) documents that perceived (rather than actual) stigma remains a major predictor of quality-of-life impairment in epilepsy independent of seizure frequency.

**Emergence — what causes stigmatised-condition distress to arise:**
Beyond the direct physiological burden of the condition itself, stigma-related distress arises from social categorisation processes that mark the bearer as contaminated, dangerous, or morally suspect — Goffman's central finding is that this process operates largely independent of the condition's actual medical risk profile.

**Reduction and absence:**
Stigma reduces through accurate public understanding of the condition's actual (rather than imagined) risk and cause, through formal reintegration processes (the biblical leprosy legislation itself includes a formal cleansing and reintegration rite, Lev 14), and through community willingness to receive the previously excluded person back into full participation.

**Location — where this is seated:**
Simultaneously biological (the underlying disease process) and thoroughly social (the stigma response, which frequently causes more lasting inner-being harm than the physical condition itself).

**External influences:**
*Cultural and religious:* Purity/impurity frameworks (biblical and cross-cultural) shape which conditions attract stigma and how exclusion and reintegration are formally managed. *Social:* Community willingness to reintegrate previously stigmatised persons is the primary determinant of long-term psychological outcome.

**Coverage assessment:** Good for the general stigma mechanism (Goffman is directly and unusually applicable); thinner for the specific ancient-Near-Eastern medical and social detail, which sits partly outside psychology's own scope.

## Outside-science inventory

- **sickness as covenantal curse or divine discipline** — illness interpreted within the covenant curses/blessings framework (Deut 28) or as divine formation (cf. M24's original note on Heb 12 discipline) is a theological category science does not adjudicate.
- **healing as a sign of the inbreaking kingdom** — the Gospels' healing narratives (including the blindness and epilepsy healings specifically named in this cluster's gloss list) function theologically as kingdom-signs, not merely as medical remediation; this exceeds health psychology's scope.
- **spiritual blindness (tufloō's metaphorical extension, 2 Cor 4:4)** — blindness as inability to perceive divine reality is a theological category, though the literal-blindness science in Section 2 remains the relevant source domain for the metaphor.
- **the purity-law framework governing leprosy** — the theological-legal structure of clean/unclean and its function within Israel's cultic system exceeds what stigma sociology alone can account for, even where stigma theory illuminates the social mechanism.

**Observation:** This cluster carries forward the old M24 file's core finding that the biopsychosocial model captures why biblical illness vocabulary integrates physical, social, and meaning-making dimensions rather than treating them separately — and extends it with a strong, well-evidenced point of contact in stigma theory for the leprosy and disability-specific content that M24's original scope did not include.

## Catalogue prompt reference notes (D9.1.1 / D9.2.1 / D11.2.1 / D12.1.1)

**D9.1.1 — Physiological/neural substrate:** Each named condition has a distinct substrate — leprosy (Hansen's disease, peripheral nerve and skin involvement), epilepsy (abnormal cortical electrical activity), fever (immune/inflammatory response), blindness (ocular or neural visual-pathway pathology) — while generalised weakness/illness draws on the shared HPA-axis and immune-suppression pathway documented in Section 1.

**D9.2.1 — Generational transmission:** Disease vulnerability generally (Section 1) has genetic components; social responses to stigmatised conditions (Section 3) are transmitted culturally across generations (purity/exclusion norms taught and enacted communally) rather than biologically. Adjustment patterns to impairment (Section 2) are shaped by family modelling of how disability is regarded and discussed.

**D11.2.1 — Innate endowment vs. acquired:** The underlying conditions are overwhelmingly acquired (through injury, infection, or disease process) rather than innate, with congenital blindness as the clear exception requiring its own adjustment-psychology profile (Section 2). The stigma response itself, however, draws on what may be an evolved, largely innate disease-avoidance psychology (the "behavioural immune system" literature — Schaller and Park) that stigmatises visible signs of possible contagion even when actual risk is absent, which is a useful frame for why leprosy specifically attracted such intense exclusion.

**D12.1.1 — Social/behavioural manifestation:** Manifests both as directly observable physical symptom/impairment and, distinctly, as social behaviour toward the afflicted person (exclusion, begging, quarantine, eventual reintegration) — this cluster is one of the clearest cases in the programme where the social response to a condition is itself as significant an inner-being datum (for both sufferer and community) as the condition's physiological substrate.
