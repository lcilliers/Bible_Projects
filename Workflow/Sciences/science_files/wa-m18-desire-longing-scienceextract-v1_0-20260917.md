## Science sections

### Section 1 — Desire, craving, and appetitive longing

**Primary field:** Motivational psychology

**Glosses covered:**
desire (H0183, H0185, H1942, H2530A, H2532A, H2836A, H2837, H4261, H8378, H8669, epithumeō-family G1939, G2442, G2691), longing (H3616, H8375, epipothēsis-family G1971, G1972, G1973, G1974), to long for (G1937, H3700, H7602A, H2968, H6165, H8373), to crave (H0404), to thirst / thirst (G1372, G1373, H6770, H6772), lacking (H2638), one who desires (epithumētēs, G1938), pleasure-loving (G5369), very hungry (G4361)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Motivational psychology distinguishes appetitive desire (drive states tied to bodily need — hunger, thirst) from higher-order acquired desire (wanting objects, status, or outcomes not tied to immediate physiological need), while recognising that biblical and everyday language frequently uses the same vocabulary (thirst, hunger, craving) metaphorically across both registers — a usage pattern this cluster's own vocabulary shares. Berridge and Robinson's incentive-salience theory (*Psychological Review*, 1998) distinguishes "wanting" (the dopaminergic drive to pursue a reward) from "liking" (the hedonic pleasure of obtaining it) — the two can dissociate, such that a person can want something intensely without it delivering proportionate pleasure once obtained, a finding directly relevant to the biblical wisdom literature's recurring observation that satisfied desire does not reliably satisfy (Ecclesiastes 6:7). Epithumeō in the New Testament spans both a positive register (desiring good gifts, longing for God) and a negative register (lusting after the forbidden) within the same lexical root — consistent with motivational psychology's treatment of desire as valence-neutral in itself, taking its moral character from its object.

**Emergence — what causes desire, craving, and longing to arise:**
Appetitive desire arises from physiological need states (hunger, thirst) signalled through well-characterised homeostatic and hedonic brain systems. Acquired desire arises from the perception of something as valuable and not yet possessed — Berridge and Robinson's incentive-salience research shows that repeated reward exposure can inflate "wanting" independent of, and sometimes far beyond, actual "liking," a mechanism directly relevant to escalating craving. Longing (as distinct from acute craving) carries a more sustained, often wistful quality — a forward-reaching desire for something genuinely valued but not immediately attainable.

**Reduction and absence — what causes desire, craving, and longing to diminish:**
Appetitive desire is resolved by attainment (eating, drinking) through normal homeostatic mechanisms. Acquired desire and craving are harder to resolve by attainment alone — per incentive-salience theory, repeated satisfaction of an inflated "wanting" state does not reliably reduce the wanting, which is part of why habitual craving (including addictive craving) resists simple satiation. Longing is resolved by the actual arrival of its object, or diminishes through the abandonment of the hoped-for outcome as unattainable.

**Location — where desire and longing are seated:**
Primarily motivational, with a genuine physiological substrate for appetitive desire (hypothalamic and dopaminergic systems) and a more diffusely cognitive-emotional substrate for longing and acquired desire. The "wanting"/"liking" dissociation documented by Berridge and Robinson shows that the felt pull of desire and the pleasure of its satisfaction are not the same system, which bears directly on why longing can persist even where past attainment has proved disappointing.

**External influences:**
*Hereditary:* Baseline reward-system sensitivity (dopaminergic reactivity) has documented heritable components relevant to how strongly desire and craving are experienced. *Developmental:* Early patterns of need-satisfaction and deprivation shape later appetitive and acquisitive desire intensity. *Cultural and circumstantial:* What is treated as desirable, and the social permission or prohibition around pursuing it, are strongly culturally shaped; genuine scarcity intensifies appetitive desire through normal homeostatic mechanisms.

**Coverage assessment:** Good. Motivational psychology and incentive-salience research provide strong, directly applicable coverage.

### Section 2 — Delight and pleasure

**Primary field:** Positive psychology / affective science — hedonic research

**Glosses covered:**
delight (H2531, H2654A, H2655, H2656, H5730B, H6026, H6027, H8173B, H8191), pleasant / pleasantness (H2896A, H5273A, H5276, H5278, H5282), pleasing (G0701, H2895), to delight (G2106, G4913, G2237 pleasure)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Kringelbach and Berridge's hedonic-hotspot research (*Trends in Cognitive Sciences*, 2009) identifies specific, small brain regions (in the nucleus accumbens and orbitofrontal cortex) whose activation is necessary for genuine pleasure/liking, distinct from the broader "wanting" circuitry addressed in Section 1 — a mechanistic basis for why an object can be desired without being genuinely enjoyed, and enjoyed without having been strongly desired beforehand. Fredrickson's broaden-and-build theory of positive emotion addresses delight's downstream cognitive effects — positive affect measurably broadens attention and builds durable psychological, social, and cognitive resources over time, distinct from its momentary hedonic value.

**Emergence — what causes delight and pleasure to arise:**
Delight arises from the direct encounter with something appraised as genuinely good, beautiful, or satisfying — engaging the hedonic-hotspot circuitry Kringelbach and Berridge identify, distinct from the anticipatory "wanting" system of Section 1. Pleasantness as a quality attributed to a person, object, or relationship names the disposition to reliably produce this response.

**Reduction and absence — what causes delight to diminish:**
Hedonic adaptation (the well-documented tendency for repeated exposure to a pleasurable stimulus to yield diminishing pleasure over time) is the primary mechanism by which delight fades even where the object itself is unchanged. Depression and anhedonia represent the clinical extreme — the hedonic-hotspot system's reduced responsiveness such that formerly delightful things no longer produce pleasure.

**Location — where delight is seated:**
A genuinely embodied affective state with a well-localised (if small) neural substrate, distinct from the broader motivational "wanting" system of Section 1 — the dissociation between these two systems is one of the more robust and analytically useful findings in this cluster's science coverage.

**External influences:**
*Developmental:* Early experience of delight offered and received (rather than only need met) shapes later capacity for pleasure. *Circumstantial:* Novelty and genuine goodness of the object both contribute to delight's intensity; over-familiarity reduces it via hedonic adaptation. *Cultural:* What is culturally sanctioned as legitimately delightful varies, though the underlying hedonic machinery appears to be a human universal.

**Coverage assessment:** Good. Hedonic and positive-affect research provide strong, well-localised coverage.

### Section 3 — Jealousy and zeal

**Primary field:** Social / evolutionary psychology — jealousy and zeal research

**Glosses covered:**
be jealous / jealous / jealousy (H7065, H7067H, H7068, H7072, H7067G — including "Jealous [God]"), zeal (G2205), zealot / Zealot (G2207, G2208, G2581)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Jealousy is motivationally distinct from ordinary desire: it arises specifically from perceived threat to a valued relationship or resource one already possesses, rather than from wanting something not yet possessed (Parrott and Smith, 1993). Evolutionary psychology treats jealousy as an adaptation protecting valuable social bonds and resources (Buss's mate-guarding research shows cross-cultural consistency in jealousy elicitors). Zeal and jealousy share a documented conceptual root across languages — Greek zēlos, like Hebrew qana, covers both "passionate protective jealousy" and "zealous, fervent pursuit," a semantic overlap the science of motivation explains by treating both as high-intensity, high-investment orientations toward a valued object, differing mainly in whether the object is already possessed (jealousy, protecting) or not yet attained (zeal, pursuing).

**Emergence — what causes jealousy and zeal to arise:**
Jealousy arises when a valued relationship, status, or possession is perceived as threatened by a rival or third party — the precondition is that the threatened thing is genuinely valued; jealousy does not arise over what one is indifferent to. Zeal arises from intense valuation of a not-yet-fully-attained goal combined with a felt readiness to act decisively and immediately in its pursuit or defence, frequently including preparedness to override ordinary social caution or personal cost.

**Reduction and absence — what causes jealousy and zeal to diminish:**
Jealousy diminishes when the perceived threat to the valued relationship or resource is resolved, or through the security that comes from a stable, trusted bond. Zeal diminishes when the valued goal is attained, is abandoned as unattainable, or loses its perceived importance relative to competing priorities.

**Location — where jealousy and zeal are seated:**
Both are primarily emotional and motivational, with strong behavioural output (protective or pursuing action) and a cognitive monitoring component (vigilance for threat, in jealousy; vigilance for opportunity to act, in zeal).

**External influences:**
*Hereditary:* Jealousy-proneness has some heritable component via attachment style and temperamental sensitivity; the jealousy mechanism itself is treated in evolutionary psychology as a species-typical, evolved response. *Attachment:* Anxious attachment significantly elevates jealousy reactivity; secure attachment reduces it. *Cultural:* Cultures vary substantially in jealousy and zeal norms and their socially sanctioned expression (the first-century Jewish "Zealot" movement being a specific historical instance of politically-directed zeal).

**Coverage assessment:** Good. Jealousy has a well-developed evolutionary and social-psychology literature; zeal is less independently studied but is well explained by the same high-investment-orientation framework.

### Section 4 — Will, willingness, and acceptance

**Primary field:** Cognitive psychology / philosophy of action — volitional psychology

**Glosses covered:**
be willing (H2974, H5069, H5068), will/desire (thelō, thelēma, G2307, G2309), acceptance (ra.tson, H7522, G0594, G4356), eagerness (prothumia, G4288), be precious (H3365, H3368, G5093), to cut off: to gain (H1214I)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Heckhausen and Gollwitzer's Rubicon model of action phases (*American Psychologist*, 1987) distinguishes the motivational phase (deliberating between options and forming an intention) from the volitional phase (planning and executing the intended action) — once an intention is formed, a qualitatively different motivational state takes over. Eagerness (prothumia) maps onto the early motivational phase — the state of readiness and forward-leaning engagement before action is taken. Acceptance research (Baumeister and Leary's need-to-belong framework, extended into approval-seeking research) addresses the psychology of being received, approved, or found favourable — ra.tson's semantic range (favour, delight, acceptance, goodwill) spans both the human dimension (social acceptance) and, in this cluster's material, a divine-favour register addressed further below.

**Emergence — what causes willingness, will, and the desire for acceptance to arise:**
Willingness arises when a potential action is evaluated positively and the person forms a settled, non-coerced intention to pursue it — ya.al/na.dav-type willingness in Hebrew usage frequently carries this voluntary, freely-chosen quality specifically, distinct from mere compliance. Eagerness arises when the intention is held with positive affect and forward anticipation. The desire for acceptance arises from the fundamental human need to belong and be favourably regarded by others (or, in this cluster's material, by God).

**Reduction and absence — what causes willingness to diminish, or the desire for acceptance to go unmet:**
Willingness diminishes when the evaluation of the action turns negative, when competing intentions take priority, or when self-regulatory resources are depleted; coercive pressure tends to convert willing action into reluctant compliance, eliminating the inner quality that makes willingness distinct from mere obedience. Unmet acceptance-seeking is a well-documented source of chronic distress in the belonging literature.

**Location — where will, willingness, and acceptance-seeking are seated:**
Volition is primarily cognitive with a genuine affective dimension — eagerness and willingness are felt states, not merely cognitive computations, and are experienced as originating from within the self as the inner being's own forward-directed energy. Acceptance-seeking is inherently relational, requiring another party whose favourable regard is sought.

**External influences:**
*Social:* Willingness is shaped by relational context — people act more willingly when they feel genuinely supported and respected rather than coerced. *Developmental:* The capacity for deliberate volitional action develops through childhood and adolescence alongside executive-function maturation. *Cultural:* The value placed on autonomous willing versus obedient following varies by cultural context.

**Coverage assessment:** Moderate. Volitional psychology and need-to-belong research together provide solid, if not exhaustive, coverage; some overlap with M64's own will/resolve territory should be reviewed in Session B given both clusters carry related vocabulary.

## Outside-science inventory

- **Jealous [God] (H7067G)** — divine jealousy as the passionate, covenant-protective attribute of God is a theological category. Science addresses human jealousy as an evolved protective response to relational threat; it does not adjudicate the claim that God's jealousy is a holy, rightly-ordered attribute rather than a human-style possessive emotion.
- **acceptance (ra.tson) in its divine-favour register** — the experience of being accepted by God, and God's gracious disposition toward his people, exceeds social-acceptance psychology's account of interpersonal approval-seeking.
- **will/desire (thelēma) in its divine-will register** — God's will as the governing purpose of all things is a theological category outside the philosophy-of-action account of human volition addressed in Section 4.

**Observation:** M18's outside-science content clusters at the divine-attribute end — jealousy, acceptance, and will each have a human register well covered by science and a divine register that is not. The cluster's most analytically distinctive finding is the shared conceptual root of jealousy and zeal (Section 3) and the wanting/liking dissociation between desire (Section 1) and delight (Section 2) — both genuine scientific findings that may illuminate why the biblical vocabulary groups these registers together rather than treating them as unrelated.

## D9/D11/D12 prompt reference notes

> These four catalogue questions (`D9.1.1`, `D9.2.1`, `D11.2.1`, `D12.1.1`) replace the retired `T7.3` prompt as of the 2026-09-17 catalogue realignment (`catalogue_version='v2-dimension-realign-20260917'`). Each is answered here from this document's science sections as the pre-analytical data input; the verse-evidence side of each question is completed at the characteristic-level answer stage, not here.

**D9.1.1 — Physiological/Neural Substrate:** Strong. This cluster has some of the best-localised substrate findings in this batch: Berridge and Robinson's incentive-salience research cleanly separates the "wanting" (dopaminergic, mesolimbic) substrate of desire (Section 1) from the "liking" (opioid, hedonic-hotspot) substrate of delight (Section 2) — a genuine mechanistic distinction, not merely descriptive. Jealousy and zeal (Section 3) engage overlapping threat/arousal and reward circuitry. Volition (Section 4) is associated with prefrontal executive systems.

**D9.2.1 — Generational Transmission:** Moderate. Attachment style (shaping jealousy sensitivity, Section 3) and early patterns of need-satisfaction (shaping desire and delight intensity, Sections 1–2) are both documented to transmit across generations through parenting and early relational experience, though this cluster's transmission evidence is less direct than, for example, M05's or M13's.

**D11.2.1 — Innate Endowment:** Strong for the substrate, weaker for specific content. Appetitive desire (hunger, thirst) and the wanting/liking dissociation are genuinely innate, evolved systems present from birth; the jealousy mechanism is treated by evolutionary psychology as a species-typical evolved response. The specific objects of acquired desire, zeal, and willingness, however, are shaped by experience and culture on top of this innate substrate.

**D12.1.1 — Social/Behavioural Manifestation:** Mixed. Desire and delight (Sections 1–2) are manifestation-neutral in themselves — they can orient toward either self-directed consumption or shared/relational goods. Jealousy (Section 3) is inherently relational and protective, oriented around an existing bond. Zeal (also Section 3) can manifest either cooperatively (zealous service) or adversarially (the historical Zealot movement's political violence is a documented instance). Acceptance-seeking and willingness (Section 4) are inherently other-directed and relational.
