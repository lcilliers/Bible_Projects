## Science sections

### Section 1 — Encouragement as social and psychological support

**Primary field:** Positive and social psychology — social support research

**Glosses covered:**
encouragement (paraklēsis), to encourage (paramutheomai), to encourage (protrepō), to encourage (sumparakaleō), to encourage (la.vav)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Social support research (Cohen and Wills' buffering hypothesis, *Psychological Bulletin*, 1985) is among the best-replicated findings in health psychology: perceived availability of supportive others buffers the physiological and psychological impact of stress. Bandura's self-efficacy theory (*Self-Efficacy: The Exercise of Control*, 1997) identifies verbal persuasion — encouragement from a credible other — as one of four primary sources of self-efficacy belief, alongside mastery experience, vicarious experience, and physiological state; encouragement that is specific and credible measurably raises a person's belief in their own capacity to act. Positive psychology's broaden-and-build theory (Fredrickson, 2001) shows that positive affect induced by encouragement widens the range of thought-action options available to a person under difficulty, rather than merely making them feel better. Paraklēsis (from para-kaleō, to call alongside) captures a relational structure — encouragement as a form of accompaniment, not merely information transfer.

**Emergence — what causes encouragement to arise and to work:**
Encouragement arises from a perceiver's recognition of another's need, discouragement, or untapped capacity, combined with the relational standing or credibility to speak into that situation. Its effectiveness depends on: the encourager's perceived credibility and goodwill (verbal persuasion is far weaker from a source the recipient does not trust or respect); specificity (general reassurance is less effective than encouragement tied to concrete, believable next steps); and timing (encouragement offered when a person is already at the edge of giving up has disproportionate effect relative to encouragement offered when things are going well). La.vav (to encourage, literally "to hearten" — from the word for heart) names the process as a strengthening of the inner disposition, not merely a change in outward behaviour.

**Reduction and absence — what causes encouragement to fail or be withheld:**
Encouragement fails when it is perceived as generic, insincere, or disconnected from the recipient's actual situation (empty reassurance can be experienced as dismissive rather than supportive — echoing the shame-research finding that surface reassurance without genuine engagement can deepen rather than relieve distress). It is withheld through inattention, competition or rivalry that makes another's success undesirable to the potential encourager, or a community culture that normalises criticism over affirmation. Its absence — sustained discouragement without input from others — is a documented risk factor for helplessness and disengagement (Seligman's learned-helplessness paradigm).

**Location — where encouragement is seated:**
Relational in origin (it requires another person) but internalised in effect — genuine encouragement changes the recipient's own self-efficacy belief and affective state, not merely their momentary mood. It operates at the interface between the social and the motivational-cognitive: words from outside the person become resources the person then draws on from inside.

**External influences:**
*Community structure:* Communities with regular structured opportunities for mutual encouragement (feedback cultures, mentoring relationships) show measurably higher individual self-efficacy and lower attrition under difficulty than communities without them. *Developmental:* Early and consistent encouragement from caregivers shapes baseline self-efficacy and resilience; children who receive primarily critical input develop lower self-efficacy independent of actual competence. *Cultural:* Norms for expressing encouragement (direct praise versus more indirect forms of support) vary across cultures, affecting how encouragement is recognised and received.

**Coverage assessment:** Good. Social support and self-efficacy research provide strong, directly applicable frameworks.

### Section 2 — Building up (edification) as cumulative, constructive investment

**Primary field:** Developmental and organisational psychology — growth-oriented relationship and mentoring research

**Glosses covered:**
building (oikodomē), to build (oikodomeō), to build up/upon (epoikodomeō)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
The oikodomeō (build) vocabulary names a distinct process from momentary encouragement: sustained, cumulative investment in another's growth, using a construction metaphor. Vygotsky's zone of proximal development (*Mind in Society*, 1978) and scaffolding research (Wood, Bruner, and Ross, 1976) describe how a more capable other provides structured support that is progressively withdrawn as competence grows — a direct analogue to "building up" as a deliberate, staged process rather than a single encouraging act. Mentoring research (Kram, *Mentoring at Work*, 1985) distinguishes career-development functions (challenging assignments, exposure, sponsorship) from psychosocial functions (role-modelling, counselling, friendship) — both are forms of "building up" a person's capacity over time. Positive organisational scholarship (Cameron, Dutton, and Quinn, *Positive Organizational Scholarship*, 2003) documents that organisations and communities oriented toward strengths-building rather than deficit-correction show higher individual flourishing and lower turnover.

**Emergence — what causes building-up to occur:**
Building up requires sustained relational investment over time, a genuine orientation toward the other's growth (rather than one's own convenience or advantage), and — per scaffolding research — support calibrated to the recipient's current capacity, neither so much that it fosters dependence nor so little that it exceeds what the recipient can currently integrate. Epoikodomeō (to build further upon) implies a cumulative process — each act of building-up presupposes and extends a foundation already laid, consistent with developmental research showing that growth is sequential and layered rather than achieved in isolated bursts.

**Reduction and absence — what prevents building-up:**
Building-up is undermined by short-termism (investment horizons too brief to see cumulative growth), by relationships oriented toward the builder's own benefit rather than the other's flourishing, and by environments that reward tearing down (criticism, rivalry, status competition) over patient construction. Its absence over a sustained period is associated in the mentoring literature with stalled skill development and lower long-term achievement, independent of raw ability.

**Location — where building-up is seated:**
Relational and developmental — located in an ongoing relationship rather than a single interaction, and oriented toward the recipient's growing capacity over time rather than their momentary state (the key structural difference from Section 1's encouragement). It has cognitive, motivational, and skill-based outcomes in the person being built up.

**External influences:**
*Community structure:* Communities with established mentoring or discipleship structures produce measurably higher rates of sustained personal growth than those relying on ad hoc encouragement alone. *Developmental history:* Individuals who were themselves patiently built up (rather than merely praised) show greater capacity to extend the same patient investment to others. *Circumstances:* Building up requires time and relational continuity that unstable, high-turnover, or crisis-driven environments make difficult to sustain.

**Coverage assessment:** Good. Scaffolding, mentoring, and developmental psychology provide solid, directly applicable coverage.

## Outside-science inventory

- **encouragement (paraklēsis) via the Paraclete** — where paraklēsis and its cognates connect to the Holy Spirit as Paraclete/Comforter (a connection carried by the shared root, not necessarily by every occurrence of the noun), the divine person and work in view is a theological category outside psychology's descriptive reach; science addresses the human experience of being encouraged, not the claim of a divine Comforter at work in it.
- **building up (oikodomeō) applied to the church/body of Christ** — where this vocabulary names the corporate, Spirit-enabled construction of the church as God's own building or temple, the theological and ecclesiological claim exceeds what mentoring and organisational-growth research addresses; science can describe how individuals grow through mutual investment but not the specifically theological claim about what is being built.

**Observation:** M52 has good, directly applicable science coverage for both its sections — the cluster names two closely related but genuinely distinct phenomena (a momentary relational act, encouragement; and a sustained developmental process, building up) that the science literature also treats as distinct. The outside-science content is concentrated at the point where the cluster's vocabulary connects to specifically Christian theological claims (the Spirit as Paraclete, the church as God's building) rather than to the underlying human phenomena themselves.

## D9.1.1 / D9.2.1 / D11.2.1 / D12.1.1 prompt reference notes

**D9.1.1 — Physiological/neural substrate:** Encouragement received activates reward and threat-reduction circuitry consistent with social-support buffering research — reduced cortisol and amygdala reactivity under stress when supportive input is present. No distinct neural substrate is documented for "building up" as a cumulative process; its effects are best measured behaviourally and developmentally (skill and competence growth) rather than via a single physiological marker.

**D9.2.1 — Generational transmission:** Both encouragement style and building-up capacity transmit generationally primarily through modelling — people who were consistently encouraged and patiently built up by caregivers or mentors show a markedly higher tendency to extend the same investment to others, a pattern documented in both attachment and mentoring literatures.

**D11.2.1 — Innate endowment vs. acquired:** Neither encouragement-giving nor building-up capacity appears in the literature as an innate trait; both are acquired relational skills, though baseline temperamental warmth (an innate, heritable disposition toward positive social engagement) likely makes their acquisition easier for some individuals than others.

**D12.1.1 — Social/behavioural manifestation:** Encouragement manifests as specific, credible verbal or relational input offered at a point of need or discouragement. Building up manifests as sustained, staged investment — teaching, correction offered constructively, opportunity-creation, and patient scaffolding of growing competence — recognisable over a longer time horizon than any single encouraging act.
