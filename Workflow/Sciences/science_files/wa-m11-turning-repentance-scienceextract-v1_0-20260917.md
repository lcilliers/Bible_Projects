## Science sections

### Section 1 — Repentance, regret, and behavioural change

**Primary field:** Clinical psychology / moral psychology

**Glosses covered:**
to repent (metamellomai, metanoeo, metanoeō), to be sorry: relent (na.cham, H5162H), contrite (H1793A)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Regret psychology (Zeelenberg and Pieters, *Regret: The Emotion That Shapes Human Behaviour*, 2007) examines regret as a counterfactual emotion, arising from the comparison between what happened and what could have happened, that motivates behaviour change when directed at changeable past actions. Behaviour-change research — Prochaska and DiClemente's transtheoretical model of stages of change (precontemplation, contemplation, preparation, action, maintenance) — addresses the psychological process of turning from one pattern to another as a staged process rather than a single event. Motivational interviewing (Miller and Rollnick) operationalises the relational conditions that facilitate change, notably that ambivalence (the simultaneous desire to change and resistance to it) is normal and must be worked with, not simply overridden.

**Emergence — what causes repentance to arise:**
Repentance arises from the recognition that one's current course is wrong (moral insight), the emotional response of remorse or regret, and the volitional turn toward a different direction. Contrition (H1793A) names the crushed, broken-heartedness register of this response specifically — a deeper and more thoroughgoing form than mere situational regret. Research on behaviour change emphasises that motivational readiness for change varies by stage, and interventions matched to the current stage are markedly more effective than those pitched at a later stage than the person has reached.

**Reduction and absence — what causes repentance not to occur:**
Repentance is blocked by the absence of moral insight (the person does not recognise the wrong), by shame rather than guilt as the dominant response (shame motivates concealment rather than repair — a well-established distinction in the guilt/shame literature, Tangney and Dearing), by denial and self-justification, and by the ambivalence that keeps a person oscillating without ever committing to the turn. Hardness of heart — a biblical category — finds a psychological analogue in the progressive suppression of moral self-monitoring through repeated moral disengagement (Bandura).

**Location — where repentance is seated:**
Cognitive, emotional, and volitional together — full repentance requires recognition of the wrong (cognitive), grief over it (emotional), and a genuine turn (volitional); the absence of any one leaves the process incomplete. Contrition's crushed-heart register places particular weight on the emotional-somatic dimension of this section's vocabulary specifically.

**External influences:**
*Community and accountability:* Communities that practise honest naming of wrong and offer genuine restoration (rather than shame-based condemnation) facilitate repentance; accountability relationships and restorative frameworks support the process. *Cultural:* The availability of a cultural or religious framework for moral repair shapes whether repentance is a live option, or whether a person is instead trapped in shame or self-punishment. *Prior experience:* Having previously experienced genuine restoration after wrongdoing is one of the most powerful motivators for a person's willingness to turn again.

**Coverage assessment:** Good. Regret, behaviour-change, and guilt/shame research together provide solid coverage for this section's vocabulary.

### Section 2 — Turning and returning as directional reorientation

**Primary field:** Cognitive and behavioural psychology — habit and reorientation research

**Glosses covered:**
to return (H8421G, G0344, G0488, G1880, G5290, H5749A), to turn away (G0654, H2559), to turn aside (H5493G, H5493H, H5493I, H7847, H5848A), to turn back (H7734), to turn/wander away (G1624)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
The shuv-family vocabulary of turning and returning has no single direct psychological equivalent, but several literatures bear on it. Habit-reversal and behavioural-redirection research (Wood and Neal's habit theory, *Psychological Review*, 2007) treats the reorientation of an established behavioural pattern as requiring both the interruption of the automatic cue-response link and the establishment of a competing, deliberately chosen path — directly relevant to the biblical vocabulary's spatial metaphor of turning off one path and onto another. Prospect theory and framing research (Kahneman and Tversky) is relevant to "turning aside" as a decision-point phenomenon — small deviations from an original course, if not corrected, compound over distance/time in a way well documented in path-dependence research (the aside-turning need not be large to have large downstream consequence).

**Emergence — what causes turning, returning, and turning aside to arise:**
Turning back (return) arises from the recognition that the original path was correct and the deviation from it was an error, combined with sufficient motivation to retrace the path rather than continue forward from the deviated position. Turning aside (the negative-pole vocabulary in this section) arises through the same general mechanism as any behavioural deviation — a competing cue, temptation, or distraction diverts attention and action from the original trajectory, often incrementally rather than through a single decisive break.

**Reduction and absence — what causes the tendency to turn aside to diminish, or return to be sustained:**
Sustained correction (as opposed to a single act of returning) requires, per habit-reversal research, the establishment of a new stable cue-response pattern, not merely a one-time reversal of direction — a single act of turning back is vulnerable to relapse unless it is followed by sustained reinforcement of the corrected path. Community accountability and periodic reinforcement (regular reorientation practices) are well-documented supports for maintaining a corrected course over time.

**Location — where turning and returning are seated:**
Primarily behavioural and volitional, with the shuv vocabulary's directional/spatial metaphor suggesting a whole-person reorientation (the entire trajectory of the person, not merely an isolated cognition or emotion) rather than a purely internal mental event. This distinguishes it somewhat from Section 1's more explicitly cognitive-emotional-volitional repentance vocabulary — turning names the observable redirection itself.

**External influences:**
*Social:* Models of others who have successfully turned back from a wrong course are a significant facilitator (social learning); communities that make return genuinely welcoming (rather than permanently marking the one who strayed) support sustained return over relapse. *Circumstances:* The presence of continuing situational triggers that originally caused the turning-aside makes sustained return substantially harder — removing or avoiding those triggers is a standard, well-evidenced element of successful behaviour-change maintenance. *Developmental:* The capacity for deliberate self-correction develops through the same executive-function maturation addressed in M15's planning and judgment sections.

**Coverage assessment:** Moderate. No single research literature addresses "turning" as directly as forgiveness or repentance research addresses their own territory, but habit-reversal, path-dependence, and behaviour-change-maintenance research together provide a genuine, applicable framework.

## Outside-science inventory

- **to return (shuv) in its covenantal register** — Israel's national turning back to YHWH after covenant unfaithfulness is a theologically and corporately structured category (covenant, national identity, prophetic call) that exceeds individual behaviour-change psychology's account of habit reorientation.
- **repentance (metanoia) as divinely-enabled** — where the New Testament treats repentance itself as granted by God (e.g., "God grants repentance," Acts 11:18) rather than purely as a human achievement of will, this exceeds the behaviour-change literature's account of repentance as a self-directed process.
- **contrite (H1793A)** in its worship-context register — the "contrite spirit" as what God himself looks to and does not despise (Psalm 51:17) — carries a theological claim about divine response to inner disposition that psychology cannot adjudicate.

**Observation:** M11's outside-science content is concentrated at the covenantal and divinely-enabled ends, consistent with its narrowed scope. With forgiveness moved to M60, this cluster is now a cleaner, more tightly focused instance of "turning" as both an inner moral event (Section 1) and a directional life-pattern (Section 2) — both well covered by science at the human level, with the theological distinctive being the covenantal object of the turning (back to God specifically) and, in the New Testament material, the claim that the capacity to turn is itself a gift.

## D9/D11/D12 prompt reference notes

> These four catalogue questions (`D9.1.1`, `D9.2.1`, `D11.2.1`, `D12.1.1`) replace the retired `T7.3` prompt as of the 2026-09-17 catalogue realignment (`catalogue_version='v2-dimension-realign-20260917'`). Each is answered here from this document's science sections as the pre-analytical data input; the verse-evidence side of each question is completed at the characteristic-level answer stage, not here.

**D9.1.1 — Physiological/Neural Substrate:** Thin to moderate. Repentance and contrition (Section 1) share substrate elements with grief and guilt processing more broadly (activation of regions associated with self-referential evaluation and negative affect), but no distinct neural signature specific to repentance as such has been isolated. Turning/returning (Section 2) is best characterised as behavioural rather than substrate-specific; habit-reversal research does point to competing basal-ganglia cue-response circuits as the relevant mechanism for why a single decision to turn back is often insufficient without sustained retraining.

**D9.2.1 — Generational Transmission:** Moderate. Community and family modelling of how wrongdoing is handled — whether met with shame-based condemnation or restorative welcome — shapes a person's own later capacity for repentance and sustained return (Section 1's community-and-accountability findings); this is a documented, if indirect, generational-transmission pathway rather than a direct hereditary one.

**D11.2.1 — Innate Endowment:** Not supported as such. Neither repentance nor the capacity to turn back from a course is treated in the literature as an innate endowment; both depend on cognitive capacities (moral insight, executive self-regulation) that develop through childhood and are then exercised through deliberate, effortful, often relationally-supported process. This bears on D11.1 as evidence for a capacity built on created cognitive/moral faculties rather than a specific innate behaviour.

**D12.1.1 — Social/Behavioural Manifestation:** Mixed, leaning toward relationally embedded even where not strictly cooperative or competitive. Repentance and turning are, in the science literature, rarely purely private acts — they are consistently shown to be facilitated or hindered by social and community context (welcome versus shame-based exclusion, availability of models who have turned successfully). The behaviour itself (turning back to a corrected path) is self-directed rather than aimed at another party, distinguishing this cluster's manifestation pattern from the clearly cooperative (M05, M39) or clearly adversarial (M06, M10) clusters reviewed in this batch.
