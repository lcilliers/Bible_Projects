## Science sections

### Section 1 — Vitality, life-energy, and psychological aliveness

**Primary field:** Positive psychology / health psychology

**Glosses covered:**
alive (H2416A chay), living (H2424 chay.yah), living thing (H2416C, G2226 zōon), vigorous (H2422), recovery (H4241 mich.yah), to rouse (H5782 ur), kinsfolk (H2416B), life (H2416E, G2222 zōē, G0979 bios), to live (H2418, H2421, H2425, G0980, G1236, G2198 zaō), to prolong (H0748, G3905)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Ryan and Frederick's subjective vitality construct (*Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 1997) defines vitality as a felt sense of aliveness and energy available to the self — distinguished from simple arousal by its positive, engaged quality. Self-determination theory (Ryan and Deci, *Self-Determination Theory*, 2017) identifies the three psychological needs whose satisfaction sustains vitality: autonomy, competence, and relatedness. Recovery research (Sonnentag, Zijlstra) addresses how vitality is restored following depletion. The distinction in the Greek vocabulary between bios (biological/circumstantial life) and zōē (life in its fuller, more comprehensive sense, especially prominent in John's Gospel) has a loose parallel in the distinction positive psychology draws between mere physiological functioning and eudaimonic flourishing — though the theological weight carried by zōē considerably exceeds any psychological construct (see Outside-science inventory).

**Emergence — what causes vitality to arise:**
Vitality arises under conditions of psychological need satisfaction (autonomy, competence, relatedness), through rest and restorative activity following depletion, and through social connection. The "to rouse" vocabulary (ur) suggests vitality as something that can be awakened from a dormant or suppressed state, consistent with recovery research's finding that vitality is renewable rather than a fixed reserve.

**Reduction and absence — what causes vitality to diminish:**
Vitality diminishes through sustained need frustration, physical depletion and illness, existential emptiness (the absence of meaning — Frankl), and depression, whose defining features include a pervasive loss of vitality independent of physical health status. This section stands in direct contrast to M24's despair and weariness content — the same underlying psychological continuum viewed from its positive pole.

**Location — where vitality is seated:**
A whole-person felt state rather than a property of any single system — Ryan and Frederick describe it as phenomenological aliveness, not merely physical energy level. It has physiological correlates (reduced fatigue markers, autonomic balance) but is not reducible to them.

**External influences:**
*Social:* Relational connection is among the strongest known producers of vitality; isolation reliably reduces it. *Developmental:* Secure attachment in childhood establishes a baseline capacity for vitality in adulthood. *Circumstances:* Adverse material or relational circumstances directly suppress vitality; their resolution restores it. *Hereditary:* Baseline energy and temperamental positive affectivity have a documented heritable component.

**Coverage assessment:** Moderate to good. Subjective-vitality research provides direct coverage of the experiential dimension; it does not reach the theological weight (life as gift, zōē as shared divine life) carried by the fuller biblical vocabulary.

### Section 2 — Breath and the biology of life

**Primary field:** Physiology / neuroscience

**Glosses covered:**
spirit/breath: breath (G4151H pneuma), spirit/breath: spirit (G4151G pneuma), to breathe (H3306, H5301, H6315)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Respiratory physiology addresses the mechanics of gas exchange sustaining biological life. Porges' polyvagal theory identifies the breath as a bidirectional interface between the autonomic nervous system and conscious regulation — slow, controlled breathing activates the parasympathetic system, reduces cortisol, and increases heart-rate variability, while the autonomic state in turn shapes breathing pattern. Contemplative neuroscience (Lutz, Davidson) documents measurable neural changes from sustained breath-focused practice. The breath is unique among autonomic processes in being the one that can be voluntarily controlled, making it the primary accessible lever for top-down regulation of otherwise automatic bodily states.

**Emergence — what causes the breath-life connection to be experientially salient:**
Breathing is, biologically, the continuous condition of being alive; its cessation is death. The experience of breath as life is most acute at its boundaries — near-suffocation, the return of breath after exertion or danger — which is likely why breath functions so readily as a symbol for life itself across cultures, not only in the biblical corpus.

**Location — where breath-life is seated:**
Both peripheral (lungs, respiratory musculature) and central (brainstem respiratory drive, with strong connections to limbic and prefrontal regulatory circuits). It is the most accessible interface in the body between voluntary control and otherwise automatic physiological process.

**External influences:**
*Developmental:* Breath as a regulatory tool is cultivated through deliberate practice, not passively inherited. *Cultural:* Numerous traditions (contemplative prayer, meditation, yoga) have independently developed breath-based practice; the neuroscience underlying their common physiological effect is well established even where their frameworks differ.

**Coverage assessment:** Thin on direct biblical-vocabulary coverage, but the physiological mechanism itself is well characterised. The pneuma/ruach terms span both "breath" and "spirit" — the breath sense has physiological coverage; the spirit sense is a theological category (see Outside-science inventory and M47, where the spirit vocabulary is treated at length as part of the constitutional "Inner Seat" cluster).

### Section 3 — Death, dying, and mortality

**Primary field:** Thanatology / health psychology of dying

**Glosses covered:**
decay (G1312 diafthora), half-dead (G2253 hēmithanēs), to put to death (G3499 nekroō), to die/destroy with (G4881 sunapothnēskō)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Kübler-Ross's stage model of dying (*On Death and Dying*, 1969) — since substantially revised and criticised as a rigid sequence but influential in establishing the psychological study of the dying process as a legitimate field — identified denial, anger, bargaining, depression, and acceptance as commonly (though not universally or sequentially) observed responses to terminal diagnosis. Terror management theory (Greenberg, Pyszczynski, Solomon, building on Becker's *The Denial of Death*, 1973) treats the awareness of mortality as a uniquely human cognitive burden that drives much of culture, self-esteem striving, and worldview defence — an influential account of death-awareness as a psychologically formative, not merely biological, fact. The "half-dead" (hēmithanēs) vocabulary, used of the man left by robbers in the Good Samaritan parable, maps onto the clinical picture of severe traumatic injury at the threshold of survival — a distinct psychological and physiological state from either full health or death, characterised by acute crisis and dependency on rescue.

**Emergence — what causes the experience and reality of dying to arise:**
Biological death arises through the cessation of the processes vitality names in Section 1 — organ failure, injury, disease, ageing. Decay (diafthora) names the biological process following death — tissue breakdown — with no meaningful reversal outside intervention. Psychologically, death-awareness (as distinct from the event of dying) arises developmentally in early-to-middle childhood as children acquire the concept of biological irreversibility, and becomes a lifelong background cognitive fact that terror management theory argues shapes behaviour well beyond conscious awareness of it.

**Reduction and absence — what causes death or its felt threat to diminish:**
Biologically, death is reversed by nothing within ordinary medical or physiological process (resuscitation addresses only the earliest window before irreversible cellular damage). Psychologically, the terror of death-awareness is buffered (per terror management theory) by cultural worldview investment, self-esteem, and close relationships — mechanisms that manage the anxiety of mortality without altering its biological finality. The "half-dead" state specifically is reversed only through effective intervention by another — rescue, treatment, care — mapping directly onto the parable's own logic.

**Location — where death and dying are seated:**
Biological death is a whole-organism event, not localised to any single system, though it is proximately caused by failure of a specific vital system. The psychological awareness and management of mortality is cognitive and affective, and — per terror management theory — deeply entangled with identity, culture, and relationship rather than a narrowly private fact.

**External influences:**
*Circumstances:* Proximate cause of death is overwhelmingly circumstantial — injury, disease, violence, age. *Developmental:* The cognitive capacity to understand death as universal, irreversible, and personally applicable develops through childhood and is not present from birth. *Cultural:* Death-awareness management (terror management theory's central finding) is heavily culturally mediated — different worldviews provide different buffers against the same underlying biological fact.

**Coverage assessment:** Moderate. Thanatology and terror management theory provide genuine coverage of the psychological experience of dying and of death-awareness; neither reaches the specifically theological content of death as consequence of sin, or of resurrection life (see Outside-science inventory).

## Outside-science inventory

- **Spirit (pneuma/ruach) in its theological register** — life as God's creative gift breathed into the creature (Genesis 2:7; Ezekiel 37) is a theological category. Physiology addresses breath as biological mechanism; the theological claim is that life originates as gift, not self-sustaining property.
- **Zōē in John's Gospel register** — eternal life as the divine life shared with believers is a theological category whose experiential correlates (joy, vitality, fullness) are partially addressed by positive psychology but whose ontological content — participation in God's own life — is not.
- **Death as consequence of sin (Genesis 3; Romans 5–6)** — the biblical aetiology of death as entering the world through humanity's rupture with God is a theological claim about death's origin and meaning that thanatology, which treats death as a biological universal requiring no special explanation, does not address.
- **Resurrection and the reversal of death** — the claim that death is not final but will be reversed by God's own act is entirely outside science's descriptive scope, which treats biological death as terminal and irreversible by definition.
- **To die/destroy with (sunapothnēskō) in its Pauline register** — dying with Christ as a theological participation, not a literal death, is a category with no scientific parallel.

**Observation:** M25's outside-science content is proportionally large relative to its size. The cluster's core theological claim — that life is gift rather than self-possessed property, and that death, though biologically real and thoroughly describable by thanatology, is not final — sits entirely outside what any of the sciences consulted here can adjudicate. The genuinely covered territory is the subjective experience of vitality, the mechanism of breath, and the psychological process of dying and death-awareness.

## D9.1.1 / D9.2.1 / D11.2.1 / D12.1.1 prompt reference notes

**D9.1.1 — Physiological/neural substrate:** Strong for breath (respiratory physiology, brainstem drive, autonomic regulation) and for death/decay (well-characterised biological process). Vitality has a real but more diffuse substrate (autonomic balance, reduced fatigue markers) consistent with its status as a felt, whole-person state rather than a single-system function.

**D9.2.1 — Generational transmission:** Limited direct heritability literature for vitality or death-awareness as such, though baseline temperamental positive affectivity (relevant to vitality) is moderately heritable. Death-awareness management strategies (terror management theory) are substantially culturally transmitted rather than biologically inherited — children acquire their society's particular buffers against mortality anxiety through socialisation, not through genetic transmission.

**D11.2.1 — Innate endowment vs. acquired:** Mixed. The biological substrate of life and death (respiration, metabolism, organismic vitality) is entirely innate/given, not acquired. The cognitive concept of death as universal and irreversible is acquired developmentally (children do not have adult death-concept from birth) rather than present from birth. Vitality's psychological expression sits between the two — the underlying capacity for aliveness is given, but its expression depends heavily on acquired conditions (need satisfaction, relational security).

**D12.1.1 — Social/behavioural manifestation:** Vitality is behaviourally and socially visible (energy, engagement, positive affect expression) and is itself substantially shaped by social conditions (relatedness). Death and dying are profoundly social events in their human experience even though biologically individual — terror management theory's central finding is that death-awareness management occurs largely through social and cultural mechanisms (worldview investment, relationship, cultural belonging) rather than private cognition alone.
