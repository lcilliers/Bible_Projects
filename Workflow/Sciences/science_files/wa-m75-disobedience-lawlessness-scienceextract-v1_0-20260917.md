## Science sections

### Section 1 — Disobedience and insubordination

**Primary field:** Social psychology — reactance and noncompliance research

**Glosses covered:**
lawlessness (G0458 anomia — general disobedient-disorder sense), insubordinate (G0506 anupotaktos), disobedience (G0543 apeitheia, G3876 parakoē), disobedient (G0545 apeithēs)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Brehm's psychological reactance theory (*A Theory of Psychological Reactance*, 1966) is the primary framework: when people perceive their freedom of action being constrained or threatened, they experience a motivational state (reactance) that drives resistance to the constraint — often including the specific behaviour being prohibited, independent of the behaviour's own appeal. This provides a plausible mechanism for a portion of the disobedience vocabulary, distinguishing reactance-driven resistance to perceived illegitimate constraint from other roots of disobedience. Bandura's moral disengagement research (*Moral Disengagement*, 2016, building on decades of prior work) identifies the specific cognitive mechanisms — euphemistic labelling, displacement of responsibility, minimising consequences, dehumanising the target of harm — by which people disable their own moral self-sanctions and thereby become capable of disobedience or wrongdoing they would otherwise regard as impermissible. Cialdini's compliance research (*Influence*, 1984) is the positive-pole mirror of this literature, useful as contrastive background for what disobedience is a departure from.

**Emergence — what causes disobedience/insubordination to arise:**
Disobedience arises when the perceived legitimacy of the authority or standard is questioned, when the cost of compliance is appraised as too high, or when reactance against felt constraint overcomes the general tendency to comply. Apeitheia (a settled disposition of unpersuadability, from the root sense "unwilling to be persuaded") suggests something more entrenched than a single act of noncompliance — a standing orientation of resistance rather than an episodic lapse, distinguishing it from ordinary situational disobedience.

**Reduction and absence — what causes disobedience to diminish:**
Disobedience reduces when the perceived illegitimacy or excessive constraint driving reactance is resolved, when the authority is restored to legitimate standing in the person's own appraisal, or when a freely chosen (rather than coerced) compliance is reached. Moral disengagement is comparatively difficult to reverse once established, since each act of disengagement makes the next easier (a self-reinforcing pattern also relevant to Section 2's hardening material).

**Location — where disobedience is seated:**
Cognitively (in the appraisal of legitimacy and cost) and volitionally (in the settled choice to resist) — an orientation of the will rather than merely a behavioural event, consistent with the general pattern noted across this cluster's sections that hardening and lawlessness are dispositional rather than momentary.

**External influences:**
*Social:* Peer conformity pressure and the perceived legitimacy of the authority structure are primary situational drivers. *Developmental:* Patterns of authority-relationship formed in childhood — particularly whether rules were explained and internalised or imposed without explanation — shape adult disobedience/insubordination tendencies (the same developmental finding noted in the old M30 file). *Hereditary:* Low conscientiousness and low agreeableness (Big Five traits with moderate heritability) predict elevated noncompliance with social norms.

**Coverage assessment:** Good. Reactance theory and moral disengagement research are well-developed and directly applicable.

### Section 2 — Hardness of heart and cognitive-moral rigidity

**Primary field:** Clinical psychology / personality psychology

**Glosses covered:**
hardening (G4457 pōrōsis, H7188/H7280C qashah family), hardness (G4643 sklērotēs), to harden (G4456/G4645 pōroō/sklērunō)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
As documented in the old M30 file's Section 2: psychopathy research (Hare's Psychopathy Checklist) identifies callousness — reduced empathy and emotional responsiveness — as a core feature of antisocial personality, directly relevant to sklērokardia's ("hardness of heart") clinical parallel. Research on progressive moral hardening (Walker and colleagues, building on Bandura's moral disengagement framework) documents that repeated moral compromise makes each subsequent compromise easier — a slippery-slope desensitisation process rather than a single decisive turn. The alexithymia literature (reduced capacity to identify and process one's own and others' emotions) offers a partial clinical parallel to the biblical category's picture of reduced inner responsiveness, though — as the old M30 file notes — the full theological category of hardness of heart exceeds any single clinical construct.

**Emergence — what causes hardening to arise:**
Hardening arises through: (1) repeated moral disengagement and the progressive habituation of conscience, each compromise lowering resistance to the next; (2) defensive emotional hardening in response to perceived threat or repeated injury — an adaptive "armour" that becomes maladaptive when sustained beyond the threat that produced it; (3) temperamental rigidity amplified by experience; and (4), in the theological register this section's science cannot itself reach, divine judicial hardening as a response to persistent, prior rejection of truth (the Exodus/Romans 9 pattern).

**Reduction and absence — what causes hardening to diminish:**
Cognitive rigidity responds to challenge and new information when the person feels psychologically secure enough to risk revising a fixed position; genuine callousness is comparatively treatment-resistant in the clinical literature — among the more difficult personality features to shift through intervention. The most radical account of hardness's reversal available to the programme is theological rather than clinical (the promised replacement of a heart of stone with a heart of flesh, Ezek 36:26), which exceeds what science alone offers by way of remedy.

**Location — where hardness is seated:**
Primarily "the heart" in the programme's own framework sense — the inner being's central integrating faculty — meaning hardness is a hardening of the whole person's orientation (affect, perception, and will together) rather than a single faculty's dysfunction. Reduced amygdala responsiveness to others' distress is a measurable neural correlate of the callousness dimension specifically, though it captures only part of the full biblical category.

**External influences:**
*Developmental:* Childhood trauma can produce defensive emotional hardening as a survival adaptation; repeated moral compromise progressively hardens moral sensitivity across the lifespan. *Hereditary:* Callous-unemotional traits show significant heritability (approximately 50% in twin studies). *Circumstances:* Sustained adversity and betrayal can drive hardening as self-protection.

**Coverage assessment:** Moderate — the clinical literature on callousness and cognitive rigidity provides real coverage; the theological category of hardness of heart, and its judicial-divine register specifically, exceeds clinical scope.

### Section 3 — Lawlessness and moral defiance

**Primary field:** Moral psychology / developmental psychology

**Glosses covered:**
lawlessness (G3892 paranomia), lawless (G0113 anomos [adjective use], G0459 anomos)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Moral development research (Kohlberg, building on Piaget) addresses how moral reasoning develops in stages and how lawbreaking relates to a person's current stage of moral development. Antisocial behaviour research — particularly Patterson's coercive family-process model — traces developmental pathways from childhood oppositional behaviour through adolescent delinquency to adult antisocial conduct, tracing lawlessness to identifiable, largely relational developmental origins rather than treating it as an unexplained given. Moral disengagement (Bandura, again directly relevant here) addresses the cognitive mechanisms by which the hold of moral/legal norms over behaviour is progressively weakened.

**Emergence — what causes lawlessness to arise:**
Lawlessness arises from: moral development failure (insufficient formation of moral reasoning capacity); situational pressures that override existing moral commitments; prior moral disengagement that has already reduced the felt authority of moral norms; and outright ideological rejection of a law's legitimacy (distinct from mere situational noncompliance — a settled conviction that the law itself lacks binding authority).

**Reduction and absence — what causes lawlessness to diminish:**
Adequate moral formation, consistent and fair accountability structures, and community norms that maintain the perceived legitimacy of law all reduce lawless behaviour; removal of social accountability and impunity conditions reliably increase it (situational-influence research on how anonymity and reduced accountability predict rule violation).

**Location — where lawlessness is seated:**
Primarily cognitive (rejection of the law's authority or legitimacy) and volitional (the settled choice to act contrary to it) — the will as the primary location, a settled orientation of the inner being against the governing norm rather than an isolated behavioural lapse.

**External influences:**
*Developmental:* Coercive family processes, peer delinquency, and inadequate moral formation are among the strongest developmental predictors. *Social:* Community norms, peer influence, and institutional structures significantly shape moral compliance or its absence. *Circumstances:* Situations that remove social accountability and provide impunity increase lawless behaviour.

**Coverage assessment:** Moderate. Developmental and moral psychology provide good coverage for the behavioural dimensions; the theological register — lawlessness as active rebellion against the Creator's own moral order, and its eschatological personification (the "man of lawlessness") — is outside science.

## Outside-science inventory

- **disobedience to God in its covenantal register** — covenant rebellion against the Creator and Covenant Lord, whose commands express his own character and the creature's good, is a theological category exceeding generic authority-compliance psychology.
- **hardening by God (the Exodus hardening pattern, Rom 9)** — divine judicial hardening as a response to persistent, prior human rejection of truth is a theological category with no clinical or social-psychological analogue.
- **lawlessness (anomia) in its eschatological register** — the "man of lawlessness" and lawlessness as a cosmic, personified power opposing God's reign is a theological category science does not reach.

**Observation:** As in the old M30 file, the covenantal frame fundamentally reshapes this cluster: reactance theory and moral disengagement capture the mechanism of resistance well, but the biblical material treats disobedience to God as categorically different from ordinary authority noncompliance — not merely a psychologically explicable failure to comply, but covenant rebellion against the one to whom the creature owes its existence and good.

## Catalogue prompt reference notes (D9.1.1 / D9.2.1 / D11.2.1 / D12.1.1)

**D9.1.1 — Physiological/neural substrate:** Hardening/callousness has the clearest substrate among this cluster's three registers — measurable reduced amygdala reactivity to others' distress cues. Reactance-driven disobedience engages general threat/autonomy-appraisal circuitry (overlapping with M01's threat-detection substrate). Lawlessness as sustained moral orientation has no single discrete substrate beyond the general moral-cognition network (ventromedial prefrontal and temporoparietal regions implicated in moral judgement, per Greene and colleagues' moral-cognition research).

**D9.2.1 — Generational transmission:** Strong transmission pathway documented for both disobedience and lawlessness — coercive family process (Patterson) traces a specific, well-evidenced intergenerational chain from childhood oppositional behaviour through parental modelling to adult antisocial conduct. Callous-unemotional traits underlying hardness of heart show substantial heritability (~50%) alongside this social-transmission pathway.

**D11.2.1 — Innate endowment vs. acquired:** Predominantly acquired — moral reasoning capacity develops through childhood and adolescence, and hardening is explicitly a *process* (the biblical vocabulary's own causative verb forms, "to harden," name a change of state over time) rather than an innate starting condition, though temperamental rigidity and callous-unemotional traits provide a heritable substrate that makes the acquired hardening process more or less likely to take hold.

**D12.1.1 — Social/behavioural manifestation:** Highly behaviourally visible — disobedience and lawlessness are, almost by definition, manifest in observable noncompliant or transgressive action; hardness of heart is somewhat less directly visible but manifests behaviourally over time as a documented pattern of reduced responsiveness to others' distress and to moral appeal, observable to a community even before the person themselves may recognise the change.
