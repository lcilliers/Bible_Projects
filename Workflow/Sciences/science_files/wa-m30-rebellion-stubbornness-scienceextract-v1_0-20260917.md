## Science sections

### Section 1 — Rebellion and defiance of authority

**Primary field:** Developmental psychology / social psychology of defiance

**Glosses covered:**
rebellion (H4776, H4777, H4805H, G3894 apeitheia note distinct root, parapikrasmos), to rebel (H4775, H4784, G3893, G1881 antiteinō-family), rebellious (H4779, H4780), rebel/robber (G3027 lēstēs), robber sense noted separately from moral rebellion, disobedience (G3876/apeitheia — the wider parakoē/apeitheia family), to disobey (G0544 apeitheō)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Developmental psychology's research on defiance and oppositional behaviour (Patterson's coercion theory, *Coercive Family Process*, 1982) treats rebellion against authority as arising from a learned, escalating interaction pattern between the defiant party and the authority figure, rather than as a fixed trait — each party's response shapes and often entrenches the other's. Social psychology of authority (Milgram's obedience studies, 1963, and their later refinements — Burger's partial replication, 2009) established both how readily most people comply with illegitimate authority and the conditions (proximity of the victim, presence of a defiant peer, questioned legitimacy of the authority) under which resistance and rebellion instead occur. Tyler's procedural justice research (*Why People Obey the Law*, 1990) found that perceived legitimacy and fairness of an authority's process, more than the severity of possible sanction, is the strongest predictor of voluntary compliance — and, correspondingly, that perceived illegitimacy is a strong predictor of rebellion.

**Emergence — what causes rebellion to arise:**
Rebellion arises from the perception that an authority's demand is illegitimate, unjust, or in conflict with the person's own values or interests, combined with sufficient perceived capacity (individually or collectively) to resist. Patterson's coercion-theory research on family and institutional settings shows that rebellion is frequently produced or intensified by the authority's own response pattern — escalating, inconsistent, or humiliating exercises of control reliably increase defiance rather than reduce it.

**Reduction and absence — what causes rebellion to diminish:**
Rebellion is reduced where the authority is perceived as legitimate and its exercise as fair (Tyler's procedural justice findings are among the most robust in this literature), where genuine voice and consultation are offered to those subject to the authority, and where consistent, non-escalating responses to prior defiance are used rather than punitive spirals. Rebellion against authority perceived as illegitimate, by contrast, is not reduced by increased sanction alone — the coercion-theory literature finds that harsher punitive responses to defiance frequently increase rather than decrease it.

**Location — where rebellion is seated:**
Behavioural and relational at its most visible level (rebellion is an enacted, observable opposition to a specific authority), with cognitive appraisal (of the authority's legitimacy) and motivational commitment (to the alternative course) underlying it. It is inherently relational — rebellion exists only in relation to an authority being resisted.

**External influences:**
*Developmental:* Patterns of parental authority exercised in childhood (consistent and legitimate versus harsh, inconsistent, or arbitrary) shape the child's later disposition toward authority generally, according to coercion-theory research. *Social:* Peer modelling of defiance substantially increases individual willingness to rebel, and conversely a lone defiant peer substantially increases others' willingness to resist illegitimate authority (a robust finding from the Milgram-tradition literature). *Cultural:* Cultures and institutions vary in how legitimacy is established and communicated, shaping baseline rebellion rates independent of the substantive fairness of any given demand. *Circumstances:* Genuine injustice or harm caused by the authority is a primary and, per the literature, often justified driver.

**Coverage assessment:** Good. Developmental and social psychology of authority and defiance provide strong, well-developed coverage.

### Section 2 — Stubbornness, hardness, and resistance to change

**Primary field:** Personality psychology / cognitive psychology of belief rigidity

**Glosses covered:**
stubborn (H5620), stubbornness (H7190, H8307), hardness/hardening (via the broader "to harden" family cross-referenced from M01's callousness content — represented here by the "insensitive"/callous glosses), be insensitive (H2954), to become callous (as background trait, cross-cluster note)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Personality psychology treats stubbornness as a low-agreeableness, high-rigidity trait profile within the Big Five framework — associated specifically with resistance to social influence and difficulty revising a held position even given contrary evidence or social pressure. Cognitive psychology's research on belief perseverance (Ross, Lepper and Hubbard, 1975) demonstrated that once a belief is formed, it tends to persist even after the original evidence for it is discredited — a well-replicated finding relevant to understanding why stubbornness so often outlasts its original justification. Motivated reasoning research (Kunda, 1990) documents that people process disconfirming evidence more critically and confirming evidence more readily when the belief in question is identity-relevant — a mechanism that helps explain why stubbornness intensifies precisely where the issue at stake touches self-concept or group identity.

**Emergence — what causes stubbornness to arise:**
Stubbornness arises from a combination of trait rigidity (a dispositional resistance to revising position), motivated reasoning (where the position is identity-relevant, disconfirming evidence is discounted), and — situationally — from prior investment in the position (sunk-cost reasoning strengthens commitment to a course already taken, independent of its merits). Hardness in its more severe, settled form (as opposed to episodic stubbornness) suggests a trait-level rather than merely situational resistance.

**Reduction and absence — what causes stubbornness to diminish:**
Belief revision is more readily achieved when disconfirming information is delivered by a trusted in-group source rather than an opposing party (identity-protective cognition research — Kahan), when the person's identity is affirmed independently of the contested position (self-affirmation theory, Steele, 1988, which finds that people become more open to disconfirming evidence after affirming an unrelated valued aspect of self), and when the change is framed as consistent with rather than a betrayal of the person's prior values.

**Location — where stubbornness is seated:**
Primarily cognitive (the specific mechanism of belief perseverance and motivated reasoning) and dispositional/trait-based (a stable individual-difference tendency toward rigidity), with volitional dimension (stubbornness has a willed, resistant quality distinct from simple ignorance or inability to understand).

**External influences:**
*Hereditary:* Trait rigidity and low openness (the Big Five dimension most associated with resistance to new ideas) show documented heritability. *Developmental:* Early formation in environments that reward inflexibility, or that make position-change feel unsafe or humiliating, shapes stubbornness as an entrenched adult trait. *Social:* Group identity strongly determines which positions become stubbornly held — positions bound up with group belonging are markedly more resistant to revision than isolated factual beliefs.

**Coverage assessment:** Good. Belief-perseverance and motivated-reasoning research are well developed and directly relevant.

### Section 3 — Refusal, rejection, and despising

**Primary field:** Social psychology of rejection and contempt

**Glosses covered:**
to reject (G0114, G0593, G0683, G1848, H2186A, H3988A, H5541A), refuse (H0830H, H4651, H6569, H6574, H7419), to refuse (H3985), to despise (H0936, H0959, G2706, G3643, G4065, H7590), to spurn (H5006), to ignore (G3878), compliant (G2138 — noted as contrast term), to leave: forsake (H5800A), be willing (H0014 — contrast term)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Social psychology's research on rejection (Leary — sociometer theory, self-esteem as a gauge of relational value; Williams — ostracism research) treats the act of rejecting or despising another as functioning, at a social level, to lower the target's relative status and signal exclusion from the rejecting party's valued in-group. Contempt research (Ekman and Matsumoto's identification of contempt as a distinct basic emotion, expressed through a characteristic unilateral facial signal) treats contempt/despising as psychologically distinct from anger — anger orients toward engagement with the offending party, whereas contempt orients toward dismissal and withdrawal of regard, treating the other as beneath consideration rather than as a worthy opponent. Gottman's marital research identifies contempt specifically (not anger) as the single strongest predictor of relationship dissolution among the negative communication patterns he catalogues ("the Four Horsemen").

**Emergence — what causes refusal, rejection, and despising to arise:**
Refusal of a specific request or command arises from a cost-benefit appraisal (the demand is judged not worth complying with) or from active opposition to the one making it. Despising/contempt arises from a global negative evaluation of the other's worth or standing — a judgment not merely that a specific act or request is wrong, but that the person or thing itself does not merit regard. Forsaking (as distinct from active despising) names withdrawal of committed presence or provision, which can arise from despising but can also arise from simple failure of commitment without active contempt.

**Reduction and absence — what causes these to diminish:**
Contempt, once established in a relationship, is unusually resistant to reduction (Gottman's finding that it is the strongest single predictor of relational breakdown reflects this durability) — it typically requires deliberate, sustained repair work (rebuilding of positive regard through repeated positive interaction) rather than resolving through simple cessation of conflict. Refusal of a specific demand is more readily reversed than global despising, since it need not imply a global negative judgment of the one making the demand.

**Location — where refusal and despising are seated:**
Refusal is primarily volitional and behavioural — a specific act of non-compliance. Despising/contempt is emotional-cognitive and relational — a global evaluative stance toward another, with a characteristic and cross-culturally recognised facial and behavioural signature. Forsaking is behavioural and relational, describing the withdrawal of presence or commitment rather than an internal evaluative state per se.

**External influences:**
*Relational:* The prior history of the relationship strongly shapes whether a specific refusal escalates into settled contempt — repeated unaddressed grievance is the most consistent precursor to contempt in Gottman's longitudinal research. *Developmental:* Modelling of contemptuous or dismissive regard for others in childhood shapes adult disposition toward contempt as a default response to conflict. *Social:* Group and status hierarchies shape who is more readily despised — contempt is disproportionately directed at those perceived as lower status or as having violated valued norms.

**Coverage assessment:** Good, particularly for contempt/despising, which is one of the more precisely characterised negative social-emotional states in the literature.

## Outside-science inventory

- **Rebellion against God (as against a human authority)** — the specific theological weight of rebellion directed at God as covenant Lord, and its consequence within the covenant relationship, exceeds social psychology's account of rebellion against human authority, which addresses legitimacy and power dynamics but not covenant fidelity.
- **Hardness of heart as divinely permitted or hardened (e.g. Pharaoh, Romans 9)** — the theological question of whether and how God may harden a heart already inclined to stubbornness is a category outside psychology's descriptive scope, which treats belief rigidity as a wholly human-level cognitive phenomenon.
- **Forsaking the covenant** — the specific theological content of Israel's or an individual's forsaking of covenant relationship with God, and its consequences, is a theological category that social psychology's more general account of relational abandonment does not reach.
- **Despising God's word or God himself** — contempt directed at the divine as an object exceeds the human-interpersonal scope of contempt research, which studies contempt between persons of comparable standing.

**Observation:** M30's outside-science content clusters at the vertical dimension — where the cluster's ordinarily interpersonal or intrapersonal phenomena (rebellion, stubbornness, despising, forsaking) are directed at God rather than at a human authority or party. The horizontal, human-level mechanics of defiance, belief rigidity, and contempt are well covered by the literature cited above.

## D9.1.1 / D9.2.1 / D11.2.1 / D12.1.1 prompt reference notes

**D9.1.1 — Physiological/neural substrate:** Moderate. Contempt has a distinct, cross-culturally recognised physiological/expressive signature (the unilateral lip-corner tightening Ekman identified) distinguishing it from anger and disgust. Belief perseverance and motivated reasoning have documented neural correlates (reduced engagement of regions associated with open evaluation of disconfirming evidence when identity-relevant beliefs are challenged), though this is a less mature literature than, for example, fear or reward substrate research.

**D9.2.1 — Generational transmission:** Substantial for the authority-response pattern specifically — Patterson's coercion-theory research documents that parenting style (harsh/inconsistent versus legitimate/consistent authority) transmits a child's disposition toward compliance or defiance into adulthood and, further, across generations of parenting style itself. Trait rigidity (low openness) shows moderate heritability. Contempt as a communication pattern is also substantially modelled and transmitted within families.

**D11.2.1 — Innate endowment vs. acquired:** Mixed. Contempt appears to be a basic, likely innate emotional category with a universal expressive signature (per Ekman's cross-cultural research). Trait rigidity/stubbornness has a documented heritable-temperament component. But the specific objects and occasions of rebellion, stubbornness, and despising — what authority is resisted, what belief is stubbornly held, whom one despises — are overwhelmingly acquired through developmental and social experience rather than innate.

**D12.1.1 — Social/behavioural manifestation:** High across the whole cluster. Rebellion, refusal, and forsaking are by definition enacted, observable behaviours directed at another party. Stubbornness and despising have a strong internal cognitive-evaluative component but are also reliably behaviourally and expressively manifest (visible resistance to persuasion; the characteristic contempt expression). None of this cluster's content is a purely private state without behavioural correlate.
