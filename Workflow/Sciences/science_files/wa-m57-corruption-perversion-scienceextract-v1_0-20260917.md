## Science sections

### Section 1 — Infidelity and adultery

**Primary field:** Relationship psychology — infidelity and betrayal research

**Glosses covered:**
adulterous (moichalis), adultery (moicheia), to commit adultery (moichaō), to commit adultery (moicheuō), adultery (ni.uph), adultery (na.a.phuph), to commit adultery (na.aph)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Infidelity research (Glass and Wright, *Journal of Sex Research*, 1992; Atkins, Baucom, and Jacobson, 2001) documents the prevalence, precipitants, and relational aftermath of extramarital involvement, finding that infidelity is predicted less by relationship dissatisfaction alone than by opportunity, situational proximity to an attractive alternative, and — in a substantial minority of cases — pre-existing personality factors (low conscientiousness, high novelty-seeking). Attachment-injury research (Johnson, *Emotionally Focused Therapy*, and colleagues) treats discovered infidelity as a distinct category of relational trauma — a betrayal that damages the fundamental sense of safety in the relationship in ways that differ qualitatively from ordinary relational conflict, often producing symptoms resembling post-traumatic stress in the betrayed partner. Buss's evolutionary psychology of jealousy (*The Dangerous Passion*, 2000) documents cross-cultural consistency in the intensity of the response to sexual infidelity specifically, tied to the evolved stakes of paternity and pair-bond security.

**Emergence — what causes adultery/infidelity to arise:**
Documented precipitants include relational dissatisfaction or emotional disconnection in the primary relationship, situational opportunity and proximity to an alternative partner, personality factors (impulsivity, low conscientiousness, high sensation-seeking), and — in a subset of cases — a deliberate, values-driven choice unrelated to relationship quality. The biblical material's own diagnosis, that adultery flows from disordered desire (see M28/desire-cluster background) rather than from relationship circumstance alone, is broadly consistent with the personality-factor strand of the research even though the theological weight it places on covenant-breaking exceeds anything the secular literature addresses.

**Reduction and absence — what prevents infidelity or repairs its aftermath:**
Prevention research identifies relationship investment, deliberate avoidance of high-risk proximity situations, and strong internalised commitment norms (see M13/M62's faithfulness material) as protective factors. Repair after discovered infidelity is documented as possible but difficult, requiring full disclosure, sustained demonstrated trustworthiness over time, and — in the emotionally-focused therapy literature — the reconstruction of attachment security rather than mere behavioural cessation.

**Location — where infidelity is seated:**
Behavioural at the point of the act itself, but with volitional (a choice against covenant commitment), motivational (unmet needs or disordered desire seeking satisfaction outside the bond), and relational (a wound inflicted on the betrayed partner and on the covenant bond itself) dimensions. The betrayed partner's trauma response is a distinct and well-documented location of this cluster's effects, separate from the unfaithful party's own inner state.

**External influences:**
*Circumstances:* Opportunity and proximity to an alternative partner are among the strongest situational predictors. *Cultural:* Norms around monogamy, and the social and legal consequences attached to infidelity, vary substantially across cultures and eras, shaping both incidence and the severity of the relational rupture it produces. *Developmental:* Attachment insecurity and modelling of infidelity in family of origin are documented risk factors.

**Coverage assessment:** Good. Infidelity, betrayal-trauma, and evolutionary jealousy research provide well-developed, directly applicable frameworks.

### Section 2 — Moral corruption, perversion, and cognitive twisting

**Primary field:** Moral psychology / organisational behaviour — corruption research

**Glosses covered:**
corruption (fthora), to pervert (diastrefō), corruption (ma.she.chat), perversion (mut.teh), perversion (te.vel), to corrupt (a.lach), to corrupt (she.chat), to pervert (sa.laph), to pervert (a.vah), to pervert (a.vat), to twist (la.phat), to twist (a.vah), to twist (a.qal), to twist (a.qash), to twist (pa.tal), to twist (sha.zar), to twist (sa.rakh), to twist: anticipate (chul), to twist: dance (chul)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
The moral-corruption literature (Ashforth and Anand's "normalization of corruption" model, *Research in Organizational Behavior*, 2003) documents how individuals and organisations progressively come to accept and routinise unethical conduct through three interlocking processes: institutionalisation (corrupt practice becomes embedded in structures and routines), rationalisation (self-justifying narratives that neutralise moral discomfort), and socialisation (newcomers are inducted into the practice as normal). Bandura's moral disengagement mechanisms (*Moral Disengagement*, 2016) — euphemistic labelling, displacement of responsibility, distortion of consequences, dehumanisation of those harmed — provide the cognitive-psychological substrate for how a person comes to see a perverted or corrupted course of action as acceptable. Cognitive-distortion research in clinical psychology (Beck's cognitive therapy; Yochelson and Samenow's criminal-thinking-errors research) documents characteristic patterns of "twisted" reasoning — minimisation, blame externalisation, entitlement thinking — that closely parallel the biblical root metaphor (twisting, bending what should be straight) running through this section's Hebrew vocabulary.

**Emergence — what causes corruption and perversion to arise:**
Corruption typically arises incrementally rather than in a single decisive act — the normalisation-of-corruption model's central finding is that most corrupt practice begins with small, self-justifiable departures that progressively widen as rationalisation and institutionalisation take hold, closely matching Ariely's incremental-dishonesty findings discussed in the M62/M13 truth material. Perversion of judgment (mut.teh — a bending aside) can arise through corrupt incentive (bribery, self-interest distorting judgment) or through inherited/socialised distortion in reasoning that the person may not recognise as distorted from the inside.

**Reduction and absence — what causes corruption to be reversed or resisted:**
Organisational-corruption research identifies external accountability structures, whistleblowing protections, and the deliberate interruption of rationalisation narratives (naming the self-justification explicitly) as the most effective counters. Individual moral-disengagement is reduced by restored empathic engagement with those harmed and by communities that consistently refuse the euphemistic reframing that sustains corrupt practice.

**Location — where corruption/perversion is seated:**
Cognitive (the twisted or bent reasoning itself) and characterological (a settled orientation once corruption is habituated), with clear behavioural and institutional expression. The "twisting" metaphor that dominates this section's Hebrew vocabulary maps closely onto cognitive-distortion research's own account of corrupted reasoning as a bending or distortion of a straight standard rather than simple ignorance of it.

**External influences:**
*Social/organisational:* Institutional structures and peer norms are the primary documented drivers of the normalisation-of-corruption pathway — corruption spreads through systems, not merely through individually corrupt persons. *Developmental:* Early exposure to modelled corrupt or dishonest reasoning shapes later susceptibility. *Circumstances:* Power without accountability is among the most consistently documented situational enablers of corruption across the organisational-behaviour literature (Kipnis's "metamorphic effects of power" research).

**Coverage assessment:** Good. Organisational-corruption and moral-disengagement research, together with cognitive-distortion research, provide strong and directly applicable coverage for this section's dominant "twisting" metaphor.

### Section 3 — Unfaithfulness and covenant betrayal

**Primary field:** Relationship and organisational-trust psychology

**Glosses covered:**
apostasy (apostasia), be unfaithful (ma.al), unfaithfulness (ma.al), degenerate (sur), lewdness (nav.lut)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Trust-violation research (the counterpart to the trust-building literature discussed under M13/M62) documents that betrayal of an explicit commitment or covenant produces a distinct and more severe relational rupture than ordinary disappointment, because it specifically violates the trustor's vulnerability-based reliance on the trustee's demonstrated fidelity (Mayer, Davis, and Schoorman's integrity component of trustworthiness). Apostasy specifically — the abandonment of a formerly held religious commitment — is addressed by the psychology of religious deconversion (Streib and colleagues, *Deconversion*, 2009), which documents typical pathways (gradual disaffiliation, crisis-triggered break, and socially-driven exit) though largely as a neutral descriptive phenomenon rather than adjudicating its moral or theological status.

**Emergence — what causes unfaithfulness/apostasy to arise:**
Ma.al (treacherous unfaithfulness, often used of covenant breach) implies a deliberate, knowing violation rather than mere drift — closer to betrayal-trauma's category of intentional trust violation than to gradual disaffiliation. Apostasy in the deconversion literature more often arises gradually, through accumulating disconfirming experience, social disembedding from the community of faith, or crisis events that the person's prior framework fails to make sense of, though sudden, decisive breaks are also documented.

**Reduction and absence:** Trust-violation research indicates that repair, where it occurs at all, requires sustained demonstrated fidelity over an extended period and is often incomplete — the violated party's baseline trust rarely returns fully to its pre-violation level even after apparent reconciliation.

**Location:** Relational and volitional — located in the breach of an existing bond of trust or commitment, with both a behavioural dimension (the act of unfaithfulness) and a characterological dimension where betrayal becomes a settled pattern (degenerate — sur, "turned aside/degenerate").

**External influences:** *Social:* Communities of accountability reduce the incidence of unfaithfulness; their absence or collapse increases it. *Circumstances:* Crisis, disconfirming experience, and social disembedding are documented drivers specifically of religious apostasy.

**Coverage assessment:** Moderate. Trust-violation and deconversion research provide genuine but partial coverage; the specifically covenantal weight of unfaithfulness to God (rather than to a human trust-partner) is addressed only at the experiential edge.

## Outside-science inventory

- **adultery as covenant metaphor for Israel's unfaithfulness to Yahweh** — where the prophetic literature uses adultery (ni.uph, na.aph) as a sustained metaphor for Israel's idolatrous unfaithfulness to the covenant, the theological weight of the metaphor (Yahweh as faithful husband, Israel as unfaithful wife) exceeds what infidelity research, which addresses actual marital betrayal, can reach.
- **apostasy (apostasia) in its eschatological register** — the great apostasy/falling-away associated with the end of the age (2 Thessalonians 2:3) is a theological-eschatological category, not merely an instance of the general deconversion phenomenon documented in psychology of religion.
- **unfaithfulness (ma.al) in its cultic-sacrilege register** — ma.al used of sacrilege against holy things (rather than interpersonal betrayal) is a covenantal-cultic category outside relationship-trust psychology's scope.

**Observation:** M57 has good-to-strong science coverage across all three sections — infidelity, moral corruption, and unfaithfulness are all well-studied human phenomena. Its most theologically distinctive feature — the systematic use of marital/sexual unfaithfulness as a governing metaphor for Israel's covenant unfaithfulness to God — is a literary-theological move that the underlying psychological literature on infidelity illuminates (by describing what the metaphor's vehicle, actual betrayal, feels like) without being able to address the metaphor's theological tenor.

## D9.1.1 / D9.2.1 / D11.2.1 / D12.1.1 prompt reference notes

**D9.1.1 — Physiological/neural substrate:** Discovered infidelity in the betrayed partner produces a documented trauma-physiology response (hyperarousal, intrusive imagery) closely paralleling other attachment-injury trauma. Corrupted/twisted moral reasoning (Section 2) engages the same prefrontal moral-cognition and self-justification circuitry implicated in motivated reasoning generally, rather than a distinct substrate of its own.

**D9.2.1 — Generational transmission:** Infidelity and unfaithfulness show documented (though moderate) intergenerational transmission via modelling — children who witness parental infidelity show somewhat elevated rates of infidelity in their own adult relationships, mediated by attachment style and normative beliefs about fidelity rather than by a direct heritable mechanism. Corrupt reasoning patterns (Section 2) transmit chiefly through social and organisational modelling (the normalisation-of-corruption pathway) rather than family-generational transmission specifically.

**D11.2.1 — Innate endowment vs. acquired:** None of this cluster's core phenomena are innate; personality factors that elevate risk (impulsivity, low conscientiousness, sensation-seeking — relevant to Section 1) do have a heritable component, but infidelity, corruption, and unfaithfulness themselves are acquired patterns of choice and habituation rather than innate drives, consistent with the biblical framing of these as culpable departures from a standard rather than as fixed dispositions.

**D12.1.1 — Social/behavioural manifestation:** Infidelity manifests as a discrete, often concealed behavioural act with severe documented relational aftermath in the betrayed party. Corruption manifests as progressively normalised departure from an original standard, typically accompanied by self-justifying rationalisation and institutional embedding. Unfaithfulness/apostasy manifests along a spectrum from gradual disaffiliation to decisive, knowing breach — the behavioural profile Session B may find useful for distinguishing which register a given occurrence of ma.al is drawing on.
