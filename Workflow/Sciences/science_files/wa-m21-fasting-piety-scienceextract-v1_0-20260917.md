## Purpose

This document is the cluster science review for M21 (live definition: "Fasting, piety, intercession, asking, begging"). It is produced as a pre-analytical asset — before the cluster's Session B analysis begins — and is the standing reference for the D9.1.1 / D9.2.1 / D11.2.1 / D12.1.1 catalogue prompts. It does not constitute the biblical analysis; it holds the scientific lens alongside which the biblical pass will be read.

The cluster's live gloss set (queried directly from `cluster_strong`, 20 active Strong's entries) is narrower and more concentrated than the vocabulary once carried by the 2026-05-13 M21 file. It centres on three coherent phenomena: fasting as embodied self-restraint, piety/devotion as a settled disposition, and asking/begging as dependent appeal — with intercession sitting at the boundary of the second and third.

## Science sections

### Section 1 — Fasting and voluntary bodily restraint

**Primary field:** Health psychology / physiology of caloric restriction

**Glosses covered:**
fasting (G0776 nēsteia), fasting (G0777 nēsteia — variant), fasting (G3521 nēsteuō), fasting (G3523 nēstis), fasting (H2908 tsom), fasting (H8589 ta.a.nit)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
The physiology of fasting is well established: short-term fasting shifts metabolism from glucose to ketone-body use within roughly 12–24 hours, and produces measurable neuroendocrine changes (elevated cortisol, altered ghrelin/leptin signalling, increased norepinephrine) that affect both mood and cognition. Mattson's research on intermittent fasting and neuronal stress resistance (*Cell Metabolism*, various, 2010s onward) documents adaptive cellular responses to periodic caloric restriction. Separately, the psychology of voluntary self-denial (self-control research broadly — Baumeister and Tice) treats fasting as a paradigm case of sustained, deliberate resistance to a basic drive, with documented effects on subsequent self-regulatory capacity in other domains (the evidence here is mixed and debated, parallel to the wider ego-depletion replication concerns). Anthropological and ritual-studies literature (Douglas, *Purity and Danger*, 1966, on bodily practice as symbolic communication) treats fasting cross-culturally as a body-based technique for marking a period as set apart.

**Emergence — what causes fasting to arise:**
Fasting is undertaken (rather than merely experienced as hunger) when a person deliberately withholds food for a purpose beyond nutrition — supplication in crisis, mourning, preparation for a significant undertaking, or the marking of a penitential or petitionary season. The self-denial is intentional; this distinguishes it sharply from involuntary hunger or deprivation (M24). Psychologically, voluntary fasting functions as a costly signal — to oneself, to others, and in the cluster's own frame, to God — that the matter at hand outweighs an ordinary bodily need.

**Reduction and absence — what causes fasting to diminish or cease:**
Fasting practice diminishes through the loss of the communal or liturgical structures that sustain it, through the substitution of merely symbolic or partial abstinence for genuine restraint, and — clinically — through the recognition that sustained fasting carries real physiological risk if practised without limit or medical caution. Historically and cross-culturally, fasting persists longest where it is embedded in a shared calendar (a fixed season or day) rather than left to spontaneous individual initiative.

**Location — where fasting is seated:**
Explicitly physical in its mechanism (the digestive, metabolic and endocrine systems) but relational and volitional in its meaning. The body is the instrument; the disposition being expressed — dependence, mourning, urgency, humility before God — is not itself physiological. This is a case where the cluster's own logic (bodily practice as the vehicle for an inner-being state) maps closely onto the general anthropological account of embodied ritual.

**External influences:**
*Circumstances:* Crisis, grief, and high-stakes petition are the most consistent triggers for occasional fasting across religious traditions. *Cultural and communal:* Fasting is overwhelmingly a practice transmitted and sustained by community and calendar rather than invented anew by individuals. *Developmental:* Formation in a fasting tradition in childhood or early adult religious formation is the strongest predictor of continued adult practice. *Hereditary:* Not a heritable trait as such, though individual variation in metabolic tolerance for fasting (blood sugar regulation, for example) has a genetic component that shapes how fasting is physically experienced.

**Coverage assessment:** Good for the physiological mechanism, thinner for the specific meaning-content (penitence, urgency, dependence before God) that the cluster's terms carry.

### Section 2 — Piety, reverent devotion, and God-orientation

**Primary field:** Psychology of religion

**Glosses covered:**
piety (G2150 eusebeia), to show piety (G2151 eusebeō), God-loving (G5377 filotheos)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Pargament's psychology of religious coping (*The Psychology of Religion and Coping*, 1997) treats piety-as-disposition — a stable, habitual orientation of reverent devotion, rather than an episodic religious feeling — as one of the more durable individual-difference variables in psychology of religion research, correlated with (though not reducible to) intrinsic religious orientation (Allport and Ross's intrinsic/extrinsic distinction, 1967). Intrinsically religious individuals hold religious commitment as a superordinate, motivating value rather than a means to social or personal ends; the cluster's eusebeō vocabulary (habitual reverent devotion) maps closely onto this construct. Emmons' work on spiritual strivings (*The Psychology of Ultimate Concerns*, 1999) treats devotion to God as a distinct category of personal goal that organises and integrates other life goals.

**Emergence — what causes piety to arise:**
Piety as a settled disposition (rather than a single act of devotion) arises through sustained practice, community formation, and — in the psychology of religion literature — through an internalised valuation of the relationship with God as intrinsically rather than instrumentally significant. It typically develops gradually, through repeated engagement with religious practice, rather than appearing as a discrete event.

**Reduction and absence — what causes piety to diminish:**
Piety diminishes when religious practice becomes extrinsically motivated (practised for social approval, status, or other secondary gain rather than as an end in itself — Allport and Ross's extrinsic orientation, which correlates with less durable religious commitment under pressure). It also diminishes through the absence of a sustaining community and through unaddressed cognitive dissonance between professed devotion and lived practice.

**Location — where piety is seated:**
Primarily motivational and relational — a stable orientation of the whole person toward God as the object of ultimate concern, rather than a discrete emotional or cognitive state. It has behavioural expression (consistent practice) and affective accompaniment (reverence, love) but its psychological core is dispositional.

**External influences:**
*Developmental:* Family and community modelling of devout practice in childhood is the strongest known predictor of adult piety. *Cultural:* Religious communities with high behavioural expectations and dense practice (daily prayer, regular fasting, structured devotion) produce and sustain higher measured piety than low-demand communities. *Circumstances:* Piety, like prayer, is often intensified under adversity, though the durable trait form is less circumstance-dependent than episodic religious feeling.

**Coverage assessment:** Moderate. Psychology of religion addresses the disposition and its correlates; it does not adjudicate whether the God toward whom piety is oriented actually exists or is worthy of it.

### Section 3 — Asking, begging, and dependent appeal

**Primary field:** Social psychology — help-seeking and appeal behaviour

**Glosses covered:**
to ask (G0154 aiteō), to ask (G2065 erōtaō), to ask (H1156 be.a), to ask (H7592 sha.al), to ask (H7593 she.e.lah), to beg (G4319 prosaiteō), to beg (H2470B cha.lah), intercession (G1783 enteuxis), to intercede (G5241 huperentunchanō)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Help-seeking research (Nadler, DePaulo — the social psychology of asking for help) identifies asking as psychologically costly precisely because it exposes dependence and vulnerability; the reluctance to ask for help even when it would plainly benefit the asker (the "help-seeking paradox") is a well-replicated finding. Flynn and Lake's research on underestimating others' willingness to help (2008) shows that people systematically overestimate the social cost of asking. Begging specifically — appeal from a position of acknowledged powerlessness or need — is addressed in the sociology and social psychology of poverty and status: it requires the surrender of self-sufficiency as a precondition of the appeal itself. Intercession — asking on another's behalf — draws on advocacy and third-party-appeal research, which finds that people are frequently more willing to ask assertively for others than for themselves (self-advocacy is harder than other-advocacy in the experimental literature — Amanatullah and Morris, 2010, on gender and self- versus other-advocacy).

**Emergence — what causes asking and appeal to arise:**
Asking arises from a perceived gap between present resources and present need, combined with the belief (however tentative) that the party being asked has the capacity and possible willingness to meet it. Begging specifically arises when the asker has no comparable resource to offer in exchange — an appeal made from acknowledged dependence rather than negotiated exchange. Intercession arises when the asker's own standing, relationship, or perceived favour is deployed on behalf of a third party who lacks it.

**Reduction and absence — what causes asking to diminish:**
Asking diminishes under perceived self-sufficiency, under shame about the need being disclosed, under past experience of appeals being refused or mocked, and under social norms that penalise dependence. It is increased by secure relationship with the one being asked (the appeal is safer where rejection or humiliation is not expected) and by precedent (prior positive responses to asking increase future asking).

**Location — where asking is seated:**
Relational and motivational — asking exists only in address to another and cannot be described purely intrapsychically. It carries an affective cost (vulnerability, potential shame) and a cognitive appraisal component (assessment of the other's capacity and willingness). Begging in particular is also socially located — it is shaped by the asker's perceived status relative to the one asked.

**External influences:**
*Social and relational:* The quality and security of the relationship with the party asked is the single strongest predictor of whether and how a person asks. *Cultural:* Cultures and communities vary substantially in the social cost attached to openly asking or begging — honour-shame cultures (including much of the ancient Near Eastern setting of this material) typically attach higher cost to unreciprocated dependence than individualist Western contexts assume. *Developmental:* Early experience of appeals being met or refused shapes a durable expectation about whether asking is safe.

**Coverage assessment:** Moderate. Help-seeking and advocacy research provide genuine coverage of the mechanism of asking; the theological content of asking directed at God specifically is addressed only at its experiential edge.

## Outside-science inventory

- **Fasting in its covenantal-penitential register** — fasting as response to God's call, or as an act performed before God rather than merely a self-regarding discipline, exceeds the physiology and psychology of caloric restriction; the theological claim that God attends to and responds to fasting is not a scientific category.
- **Intercession (enteuxis, huperentunchanō) as efficacious before God** — that a third party's appeal genuinely moves God's action on another's behalf is a theological claim about divine responsiveness, not addressed by advocacy psychology, which only describes the human mechanics of asking on another's behalf.
- **Piety (eusebeia) in its normative register** — that reverent devotion to God is the fitting, right response to who God is (rather than merely a stable psychological disposition with measurable correlates) is a theological claim.
- **God-loving (filotheos)** in its ontological sense — the claim that God is a rightful object of love — is outside science's descriptive reach; positive psychology addresses love and attachment as human phenomena, not the worthiness of their object.

**Observation:** M21's outside-science content is concentrated at exactly the point where the cluster's vocabulary turns from describing the human act (asking, restraining the body, holding a devoted disposition) to asserting that God is the real addressee and respondent. The human mechanics of fasting, piety, and appeal are reasonably well covered; the claim that these acts reach and move God is not.

## D9.1.1 / D9.2.1 / D11.2.1 / D12.1.1 prompt reference notes

These notes are prepared to assist the analyst when the D9.1.1, D9.2.1, D11.2.1, and D12.1.1 catalogue prompts are reached. They are provisional — the biblical pass may surface findings that modify or extend them. (Note: these four questions replace the retired T7.3 prompt used in the 2026-05-13 batch of cluster science reviews.)

**D9.1.1 — Physiological/neural substrate:** Fasting has a well-characterised physiological substrate (metabolic shift to ketosis, HPA-axis and neuroendocrine changes, altered ghrelin/leptin signalling) — the cluster's fasting vocabulary sits on genuine, measurable bodily process. Piety and asking have much thinner neural substrates directly studied; what exists is mostly correlational (intrinsic religiosity and prefrontal/limbic activity in devotional states) rather than mechanistic.

**D9.2.1 — Generational transmission:** Fasting and piety are both overwhelmingly transmitted through family and community formation rather than independently discovered — this is one of the more consistent findings across the cluster's three sections. There is no meaningful genetic-heritability literature for fasting or piety as such (unlike, say, trait anxiety); transmission here is cultural and practice-based, not biological.

**D11.2.1 — Innate endowment vs. acquired:** The capacity to ask for help appears to have some innate basis (infant appeal behaviour, attachment-seeking, is present from birth), but begging, fasting, and piety as specific, elaborated practices are acquired — learned within a community and tradition, not built in. The cluster is therefore weighted toward the acquired end, with only the underlying appeal/dependence motivation itself plausibly innate.

**D12.1.1 — Social/behavioural manifestation:** All three of the cluster's phenomena are inherently social/behavioural in expression — fasting is observable bodily restraint, piety is expressed through consistent visible practice, and asking/begging is by definition an interpersonal act. None of the cluster's content is a purely private, unexpressed inner state; the science literature accordingly treats all three primarily through behavioural and social-psychological method rather than purely intrapsychic measurement.
