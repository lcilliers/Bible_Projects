## Science sections

### Section 1 — Purity, cleanness, and blamelessness

**Primary field:** Moral psychology — purity and moral-foundations research

**Glosses covered:**
be pure (ta.her), clean (katharos, H2643, H2135, H2141, H2889), cleansing (katharismos, H4935), clearness (to.har), pure (hagnos, adolos, eilikrineia-family, ta.hor, H1249, H2134, H6337, G0053, G0097, G0283, G4101), pureness (te.hor, G2514), purification (hagnismos), purifying (to.ho.rah), purity (hagneia, hagnotēs), to clean (katharizō, G2511), to purify (hagnizō), innocent (akakos, anaitios, na.qi, G0172, G0338, G0121, G0185, H5355A, H5355B), innocence (H2136, H5356A), to clear (na.qah), unblemished (ta.mim, H8549H), soundness (me.tom), sinless (anamartētos), uncondemned (akatakritos), blameless (G0273, G0298, G0299)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Haidt's moral foundations theory identifies purity/sanctity as one of the primary, cross-culturally recurring foundations of moral judgment, operating in part independently of harm-based reasoning (see also M10c's review, which addresses the negative pole of this same territory — defilement and disgust). Zhong and Liljenquist's "Macbeth effect" research (*Science*, 2006) demonstrated an experimentally robust link between moral transgression and a literal desire for physical cleansing — participants recalling an unethical act showed increased preference for cleaning products, evidence that the moral-purity metaphor is not merely linguistic but psychologically operative. Innocence and blamelessness research in forensic and social psychology addresses the specific cognitive and social processes by which a person is judged free of wrongdoing — distinct from the purity register in being explicitly relational and evaluative (judged by others) rather than an intrinsic state.

**Emergence — what causes purity, cleanness, and blamelessness to arise:**
Purity in its moral register arises from the absence of transgression, or from a deliberate act of cleansing that removes the taint of a prior transgression (ritually, psychologically, or both — the Macbeth effect research suggests these are not cleanly separable even in ordinary modern psychology). Innocence and blamelessness specifically arise from a state of not having committed the wrong in question, established either by fact or by a legitimate process of judgment that clears the person.

**Reduction and absence — what causes purity to diminish or blamelessness to be lost:**
Purity and blamelessness are lost through actual transgression (a straightforward loss), but also, in moral-psychology research, through mere association with wrongdoing or wrongdoers — moral contamination by proximity is a documented phenomenon distinct from personal guilt (Rozin's "negativity bias" in contamination judgments — a small amount of "badness" spoils the perceived purity of the whole far more readily than an equivalent amount of "goodness" purifies it).

**Location — where purity and blamelessness are seated:**
Purity has the same dual somatic/moral location documented in M10c's review — disgust-rooted, extending into moral evaluation. Blamelessness and innocence are more purely social-cognitive and relational: they exist as much in the judgment of an evaluating community or court as in any intrinsic property of the person themselves.

**External influences:**
*Cultural:* Purity norms and their specific content are heavily culturally shaped, though the underlying psychological mechanism (disgust extended to moral evaluation, and the asymmetric "spoiling" effect of contamination) appears cross-culturally consistent. *Social:* Public reputation and community judgment substantially determine whether a person is treated as blameless, independent of private moral fact. *Developmental:* Purity-based moral reasoning is documented developmentally in children from an early age, often preceding fully reasoned harm-based moral judgment.

**Coverage assessment:** Good. Moral-foundations and contamination-psychology research directly address this section's vocabulary.

### Section 2 — Consecration and holy designation

**Primary field:** Psychology of religion / sociology of the sacred

**Glosses covered:**
to consecrate (qa.dash — five distinct senses: consecrate, dedicate, forfeit, holiness, prepare), holy: saint (G0040H, H6918H), sacred (hosios), unholy (anosios), to form (morfoō, tsur)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Pargament's sanctification research (*The Psychology of Religion and Coping*, 1997) documents the psychological process by which an object, person, or activity comes to be perceived as possessing sacred significance — sanctified objects and relationships are treated with markedly greater protective seriousness than equivalent unsanctified ones. Durkheim's sacred/profane framework (see also M10c) underlies the sociological account of what consecration accomplishes: the formal, socially recognised transfer of something from ordinary to set-apart status.

**Emergence — what causes consecration and holy designation to arise:**
Consecration arises through a formal, typically ritual act that a community recognises as transferring an object, place, or person from ordinary to set-apart status. Unlike the more continuous, gradual process of purity (Section 1), consecration in the psychology-of-religion literature is characteristically a discrete, marked event — a threshold crossing rather than an accumulation.

**Reduction and absence — what causes consecrated status to be violated (unholiness):**
Consecrated status is treated as violated when the set-apart object, person, or place is used, touched, or treated as though it were ordinary — see M10c's Section 2 on profanation for the fuller treatment of this negative pole.

**Location — where consecration is seated:**
Primarily social and formally-constituted rather than purely internal — consecration requires community recognition of the changed status, though the person or object so designated may also undergo (or be understood to undergo) genuine internal change.

**External influences:**
*Religious and cultural:* The mechanisms, occasions, and meaning of consecration are entirely dependent on a community's religious framework.

**Coverage assessment:** Thin. Overlaps substantially with M10c's Section 2 (profanation is this section's negative pole); the psychology-of-religion literature on sanctification is the main direct resource.

### Section 3 — Righteousness and justice

**Primary field:** Moral psychology / justice research

**Glosses covered:**
justice (G1341, H4941G, H4941H, H4941I, H4941J), just (G1342, G1738), righteousness (G1343, G2118, H6664G, H6665, H6666), righteous act (G1345), righteous (H6662), Righteousness [God] (H6664H), unjust (G0094, H5766A, H5766B, H5767), unjust-gain (H1215), unjust one (H5760), to act unjustly (H5765), Hall [of Justice] (H0197H)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Justice research spans distributive justice (Rawls's philosophical framework, extensively operationalised in social-psychological fairness research — Adams's equity theory), procedural justice (Tyler's work on why people accept outcomes as legitimate — procedural fairness of the process matters independently of the outcome's favourability to them), and retributive justice (the psychology of proportionate desert). Haidt's moral foundations theory treats fairness/justice as one of the primary cross-cultural moral foundations alongside purity/sanctity (Section 1). Third-party punishment research (Fehr and Gächter, *Nature*, 2002) demonstrates that humans reliably incur personal cost to enforce fairness norms even when they are not themselves the wronged party — evidence that justice-concern is not purely self-interested.

**Emergence — what causes concern for righteousness and justice to arise:**
Justice-concern arises from the perception that outcomes, processes, or treatment are disproportionate to desert or need — equity theory identifies perceived imbalance between one's own or another's inputs and outcomes relative to a comparison standard as the specific trigger. Righteousness as a personal quality (as distinct from justice as a state of affairs) arises through the sustained practice of right action and right judgment, consolidating into settled character.

**Reduction and absence — what causes justice-concern or righteous conduct to diminish:**
Diminished by moral disengagement mechanisms (Bandura — see M06, M10) that allow injustice to be rationalised or its victims discounted, by the normalisation of injustice through prolonged exposure, and, for personal righteousness specifically, by the same incremental-compromise erosion documented in M13's integrity section.

**Location — where righteousness and justice are seated:**
Righteousness as personal character is characterological — a stable disposition toward right action, closely related to the integrity vocabulary of Section 4. Justice as a state of affairs is more social and structural — a property of relationships, processes, and institutions rather than of any one person's inner state alone.

**External influences:**
*Developmental:* Fairness sensitivity emerges early in childhood (infants as young as 15 months show measurable surprise at unfair resource distribution in looking-time studies) and develops in sophistication through moral development (Kohlberg, Haidt). *Cultural:* What counts as a just process or outcome varies by culture, though the underlying fairness-sensitivity appears to be a human universal. *Social:* Institutions that reliably enforce justice cultivate greater trust and cooperation (Tyler's procedural-justice research) than those that do not, independent of the material outcomes they deliver.

**Coverage assessment:** Rich. Justice and fairness research is one of the most extensively developed areas of moral and social psychology.

### Section 4 — Uprightness and integrity

**Primary field:** Positive psychology / virtue psychology

**Glosses covered:**
upright: right / upright: straight (ya.shar), uprightness (H3476, H3483, H4339, H6968), upright (H5229), be upright (G3716), upright (G3717), integrity (H8537, H8538)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Integrity psychology (Palanski and Yammarino, *What Does the Concept of Integrity Hold Together?*, 2009) identifies integrity as consistency between words and actions, and between espoused and enacted values — this section's vocabulary maps closely onto M13's own integrity content (the two clusters' scopes touch here and should be compared in Session B). Uprightness (ya.shar — straight, right, without deviation) describes the person whose path and character are not crooked, a consistency metaphor central to Hebrew wisdom literature's moral vision and closely related to the purity metaphor of Section 1 (both use a spatial/physical image — clean versus stained, straight versus crooked — for a moral quality).

**Emergence — what causes uprightness and integrity to develop:**
Integrity develops through habituated, consistent conduct over time, strong moral identity (Aquino and Reed's moral-identity research — where being a moral person is central to one's self-concept, behaviour is more reliably consistent with stated values), and community accountability that names and corrects drift between profession and practice.

**Reduction and absence — what causes integrity to diminish:**
Eroded by incremental compromise, rationalisation of inconsistency, and impression management that substitutes outward performance for genuine inner consistency — see M13's Section 3 for the fuller treatment of this dynamic, which this section's uprightness vocabulary substantially overlaps.

**Location — where uprightness and integrity are seated:**
Characterological — a whole-person disposition toward consistency between inner values and outward conduct, expressed in behaviour but grounded in character rather than sustainable as pure performance.

**External influences:**
*Developmental:* Moral community membership and parental modelling are the primary external shapers. *Social:* Sustained accountability relationships that notice and name inconsistency are among the strongest supports for integrity.

**Coverage assessment:** Good, though this section substantially duplicates ground also covered in M13's own integrity section — Session B should determine how the two clusters' overlapping uprightness/integrity vocabulary is best distributed.

## Outside-science inventory

- **righteousness [God] (H6664H)** — God's own righteousness as an ontological attribute, and imputed righteousness received by faith (the Pauline register of G1343) — are theological categories that moral psychology's account of justice and fair dealing does not reach.
- **to consecrate (qa.dash) in its cultic register** — the specific mechanics of Levitical consecration (what may be consecrated, by what rite, with what consequence) is a theologically and legally structured system beyond sociology of the sacred's general account.
- **unblemished (ta.mim)** in its sacrificial register — the requirement that sacrificial offerings be without blemish, and its typological application to Christ — is a theological category exceeding the purity-psychology account of blamelessness.
- **Hall [of Justice] (H0197H)** where it names a specifically covenantal or theocratic judicial institution rather than a generic human court, its theological-political structure exceeds ordinary justice-psychology's descriptive scope.

**Observation:** M12 spans a wide range — from the somatic/disgust-rooted psychology of purity, through the sociology of consecration, to the well-developed literatures on justice and personal integrity. Its outside-science content is concentrated at the points where human purity, consecration, and righteousness language is applied directly to God or to specifically covenantal/cultic structures. The cluster overlaps meaningfully with M10c (defilement, the negative pole of Section 1) and M13 (integrity, substantially duplicated in Section 4) — both worth flagging for Session B's cross-cluster boundary review.

## D9/D11/D12 prompt reference notes

> These four catalogue questions (`D9.1.1`, `D9.2.1`, `D11.2.1`, `D12.1.1`) replace the retired `T7.3` prompt as of the 2026-09-17 catalogue realignment (`catalogue_version='v2-dimension-realign-20260917'`). Each is answered here from this document's science sections as the pre-analytical data input; the verse-evidence side of each question is completed at the characteristic-level answer stage, not here.

**D9.1.1 — Physiological/Neural Substrate:** Moderate, concentrated in Section 1. Moral purity judgment shares neural substrate (insula) with physical disgust (see M10c); the Macbeth-effect research demonstrates a measurable behavioural link between moral transgression and physical-cleansing motivation, evidence of genuine embodied substrate rather than pure metaphor. Justice/fairness judgments (Section 3) engage reward and conflict-monitoring circuitry (striatum, anterior cingulate) in neuroeconomic fairness studies (ultimatum-game research). Uprightness/integrity (Section 4) has the thinnest direct substrate literature of the four sections.

**D9.2.1 — Generational Transmission:** Moderate. Fairness sensitivity has documented early developmental emergence and is shaped by parental modelling of just and unjust treatment; purity-based moral reasoning is likewise shaped by childhood socialisation (see M10c). Consecration/holy-designation (Section 2) is transmitted through religious formation across generations by definition.

**D11.2.1 — Innate Endowment:** Partial. Fairness-sensitivity shows early, plausibly innate developmental emergence (infant looking-time studies) alongside purity/disgust's well-evidenced innate substrate (Section 1's cross-reference to M10c) — together the strongest innate-endowment case in this cluster. Integrity and consistency between values and action (Section 4), and consecration as a formal status (Section 2), are not treated as innate — they are built through habituation and community process.

**D12.1.1 — Social/Behavioural Manifestation:** Mixed, leaning cooperative/prosocial. Justice and fairness (Section 3) are inherently other-directed and often cooperative in function (third-party punishment research shows people enforce fairness norms at personal cost, a prosocial-enforcement behaviour); purity and consecration (Sections 1–2) function more to maintain a community's shared moral/sacred categories than to cooperate toward an external goal; integrity (Section 4) is primarily self-directed consistency, socially verified by others rather than aimed at them.
