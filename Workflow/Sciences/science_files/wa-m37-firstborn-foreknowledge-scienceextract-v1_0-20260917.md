## Science sections

### Section 1 — Birth order, firstborn status, and family systems

**Primary field:** Developmental psychology / family systems research

**Glosses covered:**
firstborn (G4416 prōtotokos, H1060, H1067, H6363A, H6363B), to be/bear firstborn (H1069)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Birth-order research has a long and contested history in psychology. Sulloway's *Born to Rebel* (1996) proposed that birth order systematically shapes personality and life strategy — firstborns tending toward conscientiousness, conservatism, and identification with parental authority, later-borns toward openness and rebellion — through a Darwinian "niche" framework in which siblings differentiate to reduce competition for parental resources. However, large, well-controlled subsequent studies (Rohrer, Egloff and Schmukle, *PNAS*, 2015, analysing over 20,000 individuals across three national datasets) found no reliable birth-order effect on the broad personality traits Sulloway's theory predicted, though they did confirm a small, robust effect on measured intelligence (firstborns scoring marginally higher, most plausibly attributable to differences in early parental attention and resource allocation rather than to birth order itself as a causal personality-shaping mechanism). What is more robustly established, separate from personality, is that firstborn status carries genuine differential social and material treatment in many family and legal systems historically and cross-culturally — primogeniture as an inheritance and status practice is well documented anthropologically, independent of any claimed psychological effect on the firstborn's own disposition.

**Emergence — what causes firstborn status to carry the significance it does:**
Firstborn status is assigned purely by birth sequence — it requires no achievement or personal quality. Its social and psychological significance arises entirely from the meaning a given family or legal system attaches to it (inheritance rights, family leadership expectations, ritual status), not from any well-established innate personality difference the birth-order literature can currently substantiate at the level the older, popular Sulloway account claimed.

**Reduction and absence — what causes firstborn privilege to be diminished or reassigned:**
Where legal or family systems formally reassign firstborn privilege (a documented and recurring pattern in the biblical narratives themselves — Jacob over Esau, Ephraim over Manasseh, Joseph's status among his brothers), the reassignment is a deliberate social/legal act rather than a psychological or biological correction — there is no natural process by which firstborn status is "lost" independent of such a decision.

**Location — where firstborn significance is seated:**
Entirely social, legal, and familial — a status conferred by birth order and interpreted through a given system's rules, not an inner psychological trait of the person so designated. The modest documented intelligence effect (Rohrer et al.) is the one genuinely psychological correlate with reasonably robust empirical support, and even this is most plausibly a resource-allocation effect rather than an effect of birth order per se.

**External influences:**
*Family and legal systems:* The entire significance of firstborn status is externally assigned by the surrounding family, legal, and cultural system. *Cultural:* Primogeniture and its alternatives (ultimogeniture, partible inheritance, merit-based succession) vary widely across cultures and historical periods, confirming that the significance attached to birth order is a social convention rather than a natural psychological given. *Circumstances:* Documented biblical instances of firstborn status being overridden by paternal or divine choice illustrate that even within systems that formally privilege firstborn status, the privilege is not treated as absolute or unchangeable.

**Coverage assessment:** Thin to moderate. Birth-order psychology as popularly understood (Sulloway) has been substantially undermined by later, better-controlled research; what remains solid is mostly social/legal/anthropological rather than deep psychological content.

### Section 2 — Foreknowledge, predetermination, and prediction

**Primary field:** Cognitive science / philosophy of mind (prediction and determinism)

**Glosses covered:**
foreknowledge (G4268 prognōsis), to predetermine (G4255 proorizō, G4384 protithēmi), to know/choose (G4268-family cross-reference proginōskō)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Cognitive science's predictive-processing framework (Clark, *Surfing Uncertainty*, 2016; Friston's free-energy principle) treats the brain as fundamentally a prediction-generating system, constantly anticipating future states and updating on error — a mechanistic account of a kind of "foreknowledge" operating continuously and largely unconsciously at the level of ordinary perception and action, though of course entirely unlike the theological sense of divine foreknowledge the cluster's vocabulary carries. The philosophy and psychology of determinism and free will (a live and unresolved debate spanning neuroscience — Libet's controversial readiness-potential experiments, 1983, and their extensive subsequent reinterpretation — and philosophy) addresses the more directly relevant question of whether human choices are determined by prior causes in a way that would be compatible or incompatible with genuine agency; this literature is highly contested and offers no settled resolution, only a mapped landscape of positions (hard determinism, libertarian free will, compatibilism).

**Emergence — what causes the human sense of foreknowledge/prediction to arise:**
At the level of ordinary cognition, predictive capacity arises from the brain's continuous generation of expectations based on prior experience, updated by incoming sensory error signal — a well-established mechanistic account, though one that operates on statistical regularity rather than certain knowledge of a specific unique future event, which is the sense the cluster's theological vocabulary (God's foreknowledge, predetermination) actually carries.

**Reduction and absence — what causes predictive confidence to diminish:**
Human predictive confidence is reduced by genuine novelty (events outside the statistical pattern the predictive system has been trained on), by insufficient data, and — at the more philosophical level — by the fundamentally probabilistic, non-certain character of all human prediction, which is categorically different from the certain, exhaustive foreknowledge the cluster's theological vocabulary attributes to God.

**Location — where predictive cognition is seated:**
Neurologically distributed — predictive processing is not localised to a single brain region but is treated in contemporary cognitive science as a pervasive organising principle of neural function generally, from low-level perception through to high-level planning and social cognition.

**External influences:**
*Developmental:* Predictive capacity develops through accumulated experience — infants' predictive models of the physical and social world become progressively more accurate and complex through childhood. *Circumstances:* The reliability of any prediction depends on the stability of the underlying pattern being predicted; genuinely novel or chaotic systems resist accurate human prediction regardless of experience or cognitive capacity.

**Coverage assessment:** Thin, and necessarily so — the cluster's core content (God's foreknowledge and predetermination) names an exhaustive, certain knowledge of the entire future that has no scientific analogue whatsoever; cognitive science's predictive-processing research describes a mechanistically interesting but categorically different phenomenon (statistical, probabilistic, fallible human anticipation).

### Section 3 — Calling, choosing, and encounter

**Primary field:** Social psychology of vocation / decision psychology

**Glosses covered:**
to call (on)/name (G1951 epikaleō-family), to call to/summon (G3333, G3343), to call together (G4779), to choose (G haireō-family — represented via the choosing terms in the live list), to encounter: meet/toward/chanced (H7122G, H7122H, H7122I), to read (H7123)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Vocational and calling psychology (Dik and Duffy, *Research in Personnel and Human Resources Management*, 2009, on the psychology of "calling" as a felt sense of purposive summons toward a particular path) treats the subjective experience of being called — as against merely choosing a path through ordinary preference-weighing — as a distinct psychological construct associated with greater work meaning and persistence, regardless of whether the caller is understood as transcendent, communal, or internal to the person. Decision psychology's research on choice under identity-relevance (Baumeister's work on choice and self) addresses how significant choices (choosing a person, a path, a calling) differ psychologically from routine preference decisions in their weight and their entanglement with self-concept. The "chance encounter" vocabulary (H7122 family, used famously of Ruth's "happening" upon Boaz's field) has a loose parallel in research on the psychology of serendipity and meaningful coincidence — how people retrospectively interpret chance events as significant or providential, a well-documented cognitive tendency (the broader literature on patternicity and meaning-making from randomness, Shermer and others) though one that, again, describes a human interpretive tendency rather than adjudicating whether any given "chance" encounter was in fact providentially arranged.

**Emergence — what causes the experience of being called or of significant encounter to arise:**
A felt sense of calling arises when a path or task is experienced as summoning the person from beyond their own preference-calculation — often, in the empirical literature on calling, associated with a strong sense of fit between the task and deep personal identity or values. The experience of "chance encounter" as significant arises from the retrospective interpretive work people characteristically do in assembling a coherent narrative from otherwise unremarkable coincidences, a well-documented cognitive tendency though not one that determines whether such interpretation is warranted in any given case.

**Reduction and absence — what causes the sense of calling to diminish:**
The felt sense of calling diminishes where the connection between task and identity weakens, where sustained obstacles undermine confidence that the path is genuinely summoned rather than merely chosen, or where the community or tradition that originally validated the calling withdraws its support.

**Location — where calling and significant encounter are seated:**
Calling is cognitive-motivational (a felt sense of summons) and relational (typically understood as coming from beyond the self, whether communally, transcendently, or through circumstance). The interpretation of encounter as meaningful is a cognitive, interpretive act performed after the fact.

**External influences:**
*Social and communal:* A supporting community that recognises and validates a person's sense of calling substantially strengthens and sustains it. *Developmental:* Early formation shapes what kinds of calling feel available or fitting to pursue. *Circumstances:* The specific occasions that come to be interpreted as calling or providential encounter are, by definition, circumstantial and unrepeatable.

**Coverage assessment:** Moderate. Vocational-calling psychology and the psychology of meaning-making from coincidence provide genuine, if partial, coverage of the human-experience side of this section; the theological claim of divine summons or providential arrangement is outside it.

## Outside-science inventory

- **Divine foreknowledge as exhaustive and certain (Romans 8:29, 1 Peter 1:2)** — God's knowledge of the entire future with certainty is a theological claim with no scientific analogue; predictive-processing neuroscience describes fallible, probabilistic human prediction, a categorically different phenomenon.
- **Predetermination/election as God's sovereign act (Romans 8:29–30, Ephesians 1:5)** — the theological claim that God determines outcomes, including human salvation, prior to and independent of human choice, sits within the unresolved free-will/determinism debate in philosophy but is not itself a scientific claim that any empirical finding can settle; philosophy of mind maps the conceptual terrain without resolving the theological question.
- **Firstborn status as theologically significant (Israel as God's firstborn, Exodus 4:22; Christ as "firstborn of all creation," Colossians 1:15; "firstborn among many brothers," Romans 8:29)** — the theological use of firstborn language to denote covenantal privilege and Christ's unique status exceeds the family-systems and inheritance-law content addressed in Section 1.
- **Providential encounter (Ruth's "happening" upon Boaz's field, Ruth 2:3)** — that a specific "chance" event was in fact arranged by God's providence is a theological claim about divine action in history, distinct from and not addressed by the psychology of how people interpret coincidence as meaningful.

**Observation:** M37 has an unusually high concentration of outside-science content relative to its size. Each of its three sections has a genuine but strictly limited human-level parallel (birth-order social significance, predictive cognition, vocational calling and meaning-making from coincidence); in every case the cluster's actual theological weight — certain divine foreknowledge, sovereign predetermination, providential arrangement — exceeds what any of the parallel literatures can address, since each names a specifically divine act or knowledge rather than a describable human psychological process.

## D9.1.1 / D9.2.1 / D11.2.1 / D12.1.1 prompt reference notes

**D9.1.1 — Physiological/neural substrate:** Thin to moderate. Predictive cognition (Section 2) has a well-developed, if general, neural-substrate literature (predictive-processing neuroscience treats prediction as a pervasive organising principle of brain function). Firstborn status and calling/encounter have essentially no dedicated neural substrate of their own — they are social/interpretive phenomena, not physiological states.

**D9.2.1 — Generational transmission:** Central to Section 1 by definition — firstborn status is itself a generational-sequence fact, and primogeniture systems are explicitly mechanisms for transmitting privilege and inheritance across generations. Foreknowledge and calling have no meaningful generational-transmission literature of their own beyond the general transmission of vocational and religious formation addressed elsewhere in the programme (M21).

**D11.2.1 — Innate endowment vs. acquired:** Firstborn status is neither innate nor acquired in the usual trait sense — it is a fixed, assigned fact of birth sequence with socially constructed significance. Predictive cognitive capacity is substantially innate (a basic feature of neural architecture) though its specific content is built up through acquired experience. The sense of calling is acquired through formation, reflection, and community validation rather than present from birth.

**D12.1.1 — Social/behavioural manifestation:** High for firstborn status (a publicly recognised social and legal fact) and for calling (typically expressed and validated through visible commitment to a path or vocation). Foreknowledge/predetermination, by contrast, names a state of knowledge or determination that — on the theological register the cluster carries — belongs to God and has no direct human behavioural manifestation of its own; its manifestation is only in the outcomes it is said to determine, not in any observable process.
