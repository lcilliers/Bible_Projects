## Science sections

### Section 1 — Vocal expression of distress: cry, outcry, and lament

**Primary field:** Affective science / psychoacoustics

**Glosses covered:**
outcry (H2201 za'aqah, H6682 tsevachah, G0995 boē), moaning (H1899 hegeh), to mutter (H1897 hagah)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Scherer's vocal emotion research (*Speech Communication*, 2003) identifies acoustic features of emotional vocalisations — pitch, intensity, rate, and voice quality — that reliably signal emotional states across cultures. Juslin and Laukka's meta-analysis (*Psychological Bulletin*, 2003) confirms that vocal expression of emotion is decoded accurately above chance cross-culturally, establishing a genuine cross-cultural vocal-emotion signalling system rather than a purely learned, culture-specific code. The cry of distress functions, on this account, as a social distress signal: it communicates need, demands response, and reliably activates caregiving behaviour in observers who hear it — an evolved signalling function with clear survival value, well documented from infant crying research (Soltis's cry-communication research programme) through to adult distress vocalisation. Lament as a structured cultural form (Rosenblatt's cross-cultural mourning research) addresses the communal function of ritualised vocal grief expression — outcry that is not merely an individual physiological release but a socially recognised and, in many cultures including ancient Israel's, formally patterned practice (the qinah/lament genre) that both expresses and processes grief within a community structure.

**Emergence — what causes distress vocalisation to arise:**
Emotional vocalisations arise when internal affective states exceed the capacity for silent containment — the overflow of inner experience into bodily, vocal expression. The cry of distress specifically arises in situations of genuine need and threat where the person cannot self-rescue — functionally a call for help, consistent with its evolved signalling function. Moaning (hegeh) arises from sustained distress not acute enough for full outcry but too sustained for silence — a lower-intensity, ongoing register distinct from the sharp acute cry. Muttering (hagah, shared with the old M42 file's inner-speech treatment of the same root) sits at a genuine semantic boundary within this cluster: it spans from sub-vocal or vocalised distress/complaint through to meditative rehearsal (the positive sense used of the righteous "meditating" on the law, Ps 1:2) — the analyst should expect verse context, not the term alone, to determine which register applies in a given occurrence.

**Reduction and absence — what causes distress vocalisation to diminish:**
Vocalisation diminishes through resolution of the underlying distress, through social contexts that suppress emotional expression (silence can represent either genuine peace or enforced suppression — see also the old M42 file's own note on this ambiguity), through the exhaustion of expressive capacity under prolonged distress, and through internalised norms against public emotional display. The presence of a receptive community is a well-documented enabler of vocal grief expression; its absence suppresses it — the biblical example most directly on point (Ps 137's "how shall we sing the LORD's song in a strange land") names precisely this suppression under conditions of displacement and captivity.

**Location — where distress vocalisation is seated:**
Simultaneously an inner-being expression (the grief, distress, or fear that produces it) and an outer-world acoustic event; for the programme's purposes the relevant location is the inner state the cry evidences, with the vocalisation itself serving as observable, externally verifiable evidence of that state's intensity. Physiologically, distress vocalisation is produced by autonomic arousal interacting with the vocal apparatus — it is substantially involuntary/released rather than deliberately composed, distinguishing it from ordinary structured speech.

**External influences:**
*Cultural:* Lament forms and their social acceptability are culturally shaped — cultures vary widely in whether open vocal grief expression is normative, ritualised, or stigmatised. *Social:* The presence of a receptive community enables sustained vocal expression of distress; its absence, or active hostility, suppresses it. *Circumstances:* Genuine acute threat or loss is the primary and most straightforward trigger.

**Coverage assessment:** Good. Vocal emotion research provides direct, well-developed coverage, consistent with the old M42 file's own assessment of this material.

### Section 2 — Roaring: the vocalization of overwhelming affect and aggressive dominance

**Primary field:** Psychoacoustics / comparative and evolutionary psychology of vocal threat display

**Glosses covered:**
to roar (H1993 hamah, H7580 sha'ag, G3455 mukaomai, G5612 ōryomai), roaring (H5099 nahmah, H7581 she'agah)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Roaring occupies a distinct acoustic and functional category within vocal-emotion research from the higher-pitched, help-signalling cry treated in Section 1: comparative ethology documents that low-frequency, high-amplitude vocalisation (the acoustic profile of a roar) functions across many species, humans included in attenuated form, as a threat/dominance display rather than a distress-and-appeal signal — the acoustic features themselves (low pitch exaggerating perceived body size, sustained intensity) are what make a roar read as formidable rather than vulnerable (Morton's "motivation-structural rules" research on cross-species acoustic signalling, *American Naturalist*, 1977, remains the foundational framework here). This is directly relevant to sha'ag's dual biblical usage — both the literal roar of a lion (a genuine threat-display referent) and its extension to intense human grief, rage, or anguish "roared" aloud (Job's and the Psalmist's roaring in distress) — the same acoustic register serving, depending on context, either an aggressive-dominance or an overwhelmed-distress function, a genuine ambiguity the science literature's own dual functional account of low-frequency vocalisation independently supports rather than resolves. This section's content also connects directly to M02's anger vocabulary (roaring as an expression of rage) and to M72's authority/dominance vocabulary (roaring as a display of formidable power), making it one of this batch's clearer cross-cluster convergence points.

**Emergence — what causes roaring to arise:**
Roaring arises from the most intense affective states — overwhelming grief, rage, or anguish — that exceed the capacity even of an ordinary cry, or (in its aggressive-display sense) from a deliberate or semi-deliberate assertion of formidable threat or dominance. The dual usage noted above means the same vocal act can emerge from either register, and disambiguating the two in a given text requires attention to context (is the roarer under threat, or issuing one?).

**Reduction and absence:**
As with Section 1, resolution of the underlying overwhelming state is the primary reducer for the distress-register roar; for the dominance-display register, the withdrawal or defeat of the party being displayed against, or the absence of any rival requiring such display, is the relevant reducer.

**Location — where roaring is seated:**
Physiologically demanding and acoustically extreme relative to ordinary speech or even ordinary crying — roaring recruits maximal respiratory and laryngeal effort, consistent with its function as a maximal-intensity signal in both the distress and dominance registers.

**Coverage assessment:** Moderate. Comparative/evolutionary vocal-signalling research provides a genuine and somewhat under-used point of contact; the specific human psychology of roaring (as opposed to ordinary crying) is less separately studied than the general vocal-emotion literature covering Section 1.

### Section 3 — Shouting: collective, triumphant, and summoning vocalization

**Primary field:** Social/crowd psychology — collective vocal behaviour

**Glosses covered:**
to shout (H7321 rua, H1906 hedad?, H1959 geah?, G2019 anaphōneō, G2905 kraugazō), shouting (H7452 rea, H8643 teru'ah, H8663 tesu'ah, G2906 kraugē), voice/sound: noise (G5456H phōnē), voice (H7032G/H qol)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Durkheim's concept of collective effervescence (*The Elementary Forms of Religious Life*, 1912) addresses the distinctive heightened, shared emotional state produced when a group vocalises or acts in unison — a phenomenon with a genuine and growing contemporary empirical base in synchrony research (Wiltermuth and Heath, *Psychological Science*, 2009, on synchronous activity's effect on cooperation and group bonding). Shared shouting — whether triumphant (teru'ah, the trumpet-blast/shout of acclamation associated with battle victory, coronation, and festival celebration) or summoning/alarm (rua's broader range including battle-signal and warning senses) — functions, on this account, not merely as individual vocal expression multiplied across a crowd but as a distinct collective phenomenon that measurably strengthens in-group bonding and shared identity beyond what any individual's shout alone would produce. This connects directly to M71's boasting/glorying-together vocabulary (sugkauchaomai) and to worship/praise vocabulary (live M22 territory) as a related collective-vocal phenomenon.

**Emergence — what causes collective shouting to arise:**
Collective shouting arises around shared, high-stakes, high-salience events — victory, coronation, alarm, festival culmination — where synchrony itself (many voices in a coordinated vocal act) amplifies the individual emotional state into a group-level experience with its own bonding and identity-reinforcing effects, independent of and beyond the triggering event's own significance.

**Reduction and absence:**
Absent a shared triggering event or sufficient group cohesion to produce genuine synchrony, shouting remains individual rather than collective, forfeiting the amplification effect; suppressed or forbidden shouting (in contexts of subjugation or exile) removes the community's capacity to mark and process shared triumph or alarm together.

**Location — where collective shouting is seated:**
Genuinely both individual (each participant's own vocalisation and affect) and emergent-collective (a group-level phenomenon that is not reducible to the sum of individual vocalisations) — synchrony research treats the collective bonding effect as a real, separately measurable outcome distinct from each participant's private experience.

**Coverage assessment:** Moderate and growing — collective-behaviour and synchrony research is an active contemporary field with a genuine point of contact, though the specific ancient ritual-acclamation and battle-shout forms this vocabulary names have thinner direct study than the general synchrony-bonding mechanism.

## Outside-science inventory

- **the cry of the oppressed reaching God (za'aqah as covenantal appeal, e.g. the cry of Israel in Egypt, Exod 2:23–24; 3:7)** — the theological claim that this specific cry is heard and answered by God is a covenantal category exceeding the distress-signalling function documented in Section 1.
- **the shout of the LORD / theophanic roaring (sha'ag applied to God, e.g. Amos 1:2, "the LORD roars from Zion")** — divine roaring as a theophanic and judicial pronouncement is a theological category the comparative-ethology account in Section 2 cannot itself reach, though it illuminates why roaring is the chosen register for conveying maximal, formidable authority.
- **the shout of acclamation at Christ's return (1 Thess 4:16, "with a shout... the Lord himself will descend")** — an eschatological event described partly in this cluster's own vocabulary, exceeding collective-behaviour research's descriptive scope.
- **liturgical/cultic shouting (the trumpet-blast/teru'ah of festival and covenant renewal)** — the specific ritual-legal significance of the biblical festival shout within Israel's cultic calendar exceeds general crowd-psychology research.

**Observation:** This cluster carries forward the old M42 file's own finding that vocal-emotion research maps precisely onto the biblical cry/outcry vocabulary's acoustic and functional precision, and extends it with a genuinely under-used but directly applicable comparative-ethology account of roaring and a growing synchrony-research account of collective shouting — both registers the old M42 file touched only lightly within its broader speech-focused scope.

## Catalogue prompt reference notes (D9.1.1 / D9.2.1 / D11.2.1 / D12.1.1)

**D9.1.1 — Physiological/neural substrate:** Distress vocalisation (Section 1) engages autonomic arousal interacting with the vocal apparatus, largely involuntary in onset; roaring (Section 2) recruits maximal respiratory/laryngeal effort and shares threat-appraisal circuitry with M01's fear substrate (whether producing or responding to the display); collective shouting (Section 3) additionally engages the reward and bonding circuitry associated with behavioural synchrony (oxytocin-linked systems implicated in synchrony research).

**D9.2.1 — Generational transmission:** Lament forms and their social acceptability (Section 1), the ritual occasions for collective shouting (Section 3), and the cultural coding of what a roar signals (Section 2) are all substantially transmitted through cultural and liturgical practice across generations rather than through heredity — this cluster's transmission pathway is almost entirely cultural/practice-based.

**D11.2.1 — Innate endowment vs. acquired:** The underlying vocal-signalling capacities (distress cry, threat-display roar, synchrony-seeking collective vocalisation) draw on innate, evolutionarily old signalling systems shared in modified form across species; their specific cultural forms (lament genre, festival shout, battle cry) are acquired and richly culturally elaborated on that innate base.

**D12.1.1 — Social/behavioural manifestation:** This cluster is, definitionally, entirely behavioural/manifest — every term names an audible, observable vocal act rather than a silent inner state, making it (together with M76's walk vocabulary) one of the programme's clearest cases of inner-being content that is inseparable from its outward, physically enacted expression.
