## Science sections

### Section 1 — Seeking, searching, and goal-directed inquiry

**Primary field:** Cognitive psychology / motivational psychology — curiosity and information-seeking research

**Glosses covered:**
to seek after (G1934 epizēteō), to seek out (G1567 ekzēteō), to seek (G2212 zēteō, G2614 katadiōkō, H1245 baqash, H1875 darash, H7836 shachar)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Loewenstein's information-gap theory of curiosity (*Psychological Bulletin*, 1994) proposes that curiosity, and the seeking behaviour it motivates, arises from the awareness of a gap between what one currently knows and what one wants to know — the gap itself, not merely the value of the information, generates motivational tension toward its resolution. Wilson's information-seeking-behaviour research addresses the structured, motivated pursuit of information across everyday and professional contexts, providing a general behavioural-science account of what "seeking" looks like as sustained goal-directed action rather than a single momentary act. Batson's quest religious-orientation research (*Religion and the Individual*, 1993) is particularly relevant to this cluster's theological register: quest orientation names a form of religious engagement characterised by open, ongoing questioning of ultimate questions rather than settled certainty — seeking as a way of relating to the sought object that need not terminate in a fixed, final possession of the answer, which offers a partial psychological parallel to darash's own sense of earnest, sustained inquiry (darash's root carries connotations of diligent, careful searching-out, distinct from a merely casual look). The four Hebrew and Greek seeking-verbs represented in this small cluster span a genuine intensity range — from general zēteō/baqash (ordinary seeking) through the more intensive ekzēteō/darash (seeking out, seeking earnestly) to shachar's specific "seeking at dawn" sense (seeking early and diligently, from the same root as the word for dawn) — a graded intensity structure the science literature's own distinction between casual browsing and committed, effortful information-seeking behaviour independently supports.

**Emergence — what causes seeking to arise:**
Seeking arises from the awareness of something not yet possessed, known, or attained — a gap that generates motivational tension oriented toward its resolution. Achievement-goal theory (Elliot and McGregor's 2×2 framework, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 2001) adds a further useful distinction: seeking can be approach-motivated (oriented toward attaining a positively valued object) or avoidance-motivated (oriented toward escaping a feared lack), a distinction plausibly relevant to differentiating verses where seeking is driven by desire for the sought object itself versus by fear of its absence. Religious/existential seeking (Batson's quest orientation) arises specifically from unresolved ultimate questions that resist the kind of definitive, closing-the-gap resolution ordinary information-seeking achieves.

**Reduction and absence — what causes seeking to diminish:**
Seeking resolves (in the successful case) when the sought object is found or the information gap is closed, producing the characteristic satisfaction of curiosity's resolution. It can also diminish through discouragement (repeated unsuccessful seeking reducing motivation, a pattern related to learned-helplessness dynamics discussed elsewhere in this batch, e.g. M78), through the substitution of a lesser, more easily attained object for the genuinely sought one, or — in the quest-orientation register specifically — through premature foreclosure, settling for an answer without genuine engagement with the question, which Batson's research treats as a distinct outcome from either continued seeking or genuine finding.

**Location — where seeking is seated:**
Primarily motivational and cognitive — an oriented state of the whole person moving toward a not-yet-attained object, with a genuine emotional dimension (the restlessness of the one who seeks, the anticipation attached to finding) and a behavioural dimension (directed searching activity, observable to others).

**External influences:**
*Developmental:* Curiosity and information-seeking disposition show measurable individual differences that are partly stable across the lifespan and partly shaped by whether early environments rewarded or discouraged exploratory seeking. *Social/cultural:* Communities and traditions that valorise ongoing inquiry (quest orientation, Batson) versus those that valorise settled certainty produce different seeking profiles in their members; the intensity and diligence implied by darash and shachar suggest the biblical vocabulary itself values sustained, effortful seeking over passive or perfunctory inquiry. *Circumstances:* Genuine unmet need (material, relational, or existential) is the most straightforward driver of goal-directed seeking behaviour.

**Coverage assessment:** Moderate, consistent with the old M41 file's own assessment — curiosity and information-seeking research provide real coverage of the general mechanism; the theological seeking of God specifically (darash/baqash applied to seeking God's face, God's will, or God himself) extends beyond what information-seeking research, built primarily around seeking discrete facts or objects, was designed to address.

## Outside-science inventory

- **seeking God/seeking God's face (darash, baqash applied to God as object)** — the theological category of seeking the living God — not a fact or object to be acquired and thereby resolved, but a person to be known in relationship — exceeds the information-gap model's basic structure, in which the gap closes once the sought information is obtained.
- **seeking the kingdom of God (Matt 6:33, though itself outside this cluster's specific gloss list, a natural extension of its content)** — seeking framed as the primary, ordering orientation of a whole life rather than a bounded task with a completion point is a theological-vocational category exceeding ordinary goal-directed-seeking psychology.
- **being sought by God (the reciprocal direction — God seeking out his people, e.g. the lost-sheep/lost-coin pattern)** — where the initiative of seeking is reversed and God is the seeker, this is a theological claim about divine action, not a human-seeking-behaviour phenomenon this section's literature addresses.

## Catalogue prompt reference notes (D9.1.1 / D9.2.1 / D11.2.1 / D12.1.1)

**D9.1.1 — Physiological/neural substrate:** Curiosity and information-seeking engage dopaminergic reward-anticipation circuitry (the same general system implicated in reward-seeking more broadly) — the information gap itself, prior to resolution, is associated with a genuinely aversive/motivating tension state, and its closure with a measurable reward-circuit response, giving seeking behaviour a real, if generic, physiological substrate.

**D9.2.1 — Generational transmission:** Curiosity disposition and the valuing of sustained inquiry (versus premature closure) are substantially shaped by family and community modelling — children raised in environments that reward exploratory seeking develop more durable seeking dispositions than those in environments that discourage or punish open questioning.

**D11.2.1 — Innate endowment vs. acquired:** The basic curiosity/information-gap-sensitivity mechanism is an innate, cross-species motivational system (well documented in comparative psychology); its direction toward specific objects — and especially its theological extension toward seeking God — is entirely acquired through formation, teaching, and relationship.

**D12.1.1 — Social/behavioural manifestation:** Directly behavioural — seeking is, by definition, observable directed activity (searching, inquiring, pursuing), making it one of the more externally legible states in the programme's vocabulary even though its motivational root is internal.
