## Science sections

### Section 1 — Kindness and doing good

**Primary field:** Behavioural science / positive psychology — prosocial behaviour research

**Glosses covered:**
be kind (chrēsteuomai, H2616A, H2177, H2178), good/kind (chrēstos), to do good (G0014–G0017, G2108, G2109, G2140, G2569, G2570G, H2868, H3190, H3191), good news (H1309), hating good (afilagathos — negative), be good (te.ev)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Prosocial behaviour research (Batson, Penner, Dovidio) investigates the conditions under which humans help others. Kindness science specifically (Curry, Rowland, Zlotowitz, and colleagues, *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology*, 2018 meta-analysis) has demonstrated that performing acts of kindness reliably increases the performer's own wellbeing as well as the recipient's, with effect sizes robust across cultures. The bystander effect (Darley and Latané, 1968) identified social inhibition of helping in the presence of others; subsequent research has identified the specific conditions (perceived responsibility, cost of helping, relationship to the person in need) that promote or suppress prosocial action.

**Emergence — what causes kindness and doing good to arise:**
Kindness arises from empathic concern for others, internalised prosocial values and identity, social norms that make kindness expected or rewarded, and mood states that increase other-focus. The moral elevation response (Haidt) — being moved by witnessing kindness in others — can trigger kindness as a contagion effect, spreading through observed example rather than direct instruction.

**Reduction and absence — what causes kindness to diminish:**
Kindness is reduced by self-absorption, depletion of self-regulatory resources (ego depletion — Baumeister), dehumanisation of potential recipients, and competitive or threatening social contexts that activate self-protective rather than prosocial motivation. The negative pole named directly in this cluster's own vocabulary — hating good (afilagathos) — names not merely the absence of kindness but an active aversion to it, a distinct and more severe condition than simple unkindness.

**Location — where kindness is seated:**
Primarily motivational and behavioural, with emotional antecedents (warmth, concern) and cognitive constituents (attending to others' needs and recognising an opportunity to help). "Doing good" in this section's vocabulary is characteristically active and outward — verbs of deed rather than merely disposition.

**External influences:**
*Hereditary:* Agreeableness, the personality dimension most closely aligned with kindness, has moderate heritability. *Parenting:* Authoritative, warm parenting models and reinforces prosocial behaviour; early moral socialisation shapes the internalisation of kindness as a value rather than merely an external norm. *Circumstances:* Resource abundance supports generosity; scarcity and competition reduce it. Safe, trusting social environments increase prosocial behaviour.

**Coverage assessment:** Good. Prosocial behaviour and kindness-intervention research are well developed and directly applicable.

### Section 2 — Gentleness, tenderness, and forbearance

**Primary field:** Positive psychology / personality psychology

**Glosses covered:**
gentleness (G1932, G4236, G4240, H6037, H6038), gentle (G1933, G4239, G2261, G4235, be gentle G3356), tolerance (anochē), not resentful (anexikakos), openness (haplotēs), tender/tenderness (G0527, H2949, H7390, H7391, H7401), to spare (chamal, H2550, G5339)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Gentleness as a trait — low aggressiveness combined with warmth and consideration — overlaps substantially with the Big Five's agreeableness dimension, one of the most consistently prosocial personality traits in five-factor model research (Costa and McCrae; John and Srivastava). Forbearance and non-retaliation (anexikakos, "not resentful") connect to research on dispositional forgiveness and low hostile-attribution bias (Section 3 of M06's review addresses the negative pole of this same territory). McCullough's work on interpersonal forgivingness treats the capacity to tolerate offence without immediate retaliation as a stable trait with clear behavioural and relational benefits.

**Emergence — what causes gentleness and forbearance to arise:**
Gentleness develops through the same habituation process as other character virtues (the Aristotelian account: repeated practice of restrained, considerate response consolidates into disposition), through secure attachment (which reduces the defensive reactivity that makes gentleness difficult), and through the deliberate cultivation of tolerance for others' faults or slowness. Sparing (chamal) specifically names the choice to withhold deserved harm or punishment — a volitional act distinct from mere absence of anger.

**Reduction and absence — what causes gentleness to diminish:**
Gentleness is eroded by chronic stress and depleted self-regulatory capacity (harshness increases under fatigue and threat), by repeated experience of others' harshness (which trains reciprocal hardness), and by social or professional contexts that reward assertive dominance over gentle restraint.

**Location — where gentleness is seated:**
Characterological and behavioural — gentleness is a stable disposition expressed in manner (tone, pace, restraint of force) rather than a single act. It has an affective substrate (low reactive arousal, warmth) and a volitional dimension (the deliberate moderation of one's own power or claim in dealing with another).

**External influences:**
*Hereditary:* Agreeableness has moderate heritability. *Parenting:* Modelling of gentle, restrained response to provocation is among the strongest shapers of a child's own capacity for gentleness. *Circumstances:* Positions of power or strength make gentleness a meaningful, voluntary restraint rather than a default; contexts of threat or scarcity make it harder to sustain.

**Coverage assessment:** Good. Agreeableness and forbearance/forgivingness research provide solid coverage.

### Section 3 — Friendship and hospitality

**Primary field:** Social psychology / positive psychology

**Glosses covered:**
friend (re.eh, H7463), friendly/friend (filos, G5391, G5384), friendship (filia, G5373), like-minded (isopsuchos, homofrōn), brotherly love (filadelfia), loving the brothers (filadelfos), husband-loving (filandros), child loving (filoteknos), hospitable (filoxenos), hospitality (filoxenia), to kiss (katafileō, H5401A), kiss (filēma, H2705, G5370), participant (koinōnos), participation (koinōnia, G3352)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Friendship psychology addresses one of the most robust wellbeing predictors identified in the field: the quality of close social relationships (Diener, Seligman). Aristotle's tripartite friendship taxonomy (friendships of utility, pleasure, and virtue) has been partially operationalised in contemporary relationship research. Baumeister and Leary's need to belong (*Psychological Bulletin*, 1995) establishes belonging as a fundamental human motivation whose frustration produces measurable distress across cultures. Hospitality research (cross-cultural anthropology of guest-host relations, building on Pitt-Rivers) documents hospitality as a near-universal mechanism for extending the circle of belonging to incorporate the outsider.

**Emergence — what causes friendship and hospitality to arise:**
Friendship arises through proximity and repeated exposure, similarity (like-mindedness is among the strongest predictors of friendship formation), reciprocal self-disclosure, and shared experience. Hospitality (filoxenia) arises from the deliberate extension of the friendship/kinship circle to a stranger who has no prior claim on it — a distinct act from ordinary friendship-formation because it is offered rather than mutually built up over time.

**Reduction and absence — what causes friendship and hospitality to diminish:**
Social bonds weaken through geographic separation, competing demands on time and attention, conflict and betrayal, and the substitution of shallow connection for deep relational investment. Cacioppo's epidemiology of loneliness documents the serious health costs of social disconnection. Hospitality diminishes in contexts of scarcity, fear of the stranger, or social norms that discourage extending trust beyond an established in-group.

**Location — where friendship and hospitality are seated:**
Relational by definition — requiring at least two parties, located in the space between persons, sustained by ongoing interaction, and experienced individually as warmth, trust, and belonging. Neurologically, social bonding involves the opioid system (the pleasure of company) and the oxytocin system (trust and closeness); social exclusion activates the same neural pain circuitry as physical injury (Eisenberger).

**External influences:**
*Developmental:* Early peer relationships and sibling bonds shape the capacity for friendship; secure attachment provides the template for trusting, reciprocal friendship. *Cultural:* The definition of friendship, the depth of expected disclosure, and the norms of hospitality vary significantly across cultures — some cultures treat hospitality to strangers as a near-sacred obligation, others as optional courtesy. *Circumstances:* Shared adversity is among the most powerful friendship-forgers.

**Coverage assessment:** Good. Social bonding, belonging, and friendship psychology are well-developed research domains; cross-cultural hospitality research is thinner but directly relevant.

### Section 4 — Reconciliation and relational repair

**Primary field:** Social psychology / clinical psychology

**Glosses covered:**
to reconcile (G0604, G2132, G2644), be reconciled (G1259), reconciliation (katallagē)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Reconciliation research in social psychology (Nadler and Saguy) distinguishes reconciliation (the restoration of relationship) from forgiveness (the internal release of resentment) — reconciliation requires both parties' participation and is therefore more contextually dependent than forgiveness alone. Restorative justice research (Zehr, *Changing Lenses*, 1990) demonstrates that processes oriented toward restoring damaged relationships often produce better outcomes for all parties than purely punitive alternatives.

**Emergence — what causes reconciliation to become possible:**
Reconciliation becomes possible when there is sufficient goodwill and acknowledgement on both sides to begin the process of repair, or when a third party's mediating action creates the conditions for both parties to re-engage. It is more fragile than forgiveness because it requires the cooperation of both parties and is affected by ongoing relationship dynamics.

**Reduction and absence — what prevents reconciliation:**
Reconciliation is prevented by the absence of genuine acknowledgement from the offending party, the injured party's inability to trust again, and ongoing conditions that make the original harm likely to recur.

**Location — where reconciliation is seated:**
Relational — located in the restored or being-restored relationship between parties rather than in either individual's inner state alone.

**External influences:**
The social and community context dramatically shapes whether reconciliation is possible. The presence of trustworthy third parties (mediators, community structures) facilitates it.

**Coverage assessment:** Moderate. This is a small section within M05; the fuller reconciliation literature (including its theological "divine-human reconciliation" register) is addressed more extensively elsewhere in the programme's cluster set (M11's turning/return territory and any dedicated reconciliation cluster).

## Outside-science inventory

- **pious (cha.sid) in its covenant-loyalty register** — cha.sid names a disposition of covenant faithfulness toward God specifically, a theologically structured relational category that science does not capture in its account of prosocial or relational loyalty toward other humans.
- **reconciliation (katallagē) in its theological register** — where this term names the God-human reconciliation accomplished through Christ, it involves a divine initiative not requiring the human party's prior readiness — a structure with no direct science analogue (see the discussion in M11's review).
- **brotherly love (filadelfia) and loving the brothers (filadelfos) within the church** — where these describe the constituted family-bond of the believing community specifically, the theological ground of that bond (shared adoption as children of God) exceeds ordinary friendship psychology's account of similarity-based bonding.

**Observation:** M05's outside-science content is modest and concentrated at the theological-relational edges (covenant loyalty, reconciliation with God, church family-bond). The bulk of the cluster — ordinary human kindness, gentleness, friendship, and hospitality — is well covered by science.

## D9/D11/D12 prompt reference notes

> These four catalogue questions (`D9.1.1`, `D9.2.1`, `D11.2.1`, `D12.1.1`) replace the retired `T7.3` prompt as of the 2026-09-17 catalogue realignment (`catalogue_version='v2-dimension-realign-20260917'`). Each is answered here from this document's science sections as the pre-analytical data input; the verse-evidence side of each question is completed at the characteristic-level answer stage, not here.

**D9.1.1 — Physiological/Neural Substrate:** Moderate. Friendship and hospitality (Section 3) engage the opioid and oxytocin bonding systems well documented in social neuroscience; social exclusion (the failure of this cluster's territory) activates the same neural pain circuitry as physical injury (Eisenberger). Kindness and gentleness (Sections 1–2) are less clearly tied to a specific substrate and are better characterised as trait/behavioural constructs than embodied states.

**D9.2.1 — Generational Transmission:** Well documented. Parental modelling of gentle, restrained response to provocation (Section 2) and of kindness and hospitality (Sections 1, 3) are among the strongest shapers of a child's own later disposition — this is one of the more consistently generationally-transmitted characteristics in the programme's science coverage.

**D11.2.1 — Innate Endowment:** Partial. Agreeableness, the personality dimension underlying both kindness and gentleness, has documented heritable components (moderate, per twin studies), and the need to belong (underlying friendship-seeking) is argued by Baumeister and Leary to be a fundamental, near-universal human motivation. Hospitality to the stranger specifically, however, is not treated as innate — cross-cultural variation in hospitality norms is substantial, suggesting it is significantly shaped rather than given.

**D12.1.1 — Social/Behavioural Manifestation:** Strongly cooperative. Every section of this cluster is by nature other-directed and prosocial — kindness, gentleness, friendship, hospitality, and reconciliation all function to build, extend, or repair relational bonds rather than to compete or exclude. This cluster shows one of the clearest cooperative-manifestation profiles reviewed in this batch, comparable to M39.
