## Science sections

### Section 1 — Attachment, romantic and familial love, and affection

**Primary field:** Developmental psychology / attachment theory

**Glosses covered:**
affectionate (filostorgos), brotherly love (filadelfia), love (agapē), lovely (prosfilēs), lover of good (filagathos), to love (agapaō), to love (fileō), be lovely (na.ah), beloved (o.hav), beloved (ye.di.dut), love (a.ha.vah), lovely (na.eh), lover (a.hav), to love (cha.vav), to love: friend (a.hev), to love: lover (a.hev)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Bowlby's attachment theory (*Attachment and Loss*, 1969–1980) remains the foundational account of love as an evolved bonding system for the protection of offspring and the maintenance of close relationships across the lifespan. Ainsworth's Strange Situation (1978) identified stable attachment patterns (secure, anxious, avoidant) that persist into adult relational style; Hazan and Shaver (1987) extended the framework to adult romantic love, showing that adult pair-bonds replicate infant attachment dynamics. Sternberg's triangular theory of love (1986) — intimacy, passion, commitment — and Hatfield's distinction between passionate and companionate love provide the standard taxonomy for differentiating the register captured by fileō (affectionate, friendship-adjacent love) from the more comprehensive and often commanded agapē. Harlow's contact-comfort research established that physical affection, not merely provision, is necessary for healthy attachment formation.

**Emergence — what causes love and affection to arise:**
Attachment bonds form through consistent caregiver responsiveness, proximity, and physical contact in infancy; adult love arises through initial attraction, repeated positive interaction, reciprocal self-disclosure, and the activation of the attachment system by the other's attentiveness. Oxytocin release through touch, eye contact, and social attunement provides the hormonal substrate for bonding. Filostorgos (natural family affection) and filadelfia (brotherly love) name love that arises from kinship or shared community rather than from romantic attraction — kin-selection theory in evolutionary biology (Hamilton) accounts for the strength and near-universality of this register independent of the reciprocity dynamics that govern non-kin relationships.

**Reduction and absence — what causes love and affection to diminish:**
Attachment bonds weaken through separation, repeated unresponsiveness or rejection, betrayal of trust, and sustained neglect. Adult romantic love diminishes through loss of intimacy, hedonic adaptation in the passion dimension, or erosion of commitment. Avoidant attachment represents a learned suppression of attachment needs following early rejection — love reduced not because the underlying capacity is absent but because it has been defensively inhibited. Family and community affection (filostorgos, filadelfia) can be undermined by prolonged conflict, but shows more resilience than romantic attachment because it is less contingent on ongoing positive exchange.

**Location — where love and affection are seated:**
Neurological, hormonal, emotional, cognitive, and behavioural — among the most multi-dimensional of the emotions. Neurologically, the opioid system (pleasure of proximity), oxytocin system (bonding), and dopamine system (reward) are all implicated. Emotionally it involves warmth, care, and longing; cognitively, attentiveness to and preoccupation with the beloved; behaviourally, proximity-seeking and protective action.

**External influences:**
*Hereditary:* Oxytocin receptor gene variants are associated with individual differences in affiliative bonding capacity; attachment style has a heritable temperamental component. *Parenting:* Quality of early caregiving is the primary shaper of attachment style, which in turn shapes the capacity for and pattern of adult love. *Cultural:* Norms for expressing affection, and the relative weighting of romantic versus familial/communal love, vary substantially across cultures; the biblical vocabulary spans both a Hebrew covenantal-affection register (a.ha.vah) and a differentiated Greek vocabulary (agapē, fileō, storgē-cognates) reflecting different cultural framings of love. *Circumstances:* Safety and mutual vulnerability support the development of love; chronic threat and instability undermine it.

**Coverage assessment:** Rich. Love and attachment are among the most extensively researched domains in developmental, social, and clinical psychology.

### Section 2 — Misdirected love and rivalrous attachment

**Primary field:** Motivational psychology / addiction and behavioural science

**Glosses covered:**
love of dispute (filoneikia), love of money (filarguria)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
These two glosses name love (fil-) directed toward an object other than a person — contention itself, and money. Behavioural addiction research (Griffiths' components model of addiction, 2005) treats compulsive pursuit of non-substance rewards (money, status, conflict/winning) as capable of producing the same motivational hijacking documented in substance addiction — salience, mood modification, tolerance, and conflict with other life domains. Materialism research (Kasser, *The High Price of Materialism*, 2002) documents that the prioritisation of money and possessions as life goals is robustly associated with lower wellbeing and relational quality, consistent with the cluster's implicit framing of these as disordered rather than neutral attachments. Contentiousness as a motivated preference for conflict (filoneikia — literally, friendship toward strife) has less direct psychological literature but overlaps with trait competitiveness and status-contest research.

**Emergence — what causes misdirected love to arise:**
Love of money arises when material security or status becomes a proxy for needs that are more fundamentally relational or existential (security, worth, control) — the reward value of money can come to substitute for these underlying needs, a substitution documented in terror-management and self-determination research. Love of dispute arises from a motivational orientation toward winning or dominance in social exchange that displaces the pursuit of relational good; it can be reinforced by early environments that reward contentiousness or model conflict as the primary mode of engagement.

**Reduction and absence:** Both reduce when the underlying substitute need (security, status, belonging) is met through healthier means, or when the person's value hierarchy is deliberately reordered — values-clarification interventions in the self-determination-theory literature reliably reduce materialistic orientation when they succeed in reconnecting behaviour to intrinsic goals.

**Location:** Motivational and characterological — these are misdirections of the same attachment/desire system described in Section 1, not a separate faculty. The cluster groups them under "love" precisely because they represent the same capacity for devoted attention and investment, aimed at objects incapable of receiving or reciprocating it as a person can.

**External influences:** Cultural valuation of wealth and status, family modelling of contentiousness or materialism, and economic insecurity are the principal external shapers documented in the literature.

**Coverage assessment:** Thin but genuine. These two glosses sit at the edge of love/attachment research proper and are better covered by materialism and behavioural-addiction research than by attachment theory directly.

### Section 3 — Total consecration and the ban (cherem)

**Primary field:** Anthropology of religion / ritual and sacrifice studies

**Glosses covered:**
devoted thing (che.rem), to devote/destroy (cha.ram)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Che.rem names a category with no close secular-psychological analogue: something (or someone) set apart irrevocably for God, which in its most severe biblical application meant total destruction as an offering that could not be reclaimed for ordinary use. Anthropological and religious-studies literature on sacrifice and taboo (Douglas, *Purity and Danger*, 1966; Girard's mimetic theory of sacred violence, *Violence and the Sacred*, 1972) addresses the general human phenomenon of setting objects, persons, or actions apart as sacred and therefore removed from ordinary transactional use — the closest available framework, though it describes the sociology and phenomenology of such practices rather than adjudicating their theological meaning.

**Emergence — what causes something to be set apart as cherem:**
In the biblical material this is an act of divine command or covenantal declaration, not a psychological or emergent human state — which is precisely why it sits outside ordinary "devotion" psychology. The nearest human-experience analogue is the psychology of irrevocable commitment (vows, oaths) discussed under M64 (Will & Resolve): a declared, binding removal of an object or person from ordinary disposal.

**Reduction and absence; Location; External influences:** Not meaningfully addressed by human science in the terms this cluster's other sections use — see the outside-science inventory below.

**Coverage assessment:** Very thin. This is a theological-covenantal category with only distant anthropological analogues; a short, honest treatment is preferred here over forcing a psychological frame onto it.

## Outside-science inventory

- **cherem (che.rem, cha.ram) as covenantal ban** — the sacred act of setting apart for destruction or exclusive divine use is a theological-covenantal category (holy war, tabernacle/temple dedication, the ban on Canaanite cities) with no meaningful equivalent in human science's account of love, devotion, or attachment. It is grouped in this cluster only because the English gloss "devoted" is shared with ordinary devotion vocabulary — a lexical rather than phenomenological connection, worth flagging explicitly for Session B.
- **agapē as commanded, unconditional love** — the specifically commanded quality of agapē (love owed to enemies, strangers, and those from whom no reciprocal benefit is expected) exceeds what attachment and evolutionary bonding theory can generate from their own explanatory resources, which predict in-group and reciprocity-conditioned attachment as the baseline.
- **love (a.ha.vah) applied to God, and to Israel as the object of God's love** — covenantal love with a specific divine subject and object is a theological category that science addresses only at the experiential edge (the human experience of being the object of steadfast love), not at the level of the claim itself.

**Observation:** M51's most significant analytical feature is that it yokes together two genuinely distinct phenomena under one English root ("love"/"devoted"): ordinary human attachment and affection (rich science coverage, Section 1), and covenantal total consecration (che.rem, almost no science coverage, Section 3). This divergence in coverage is itself a finding worth carrying into Session B — the cluster's own internal structure may need to register that these are not variations on a single scientific phenomenon.

## D9.1.1 / D9.2.1 / D11.2.1 / D12.1.1 prompt reference notes

**D9.1.1 — Physiological/neural substrate:** Section 1 (attachment/affection) has a well-documented substrate — oxytocin, vasopressin, the dopaminergic reward system, and opioid-mediated proximity pleasure. Section 2 (misdirected love) recruits the same reward circuitry redirected toward non-relational objects (money, winning), consistent with behavioural-addiction neuroscience. Section 3 (cherem) has no neural substrate in the literature — it is not a psychological state at all in the biblical material's own terms, but a covenantal-legal act.

**D9.2.1 — Generational transmission:** Attachment style, and the affectional patterns that flow from it, transmit generationally with well-documented fidelity — the intergenerational transmission of attachment is one of the most replicated findings in developmental psychology (parents' own attachment classifications predict their children's with above-chance accuracy). Materialistic and contentious orientations (Section 2) transmit through modelling and through shared economic/social circumstance rather than through a distinct heritable pathway. Cherem, as a covenantal-legal category, transmits (where it transmits at all) through community memory, narrative, and instruction rather than through any psychological mechanism.

**D11.2.1 — Innate endowment vs. acquired:** The capacity for attachment and affection is innate (a species-typical bonding system, per Bowlby and evolutionary attachment theory) while its specific objects, security, and expression are acquired through experience. Love of money and love of dispute are acquired substitute-attachments rather than innate orientations — they represent the innate capacity for devoted attention misdirected by acquired circumstance and habituation, not a separate innate drive.

**D12.1.1 — Social/behavioural manifestation:** Attachment and affection manifest behaviourally as proximity-seeking, self-disclosure, protective and caregiving action, and sustained preference for the beloved's company and wellbeing. Misdirected love manifests as disproportionate time, resource, and emotional investment in money or contention relative to persons. Cherem manifests, where it appears in the text, as an act of formal, communal, often irreversible removal of persons or objects from ordinary use — a behavioural category unlike anything else in this cluster, underscoring the Section 3 divergence noted above.
