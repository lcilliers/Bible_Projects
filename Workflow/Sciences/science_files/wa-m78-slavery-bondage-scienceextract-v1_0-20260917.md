## Purpose

This document is the cluster science review for M78. It is produced as a pre-analytical asset for the D9.1.1 / D9.2.1 / D11.2.1 / D12.1.1 catalogue prompts. It does not constitute the biblical analysis.

The live gloss list (22 terms, queried 2026-09-17) covers two registers: slavery/enslavement proper (doulos/doulē/douleia/douleuō/doulagōgeō family) and burden (a large Hebrew "burden" family — massa, sebel, and related terms, plus Greek baros/phortion). The slavery vocabulary is read here through the old M36 file's Section 2, which is reused and adapted below; the burden vocabulary is new content requiring its own section, since it names not captivity itself but the weight of what must be carried under compulsion or duty — related to but analytically distinct from M70's positive "support/bearing" vocabulary and worth cross-referencing there.

## Science sections

### Section 1 — Slavery, captivity, and coercive powerlessness

**Primary field:** Trauma psychology / social psychology

**Glosses covered:**
slave (G1401 doulos, G3610 oiketēs), female slave (G1399 doulē), slavery (G1397 douleia, H5659 avdut), be a slave (G1398 douleuō), to enslave (G1396 doulagōgeō, G1402 douloō, G2615 katadouloō), fellow slave (G4889 sundoulos)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
As documented in the old M36 file: van der Kolk's trauma research (*The Body Keeps the Score*, 2014) addresses the psychological effects of captivity and coercive control on the whole person, body included. Seligman's learned-helplessness framework applies directly to slavery contexts — exposure to inescapable, uncontrollable control produces passive, helpless, and cognitively impaired states that persist even after the controlling condition is removed. Herman's analysis of complex trauma (*Trauma and Recovery*, 1992) addresses the long-term psychological effects of sustained captivity and powerlessness, identifying identity disruption, chronic trauma responses, and — a particularly well-documented and psychologically significant finding — the tendency toward internalisation of the enslaver's own view of the enslaved person as a further, compounding injury beyond the physical bondage itself. Paul's deliberate appropriation of doulos as chosen identity ("bondservant of Christ Jesus") is a striking reversal that the science literature on coerced captivity, by its nature, has no direct parallel for — consent and chosen submission are definitionally excluded from what slavery research studies.

**Emergence — what causes the enslaved state to arise:**
Slavery as a social institution arises from the exercise of coercive power — one party using force, threat, or structural advantage to compel another's service without consent. The psychological experience of being enslaved arises from the combination of powerlessness, identity assault, and the sustained, ongoing threat of harm — the qualities that Section 1 of M01 identified as producing acute fear and, over time, learned helplessness and despair when the threatening force is inescapable.

**Reduction and absence — what causes the enslaved state to diminish:**
Liberation from coercive slavery requires the removal of the coercive power itself — internal psychological adaptation alone does not end slavery, since it is first and irreducibly a social-structural condition, not merely an inner state. Psychological recovery from slavery-related trauma follows the same general trajectory documented for complex trauma recovery more broadly: safety established first, then reconnection to trustworthy relationship, then integration of the experience into a coherent life narrative (Herman's three-stage model). Paul's theological "slavery to Christ" is presented as paradoxically the only genuine freedom, with slavery to sin as the alternative bondage that in fact produces death — a reversal the psychological literature on coerced captivity cannot itself generate, since it has no category for a form of "enslavement" that is simultaneously freedom.

**Location — where enslavement is seated:**
Coercive slavery produces effects across essentially every inner-being dimension the programme tracks — emotional (chronic fear, shame, grief), cognitive (restricted agency, disrupted self-concept), relational (damaged trust, disrupted attachment), and somatic (chronic stress physiology, the HPA-axis dysregulation documented across M01 and M73). It is arguably the most comprehensive single inner-being assault named anywhere in the programme's vocabulary, consistent with the old M36 file's own assessment.

**External influences:**
*Social and structural:* Slavery is entirely a social institution — it arises from structural power arrangements, not from any individual characteristic of the enslaved person. *Historical:* The biblical world was pervasively shaped by slavery as a normal social reality across multiple, quite different legal forms (debt-slavery, chattel slavery, war captivity), and the cluster's vocabulary must be read against that varied historical background rather than a single modern conception of slavery.

**Coverage assessment:** Moderate to good. Trauma psychology and social psychology of coercive control provide substantial, directly relevant coverage; the theological reappropriation of slavery language (doulos of Christ) is entirely outside this literature's reach.

### Section 2 — Burden: the psychology of imposed weight and load

**Primary field:** Occupational and health psychology — workload and allostatic load research

**Glosses covered:**
burden (G0922 baros, G5413 phortion, H0405 aramaic massa-related, H2960 tirchah, H3053 yehav, H4614 ma'amasah, H4853A massa, H5385 nesu'ah, H5447/H5448/H5449/H5450 sebel family)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
The sheer number of distinct Hebrew terms for "burden" in this section (a substantially larger dedicated vocabulary than most comparable concepts in the corpus) is itself an analytically notable finding, worth carrying forward to Session B — the biblical languages distinguish burden by cause, size, and register (forced labour's sebel, a general load's massa, a felt heaviness's ma'amasah) more granularly than English's single word. McEwen's allostatic load framework (*New England Journal of Medicine*, 1998) provides the primary physiological correlate: sustained demand on the body's stress-response systems, without adequate recovery, produces a cumulative "wear and tear" (allostatic load) that is functionally the biological instantiation of what the burden vocabulary names — a weight that, if not periodically relieved, damages the systems bearing it. Maslach's burnout research (*Journal of Occupational Behavior*, 1981, and the extensive subsequent programme) documents the specific psychological syndrome (exhaustion, cynicism, reduced efficacy) produced by chronic, excessive occupational or caregiving load — directly relevant to sebel's specific historical sense of imposed forced-labour burden (the term used of Israel's burden under Egyptian and later corvée labour). Schnall, Zadra and Proffitt's embodied-cognition research (*Journal of Experimental Social Psychology*, 2010 and related studies) found that people under psychological burden (sadness, isolation, fatigue) literally perceive physical inclines and distances as steeper and longer — evidence that the "weight" metaphor for psychological burden is not merely figurative but reflects a genuine perceptual-cognitive interaction between felt inner state and embodied spatial cognition, directly relevant to why "burden" language is such a natural and non-arbitrary vehicle for describing oppressive inner experience.

**Emergence — what causes burden to arise:**
Burden arises when demand — physical labour, responsibility, care for others, moral or emotional weight — exceeds, or threatens to exceed, the resources and recovery time available to bear it. Imposed burden (sebel, the forced-labour sense) differs psychologically from chosen burden (a freely accepted responsibility) even where the objective physical load is identical — perceived control over whether and how a load is carried is among the strongest predictors of whether the same objective demand produces manageable effort or damaging strain (Karasek's demand-control model of occupational stress).

**Reduction and absence — what causes burden to diminish:**
Rest and recovery periods (McEwen's allostatic-load framework identifies adequate recovery, not merely reduced demand, as the operative variable), the sharing of load across multiple bearers (directly relevant to M70's positive support vocabulary as burden's counterpart), and increased perceived control over the burden all reduce its damaging effect. The removal of the burden altogether, where the burden is unjustly imposed, is the structural (rather than merely coping-level) resolution — psychological adaptation to an unjust burden is not the same as its removal, a distinction with direct relevance to how the biblical corpus treats forced labour and oppression as conditions to be ended, not merely endured better.

**Location — where burden is seated:**
Simultaneously physical (the literal load, where applicable), physiological (allostatic load on the stress-response system), cognitive (the appraisal of demand against resource), and — per the embodied-cognition research — perceptual, in a genuinely measurable way that blurs the line between "merely metaphorical" and "physically real" burden.

**External influences:**
*Social and structural:* Whether burden is justly or unjustly imposed, and whether it can be shared, are primarily structural/relational questions rather than individual-psychological ones. *Circumstances:* Sustained high demand without relief (the corvée-labour pattern) is the clearest and most severe form. *Developmental:* Children who bear inappropriate caregiving or responsibility burdens too early (parentification, in the family-systems literature) show measurable long-term effects on adult stress-response calibration.

**Coverage assessment:** Good. Occupational health psychology, allostatic-load physiology, and embodied-cognition research together provide strong, multi-angle coverage.

## Outside-science inventory

- **slavery to God as a chosen theological identity** — the Pauline use of doulos as a voluntary, honoured status (in contrast to every psychological finding about coerced captivity, which by definition excludes consent) is a theological category with no analogue in slavery/trauma research.
- **"my burden is light" (Matt 11:30)** — Christ's yoke as a burden that, paradoxically, does not produce allostatic damage is a theological claim about a qualitatively different kind of load-bearing relationship, exceeding the occupational-burden literature's descriptive scope.
- **liberation from slavery to sin/death as a theological category** — the Pauline framework in which slavery to sin, not merely social captivity, is the deeper bondage requiring a specifically redemptive (not merely social-structural) liberation is outside science's reach.
- **corporate/covenantal bondage (Israel's slavery in Egypt as formative covenant memory)** — the theological significance of the Exodus as paradigmatic liberation, invoked throughout the canon as the ground of covenant obligation, exceeds what trauma and burden psychology alone address.

**Observation:** As with the old M36 file's assessment, the theological reappropriation of slavery language (Paul as doulos of Christ) is the single largest point at which this cluster's content departs entirely from what slavery and trauma research can address — the same word family names the worst documented human condition and, in reappropriated form, a chosen relationship of honour, and the verse evidence will need to carry that distinction carefully.

## Catalogue prompt reference notes (D9.1.1 / D9.2.1 / D11.2.1 / D12.1.1)

**D9.1.1 — Physiological/neural substrate:** Coerced slavery/captivity produces well-documented HPA-axis dysregulation and complex-trauma neurobiological changes (van der Kolk); burden specifically maps onto McEwen's allostatic-load framework — cumulative physiological wear from sustained demand without adequate recovery, plus the Schnall et al. embodied-perceptual finding that psychological burden measurably alters spatial/effort perception.

**D9.2.1 — Generational transmission:** Complex trauma from captivity/enslavement shows documented intergenerational transmission (epigenetic and attachment-mediated pathways, paralleling the ACEs research cited across M01/M24/M73); burden imposed on children (parentification) likewise shows measurable transmission into adult stress-response patterns.

**D11.2.1 — Innate endowment vs. acquired:** Both slavery and burden are, by definition, imposed/acquired conditions rather than innate ones — no person is born enslaved or burdened in the psychological sense the cluster addresses; the underlying stress-response and threat/control appraisal systems that determine how the imposed condition is experienced are, however, part of innate human physiology (shared substantially with M01's threat-response architecture).

**D12.1.1 — Social/behavioural manifestation:** Overwhelmingly social-structural in origin (both slavery and imposed burden arise from the actions of other people or systems, not from the bearer's own disposition) while producing intensely internal, whole-person effects — this cluster is a clear case, alongside M01 Section 5 and M72 Section 3, where the programme's finding-level analysis needs to track both the external imposing relationship and the internal inner-being consequence as distinct but linked data.
