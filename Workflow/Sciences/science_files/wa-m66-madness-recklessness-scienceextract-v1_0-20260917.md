## Science sections

### Section 1 — Madness, mania, and psychotic states

**Primary field:** Clinical psychology / psychiatry

**Glosses covered:**
be insane (parafroneō), insanity (mania), insanity (parafronia), to rave (mainomai), be mad (sha.ga), madness (ho.le.lah), madness (ho.le.lut), madness (shig.ga.on)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Psychiatry addresses the clinical conditions that produce loss of rational self-direction. Mania (DSM-5, bipolar I disorder — manic episode) is characterised by elevated or irritable mood, decreased need for sleep, grandiosity, racing thoughts, increased goal-directed activity, and impulsive behaviour. Psychosis involves loss of contact with shared reality — hallucinations, delusions, disorganised thinking. Twin and family studies place bipolar disorder's heritability at roughly 60–80% concordance in identical twins, and schizophrenia-spectrum psychosis at roughly 80% heritability, making this cluster's clinical register among the most strongly genetically loaded conditions in psychiatry. The Greek mania and Hebrew shig.ga.on carry both a clinical sense and a rhetorical-social sense — sometimes naming genuine mental disturbance, sometimes naming behaviour that a community judges as violating the bounds of rational, acceptable conduct without implying actual clinical disturbance. Session B should test which register is operative in each occurrence rather than assume clinical psychosis is always in view.

**Emergence — what causes madness/psychosis to arise:**
Clinical mania arises from genetic predisposition, sleep disruption, substance use, and — in first episodes especially — significant life stress acting on that predisposition. Psychosis arises from genetic vulnerability interacting with environmental triggers, including cannabis use (particularly high-THC preparations) in genetically susceptible individuals, and acute overwhelming stress in reactive psychosis. The rhetorical/hyperbolic uses of the vocabulary instead name behaviour judged by a community's standards as violating rational, expected conduct — a social-evaluative phenomenon rather than a clinical one.

**Reduction and absence — what causes madness/psychosis to diminish:**
Clinical mania and psychosis respond, for many patients, to pharmacotherapy (mood stabilisers, antipsychotics), alongside regular sleep, reduced substance use, and social support as behavioural moderators. In the rhetorical sense, the "mad" label ceases to apply once the community judges the person's conduct to have returned within the bounds it recognises as rational.

**Location — where madness is seated:**
For clinical conditions: neurological, in the dysregulation of dopaminergic, serotonergic, and noradrenergic neurotransmitter systems and in documented structural and functional brain changes associated with psychosis and mania. For rhetorical uses: social, located in a community's evaluative judgment of behaviour rather than in any internal clinical state of the person so labelled.

**External influences:**
*Hereditary:* The clinical conditions named in this section carry among the highest heritabilities of any psychiatric conditions. *Developmental:* Adverse childhood experiences increase psychosis risk in genetically vulnerable individuals. *Substances:* Cannabis, particularly high-potency preparations, significantly increases psychosis risk in vulnerable individuals. *Social/cultural:* The definition of, and social response to, "madness" is itself culturally shaped — behaviour one community labels mad another may frame quite differently, which bears directly on the rhetorical-register uses of this section's vocabulary.

**Coverage assessment:** Good for the clinical conditions; the rhetorical uses of the vocabulary are, by their nature, less about a clinical state than about a community's own social-evaluative judgment, and are largely addressed from the biblical pass rather than the clinical literature.

### Section 2 — Recklessness and impulsivity

**Primary field:** Personality and developmental psychology — impulse-control research

**Glosses covered:**
reckless (propetēs), be reckless (pa.chaz), recklessness (pa.chaz), recklessness (pa.cha.zut)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Impulsivity research (Eysenck's early trait work; Dickman's distinction between functional and dysfunctional impulsivity, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 1990) treats recklessness as a failure of inhibitory control — the override of deliberative judgment by an immediate action-tendency. Adolescent risk-taking research (Steinberg's dual-systems model, *Developmental Review*, 2008) documents that the brain's reward-sensitivity system matures earlier than its prefrontal regulatory system, producing a developmentally normal window of elevated impulsivity and risk-taking in adolescence and early adulthood, distinct from a stable adult trait of recklessness. Propetēs (literally "falling forward" — headlong, precipitate) captures exactly this failure-of-restraint quality: action that outruns the deliberative process that would ordinarily check it.

**Emergence — what causes recklessness to arise:**
Recklessness arises from weak inhibitory control (whether developmentally normal, as in adolescence, or a more stable trait pattern in adulthood), from reward-sensitivity that outweighs risk-assessment in the moment of decision, and from situational factors (time pressure, emotional arousal, peer presence) that are well documented to further degrade deliberative restraint. Dysfunctional impulsivity (Dickman) is distinguished from functional impulsivity (quick, effective action in contexts that genuinely reward speed over deliberation) precisely by whether the situation actually called for restraint that was overridden.

**Reduction and absence — what causes recklessness to diminish:**
Prefrontal-cortex maturation (the normal developmental trajectory through the mid-twenties) reduces adolescent-typical impulsivity; for stable adult trait impulsivity, cognitive-behavioural interventions that build deliberate pause-and-evaluate habits, and environments that reduce the situational triggers of impulsive action (time pressure, high arousal, peer instigation), are the primary documented moderators.

**Location — where recklessness is seated:**
Neurocognitive (the balance of reward-sensitivity and prefrontal inhibitory control) and behavioural (action taken without adequate deliberation) — a specific failure mode within the broader executive-function territory addressed under M63 and M64, rather than a separate faculty.

**External influences:**
*Developmental:* Adolescence carries a well-documented, biologically-grounded window of elevated impulsivity independent of character; this is worth distinguishing from settled adult recklessness as a character trait in Session B. *Hereditary:* Impulsivity and risk-tolerance have documented heritable components. *Social:* Peer presence measurably increases risk-taking and reckless behaviour beyond what the same individual shows alone (Steinberg's peer-effect findings).

**Coverage assessment:** Good. Impulse-control and adolescent risk-taking research provide strong, directly applicable coverage.

### Section 3 — Fantasies and mental wandering

**Primary field:** Cognitive psychology — default mode network and mind-wandering research

**Glosses covered:**
fantasies (har.hor)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Killingsworth and Gilbert's mind-wandering research (*Science*, 2010 — "A Wandering Mind Is an Unhappy Mind") found that the mind wanders from the task at hand roughly 47% of waking time, and that this wandering is associated with lower momentary happiness regardless of the content of the wandering thought. Default-mode-network research (Raichle and colleagues) identifies the specific brain network active during unstructured, internally-directed thought — daydreaming, rumination, and undirected fantasy all draw on this same network, active precisely when goal-directed external attention is disengaged. Har.hor (uncontrolled mental imagining, agitated fantasy — used in Daniel of Nebuchadnezzar's disturbed thoughts) names a specific, often distressing register of this general mind-wandering capacity rather than neutral daydreaming.

**Emergence — what causes disturbed fantasy/mental wandering to arise:**
Mind-wandering arises naturally whenever external, goal-directed attentional demand is low, engaging the default mode network by default. Har.hor's more agitated, disturbed register specifically suggests wandering thought that has become distressing or uncontrolled rather than benign — closer to intrusive or ruminative cognition (well documented as a feature of anxiety and depression) than to ordinary pleasant daydreaming.

**Reduction and absence:** Mind-wandering reduces under conditions of high, engaging external attentional demand and increases under boredom, fatigue, or emotional distress. Where it has become distressing and intrusive rather than benign, the same interventions effective for rumination generally (attentional retraining, mindfulness-based approaches) are documented to help.

**Location:** Cognitive — located in the default mode network's characteristic activity pattern, engaged whenever directed external attention disengages.

**External influences:** Sleep quality, stress level, and the presence or absence of engaging external demands are the primary documented situational shapers of both the frequency and the distressing or benign character of mind-wandering episodes.

**Coverage assessment:** Thin. A single gloss; default-mode-network and mind-wandering research provide a genuine but general frame rather than content specific to this term.

## Outside-science inventory

- **madness (shig.ga.on) as covenant curse** — where shig.ga.on appears among the covenant curses (Deuteronomy 28) as a divinely imposed judgment for covenant unfaithfulness, the theological claim of divine judicial action exceeds clinical psychiatry's descriptive account of mania and psychosis as naturally-caused conditions.
- **madness attributed as a false accusation against righteous or prophetic conduct** — where the "mad" label is applied polemically to prophetic or faithful conduct that a hostile community judges irrational (a documented pattern in both testaments), the theological point being made — that the world's judgment of madness can itself be a form of blindness to what is actually right — is a matter for the biblical pass, not psychiatry, which can only describe the social-evaluative mechanism, not adjudicate whose judgment is correct.

**Observation:** M66 has good coverage for its two larger sections (clinical madness and recklessness) and thin, general coverage for the single-gloss mind-wandering section. As with the earlier M16 review's Section 2, careful attention in Session B to which register (clinical, rhetorical, or theological-judicial) a given occurrence of the madness vocabulary is drawing on will be important, since the science coverage differs sharply across these registers.

## D9.1.1 / D9.2.1 / D11.2.1 / D12.1.1 prompt reference notes

**D9.1.1 — Physiological/neural substrate:** Clinical mania and psychosis have a well-documented neural substrate (dopaminergic, serotonergic, and noradrenergic dysregulation; structural and functional brain changes). Recklessness/impulsivity is grounded in the balance between reward-sensitive and prefrontal-regulatory neural systems. Mind-wandering/fantasy engages the default mode network specifically.

**D9.2.1 — Generational transmission:** Clinical mania and psychosis show strong documented heritable/generational transmission (60–80% twin concordance for bipolar disorder, roughly 80% for schizophrenia-spectrum conditions) — among the highest of any conditions addressed across this whole batch of reviews. Recklessness/impulsivity shows moderate heritable transmission via temperament. Mind-wandering has no documented generational-transmission pattern distinct from general cognitive-style inheritance.

**D11.2.1 — Innate endowment vs. acquired:** Clinical mania and psychosis are substantially innate/heritable in their vulnerability, with environmental factors (stress, substance use) acting as triggers on that innate predisposition rather than as sole causes. Impulsivity/recklessness has a genuine heritable trait component but is also strongly age-graded (developmentally normal in adolescence, per Steinberg) and situationally modulated. Mind-wandering is a universal, innate feature of human cognition (default mode network activity) rather than an acquired trait, though its distressing or benign character is shaped by acquired emotional state.

**D12.1.1 — Social/behavioural manifestation:** Clinical madness manifests as observable disorganised thought, mood elevation or disturbance, and impaired reality-testing, clinically distinguishable from culturally-judged "irrational" but non-clinical conduct. Recklessness manifests as action taken without proportionate deliberation, often but not always followed by regret once deliberative capacity re-engages. Mind-wandering/fantasy manifests internally and is behaviourally detectable chiefly through reduced task engagement and, where distressing, through visible agitation.
