## Science sections

### Section 1 — Unbelief and disbelief

**Primary field:** Psychology of religion

**Glosses covered:**
to disbelieve (apisteō), unbelief (apistia), unbelieving (apistos), faithlessness (H4878)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Exline's research on spiritual struggle (*Journal for the Scientific Study of Religion*) is the most directly relevant body of work — the empirical finding that spiritual struggle (wrestling with God, doubt, anger at God) is common, often painful, and does not automatically resolve toward either faith or unbelief. Batson's quest orientation (distinguished from extrinsic and intrinsic religiosity) describes an open, questioning relationship to religious belief that includes sustained doubt as a stable stance rather than a transitional state. Hunsberger and Altemeyer's research on committed unbelief (*Atheists: A Groundbreaking Study of America's Nonbelievers*, 2006) documents unbelief as itself a stable, reasoned, often deeply held position rather than merely an absence of belief.

**Emergence — what causes unbelief and disbelief to arise:**
Disbelief arises through several documented pathways: intellectual objection (perceived incoherence or implausibility of religious claims), negative religious experience (unanswered prayer, perceived divine absence or abandonment, harm experienced within a religious community), gradual disengagement through reduced practice and declining social reinforcement, and, less commonly, a considered philosophical rejection reached through sustained reflection rather than crisis. Faithlessness in its more relational sense (the failure to trust or remain loyal to a prior commitment) is documented in attachment and commitment research as arising from repeated disappointment or betrayal by the trusted party.

**Reduction and absence — what causes unbelief to diminish:**
Unbelief is reduced, per the psychology of religious conversion literature, by positive relational encounter within a believing community, by resolution of the specific intellectual or experiential objection that produced it, and — in Exline's spiritual struggle research — by finding a framework that can hold the struggle without requiring its premature resolution. Committed, considered unbelief (as distinct from transitional doubt) is comparatively stable and, per Hunsberger and Altemeyer, is not reliably "reduced" by argument alone.

**Location — where unbelief is seated:**
Cognitive (the assessment that a claim is false or unsupported), emotional (the pain or relief that frequently accompanies both the struggle toward and the settling into unbelief), and social (unbelief is powerfully shaped by, and shapes, one's belonging within or exit from a religious community). It is not a single faculty's operation but, like faith itself, a whole-person orientation.

**External influences:**
*Developmental:* Absence of childhood religious formation is among the strongest predictors of adult non-affiliation. *Social:* Loss of religious community membership is closely associated with the consolidation of unbelief; community re-entry is correspondingly associated with its reversal. *Circumstances:* Unresolved suffering, perceived divine failure to intervene, and negative experience within a religious institution are the most consistently documented situational triggers.

**Coverage assessment:** Moderate. Psychology of religion has developed a genuine literature on doubt, spiritual struggle, and committed unbelief, though it is smaller than the literature on positive religious belief (now M13's territory).

### Section 2 — Religion as (self-made) practice

**Primary field:** Psychology of religion / sociology of religion

**Glosses covered:**
religion (thrēskeia, G1175), self-made religion (ethelothrēskia, G1479)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Batson, Schoenrade and Ventis's distinction between intrinsic religiosity (religion as an internalised end in itself) and extrinsic religiosity (religion as a means to social, psychological, or status ends) is the foundational framework for distinguishing genuine religious orientation from religion practised for other ends. Allport's original formulation of this distinction (1950s–60s) already anticipated the New Testament's own distinction between thrēskeia as outward religious practice and ethelothrēskia — self-devised, self-directed religious observance not answerable to genuine relationship with God. Sociology of religion's ritual studies (Durkheim's foundational account of religion as social solidarity-generating practice) addresses religious observance as a communal, patterned behaviour independent of the practitioner's inner belief state — directly relevant to a term (thrēskeia) that names the outward practice rather than the inner disposition.

**Emergence — what causes religious practice (including self-made religion) to arise:**
Outward religious practice arises from socialisation into a religious community's patterns, from the human tendency to ritualise significant beliefs and commitments (Durkheim), and — for self-devised religious practice specifically — from the desire to construct a system of observance that feels appropriately rigorous or effective without submitting to an external authority's actual terms. Extrinsic religiosity (Batson) arises when religious practice is adopted instrumentally, for social belonging, status, or psychological comfort, rather than as a genuine response to the object of belief.

**Reduction and absence — what causes religious practice to diminish or be exposed as self-made:**
Extrinsic or self-devised religious practice is documented to be comparatively fragile — it diminishes when its instrumental payoff (social approval, psychological comfort) is no longer delivered, or when it is confronted with a standard of genuine relational accountability it was constructed to avoid. Intrinsic religious orientation, by contrast, is more stable under adversity precisely because it is not contingent on the practice's secondary benefits.

**Location — where religious practice is seated:**
Primarily behavioural and social — thrēskeia names an observable, patterned outward practice, not an inner state per se. Self-made religion (ethelothrēskia) adds a volitional dimension: the deliberate construction of one's own terms of observance, located in the will's choice of what to submit to.

**External influences:**
*Social and cultural:* The form outward religious practice takes is entirely culturally shaped, though the underlying tendency to ritualise significant commitments appears to be a human universal (Durkheim). *Circumstances:* Communities under stress or seeking control over uncertain circumstances show elevated rates of constructed, self-devised ritual practice — a documented pattern in the anthropology of magic and ritual under uncertainty (Malinowski).

**Coverage assessment:** Thin. This is a small, narrowly defined section; sociology and psychology of religion provide a genuine but not extensive framework for the intrinsic/extrinsic and authentic/self-devised distinctions the two glosses name.

## Outside-science inventory

- **unbelief (apistia) as a moral/spiritual condition** — not merely cognitive doubt but, in its fullest biblical register, the settled refusal of the covenant God — is a theological category that exceeds the psychology of religious doubt described in Section 1.
- **self-made religion (ethelothrēskia)** in its fullest theological register — religious observance that substitutes human invention for genuine submission to God — names a specifically theological failure (worship offered on one's own terms rather than God's) that sociology of religion can describe only from the outside, as a pattern of behaviour, not evaluate as such.
- **faithlessness (H4878)** where it names covenant breach specifically — Israel's unfaithfulness to YHWH as covenant partner — is a theological category of relational betrayal within a specific covenant structure, not merely the psychological phenomenon of broken trust.

**Observation:** M31 is now the thinnest cluster in this batch, and deliberately so — with its positive faith content relocated to M13, what remains is a tightly-scoped pairing of unbelief and (self-made) religious practice. Both retain genuine, if modest, science coverage; the theological distinctive in both cases is the specifically covenantal object being refused or substituted for — a structure sociology and psychology of religion can observe from outside but not adjudicate.

## D9/D11/D12 prompt reference notes

> These four catalogue questions (`D9.1.1`, `D9.2.1`, `D11.2.1`, `D12.1.1`) replace the retired `T7.3` prompt as of the 2026-09-17 catalogue realignment (`catalogue_version='v2-dimension-realign-20260917'`). Each is answered here from this document's science sections as the pre-analytical data input; the verse-evidence side of each question is completed at the characteristic-level answer stage, not here.

**D9.1.1 — Physiological/Neural Substrate:** Thin. Neither unbelief nor self-made religious practice has a documented distinct physiological or neural substrate in the literature; both are better characterised as cognitive-evaluative and social-behavioural phenomena than as arousal or embodied states. (Contrast M13, where positive faith/trust does connect to attachment-system neurobiology.)

**D9.2.1 — Generational Transmission:** Well documented for the absence side: lack of childhood religious formation is among the strongest predictors of adult non-affiliation and unbelief, making non-transmission itself a generationally patterned phenomenon. Self-made religious practice is not specifically studied as generationally transmitted, though ritual innovation under communal stress (Malinowski) can become entrenched and passed down within a group.

**D11.2.1 — Innate Endowment:** Not supported. Neither unbelief nor self-devised religious practice is treated in the literature as an innate endowment; both are constructed through socialisation, experience (including negative religious experience), and, in the case of self-made religion, deliberate human invention — bearing on D11.1 as evidence against an innate-endowment reading and toward a fallen-condition/constructed-response reading.

**D12.1.1 — Social/Behavioural Manifestation:** Mixed. Unbelief manifests both privately (an internal cognitive-emotional stance) and socially (community exit, public identification as non-religious). Self-made religion is inherently social/behavioural — thrēskeia by definition names an outward, observable practice — but is neither clearly cooperative nor competitive in the behavioural-science sense; it is better described as self-directed substitution than as either prosocial or adversarial behaviour.
