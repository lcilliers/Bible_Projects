## Science sections

### Section 1 — Will, volition, and intentional resolve

**Primary field:** Cognitive psychology / philosophy of action

**Glosses covered:**
self-chosen (authairetos), intention (ye.tser), to will (tse.va)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Heckhausen and Gollwitzer's Rubicon model of action phases (*American Psychologist*, 1987) distinguishes the motivational phase (deliberating between options) from the volitional phase (planning and executing an intention already formed) — once an intention is formed, a qualitatively different, more committed motivational state takes over. Libet's neuroscience experiments (*Brain*, 1983) on the timing of neural preparation relative to conscious awareness of intention remain a contested but influential reference point in free-will and volition research. Ye.tser (inner inclination or impulse — developed in rabbinic thought into the dual "good inclination/evil inclination" framework) is psychology's nearest Hebrew analogue to a pre-deliberate motivational orientation — dual-process motivation theory (Evans, Kahneman) similarly distinguishes automatic, appetitive processing from deliberate, value-governed processing, though without the rabbinic tradition's explicitly moral framing of the two inclinations. Authairetos (self-chosen, of one's own accord) names specifically voluntary, non-coerced willing, a distinction central to self-determination theory's contrast between autonomous and controlled motivation (Deci and Ryan).

**Emergence — what causes willing and resolve to arise:**
Willing arises when a potential course of action is evaluated positively and a settled intention is formed to pursue it — the Rubicon model's key finding is that this crossing point produces a genuinely different psychological state (more committed, more resistant to distraction) than mere preference or deliberation. Ye.tser as pre-deliberate inclination arises from the interaction of temperament, habituated pattern, and situational cue, operating prior to and shaping conscious deliberation rather than being identical with it.

**Reduction and absence — what causes will/resolve to fail or weaken:**
Resolve weakens when the evaluation of the intended action turns negative, when competing intentions gain priority, when self-regulatory resources are depleted (ego-depletion research, though now contested in its strongest form, still documents real limits on sustained effortful self-direction), or through akrasia (weakness of will — acting against one's own better, formed judgment). Coercion converts willing action into reluctant compliance and measurably eliminates the distinct psychological quality (commitment, persistence) that authairetos-type willing carries.

**Location — where will and resolve are seated:**
Primarily cognitive and volitional, with a genuine affective dimension — the prefrontal cortex is the primary neural site of intentional planning and inhibitory control, but resolve is experienced as an inner, felt directedness of the self, not merely a computation. Ye.tser locates something deeper still: a pre-deliberate motivational orientation from which conscious willing itself arises.

**External influences:**
*Social:* Willingness is shaped by relational context — people are measurably more willing to act, and sustain that willingness longer, when they feel genuinely respected and supported rather than coerced. *Developmental:* Executive-function capacity underlying deliberate volition develops through childhood and adolescence. *Cultural:* The relative value placed on autonomous willing versus obedient following varies across cultures (compare M54's obedience material).

**Coverage assessment:** Moderate. Volitional psychology and dual-process motivation theory provide a solid framework; ye.tser as the deepest pre-deliberate motivational orientation is only partially reachable by any secular framework, a finding the earlier M29 review also noted.

### Section 2 — Planning and devising

**Primary field:** Cognitive and motivational psychology — goal-setting and prospective cognition

**Glosses covered:**
plan (boulē), plan (boulēma), purpose (prothesis), purpose (fronēma), to plan (bouleuō), to plan (boulomai), to plan/present (protithēmi), plan (za.mam), to devise (ba.da), to devise: count (cha.shav), to devise: design (cha.shav), to devise: devise (cha.shav), to plan (uts), to plan (a.shit)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Locke and Latham's goal-setting theory (*A Theory of Goal Setting and Task Performance*, 1990) remains among the most extensively validated findings in organisational psychology: specific, challenging goals reliably outperform vague or easy ones in motivating sustained performance. Gollwitzer's implementation-intention research demonstrates that forming specific if-then plans dramatically increases follow-through relative to a mere intention to act. Szpunar's prospective-cognition research documents the cognitive capacity for mental time-travel into not-yet-existing future states, which underlies all planning. Cha.shav (to devise, plan, reckon, or design — a term whose semantic range runs from neutral calculation to scheming) is the largest single root in this section and carries a notably morally ambivalent range in Hebrew, capable of naming both wise design and malicious plotting — a range worth testing carefully in the biblical pass rather than assuming a uniformly positive or negative valence. Purpose (prothesis, fronēma) names a more comprehensive, settled orientation of the whole person toward their telos, of which specific plans are particular expressions.

**Emergence — what causes planning and devising to arise:**
Planning arises from identifying a valued but not-yet-achieved goal, the cognitive capacity to represent the future state and the path toward it, and sufficient motivation to invest effort in pursuit. Devising in cha.shav's morally ambivalent sense can arise either from constructive problem-solving orientation or from a hostile, calculating orientation toward another's harm — the same underlying cognitive machinery (prospective representation, means-end reasoning) applied to different ends.

**Reduction and absence — what causes planning to fail:**
Planning fails through absent clear values that would motivate goal pursuit, executive-function deficits, and — the single most robust finding in implementation-intention research — the failure to specify when, where, and how an intended action will actually occur, leaving a wanted outcome without an executable plan.

**Location — where planning is seated:**
Primarily prefrontal and cognitive — planning is a paradigmatic executive function dependent on working memory, prospective representation, and inhibitory control. Purpose (fronēma) extends beyond discrete planning to the whole-person orientation (values and identity) that determines which goals are judged worth pursuing in the first place.

**External influences:**
*Mentorship and counsel:* Social input measurably improves decision and plan quality (see M63 Section 2's advice-taking research); the wisdom literature's consistent commendation of seeking counsel before finalising plans is empirically well-supported. *Cultural:* Future-time-orientation (Zimbardo and Boyd's time-perspective research) varies across cultures and predicts systematic-planning tendency. *Developmental:* Planning capacity matures through adolescence and early adulthood alongside prefrontal-cortex development.

**Coverage assessment:** Good. Goal-setting and prospective-cognition research provide strong, directly applicable coverage.

### Section 3 — Choice and selection

**Primary field:** Decision psychology / cognitive psychology

**Glosses covered:**
to choose (haireō), to choose (hairetizō), to choose (procheirotoneō), chosen (ba.chir), chosen (qa.ri), to choose (ba.char)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Kahneman's *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (2011) and Ariely's *Predictably Irrational* (2008) document the systematic, often non-rational ways human preference is formed and expressed in choice behaviour. Schwartz's paradox-of-choice research (*The Paradox of Choice*, 2004) demonstrates that an excess of available options can reduce, rather than increase, satisfaction with the option eventually chosen — relevant background for reading the biblical material's own contrasting emphasis on clear, decisive, often binary choice (a covenant path chosen among a small, well-defined set of alternatives, as in Deuteronomy's "choose life") against a modern choice-environment of near-unlimited options. Chosen (ba.chir, qa.ri) in its biblical register frequently names not a human act of preference-selection at all but the state of having been selected by another (typically God) — a distinct and directionally reversed sense from ordinary choice psychology, which studies the chooser rather than the chosen.

**Emergence — what causes deliberate choice to arise:**
Choice arises from the evaluation of available alternatives against a value structure, weighted (per behavioural-economics research) by framing, loss aversion, and present-bias effects that make human choice reliably depart from the fully rational-actor model assumed by classical decision theory. Ba.char's directness (a decisive selecting-out of one option from a set, often in covenantal contrast — choosing God/the path of life over the alternative) fits closely with binary-choice research showing that clearly framed, limited-option decisions are made with greater confidence and follow-through than decisions amid excessive option sets.

**Reduction and absence — what causes choice to fail or be avoided:**
Choice paralysis increases with option overload (Schwartz) and with high perceived stakes absent adequate information. Choice avoidance (deferring or refusing to choose) is itself a well-documented decision-psychology phenomenon, distinct from either choosing well or choosing badly.

**Location — where choice is seated:**
Cognitive and volitional — the evaluative-comparative process itself is cognitive; the resulting commitment to one alternative over others is volitional, converging with Section 1's account of resolve once a choice has actually been made (per the Rubicon model).

**External influences:** *Structural:* The number and framing of available options substantially shapes decision quality and satisfaction (Schwartz, Kahneman and Tversky's framing research). *Cultural:* The prevailing choice architecture of a society (number of live options, degree of individual versus communal decision-authority) shapes how choice is experienced and exercised.

**Coverage assessment:** Good for the mechanics of choice; the biblical "chosen by God" sense (ba.chir, qa.ri) is a distinct, largely outside-science category noted below.

### Section 4 — Vowing and dedication

**Primary field:** Commitment-device psychology / behavioural economics

**Glosses covered:**
to consecrate: dedicate (qa.dash), to dedicate (na.zar)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Ariely and Wertenbroch's commitment-device research (*Psychological Science*, 2002) documents that binding oneself in advance to a future course of action — a deliberate self-imposed constraint, publicly declared and costly to break — is a genuinely effective strategy for overcoming predictable future weakness of will, precisely because it removes the option of later, weaker-willed reconsideration. Vows and dedications (na.zar — the Nazirite vow being the paradigm case) function psychologically as commitment devices of exactly this kind: a present, strong-willed self binds a future, potentially weaker-willed self to a specific course. Public, ritually-marked commitment (research on public versus private goal declaration — Gollwitzer's own later work found public commitment can cut either way, sometimes producing premature satisfaction rather than follow-through, depending on whether the declaration is treated as identity-confirming in itself or genuinely binding) adds a further social-accountability dimension not present in a purely private resolution.

**Emergence — what causes vowing/dedication to arise:**
A vow arises when a person anticipates their own future wavering and deliberately constructs a binding commitment — often public, often costly to break — to guard against it, or when a person seeks to formally and irrevocably set something or someone apart for a sacred purpose. The qa.dash (consecrate) register specifically names the dedication of a person, object, or time to God, distinct from an ordinary self-binding commitment device in that its object is holiness/set-apartness for God rather than merely constraint of future behaviour.

**Reduction and absence — what undermines a vow's binding force:**
Commitment-device research finds that vows lose their binding force when the cost of breaking them is not genuinely enforced (a vow with no real consequence for breach functions little better than an ordinary, revisable intention), or when the declaring party never genuinely internalised the commitment in the first place (public declaration used to signal identity rather than to bind future action — the finding noted above that public commitment can sometimes substitute for, rather than secure, actual follow-through).

**Location — where vowing/dedication is seated:**
Volitional, with a strongly institutional/relational dimension — a vow's binding force depends on social and, in the biblical material's own frame, divine accountability structures external to the vower's private resolve alone.

**External influences:** *Social/institutional:* Community and legal enforcement mechanisms are what give a vow force beyond ordinary intention — commitment devices work because breaking them is made genuinely costly. *Religious framework:* In the biblical material, the seriousness of a vow derives specifically from its being made before God, not merely before a human community, which is the theological dimension exceeding secular commitment-device psychology.

**Coverage assessment:** Moderate. Commitment-device research is well developed and directly illuminates the mechanics of vowing; the specifically sacred, God-directed dedication register (qa.dash) is only partially reached by it.

## Outside-science inventory

- **chosen (ba.chir, qa.ri) as divine election** — where these terms name God's own choosing of a person or people (rather than a human act of choice), the theological doctrine of election is a category outside decision psychology, which studies the chooser's process, not the claim of a sovereign divine choice made of the chosen.
- **God's will (thelēma) as governing purpose** — as noted in the superseded M29 review, divine will as the sovereign governing purpose of all things is a theological category with no analogue in human volitional psychology, which addresses only the willing creature.
- **vows and dedication (qa.dash, na.zar) made specifically before God** — the theological seriousness of a vow's being sworn before, and binding in relation to, God specifically (rather than merely a human community or one's own future self) exceeds commitment-device psychology's secular account of why binding pre-commitments work.

**Observation:** M64 draws together several previously separately-reviewed phenomena (will, planning, choice, vowing) under one cluster, and each sub-phenomenon has solid independent science coverage. The recurring outside-science theme across all four sections is the same: where this cluster's vocabulary names an act of God (electing, willing, receiving a vow) rather than an act of the human person, the relevant psychology addresses only the human side of the transaction.

## D9.1.1 / D9.2.1 / D11.2.1 / D12.1.1 prompt reference notes

**D9.1.1 — Physiological/neural substrate:** Will, planning, and choice are all substantially prefrontal-cortex-dependent executive functions (working memory, inhibitory control, prospective representation); the "crossing the Rubicon" shift from deliberation to committed intention is associated with documented changes in neural engagement consistent with reduced further evaluative processing once resolve is formed. Vowing/dedication has no distinct neural substrate beyond the general commitment/resolve circuitry, though its public and ritual dimensions likely additionally engage social-cognition and self-presentation networks.

**D9.2.1 — Generational transmission:** Planning orientation and future-time-perspective (Section 2) show documented cultural/generational transmission via family and community modelling (Zimbardo and Boyd's time-perspective research). Vow-keeping and commitment-device use as a cultural practice transmits through community teaching and example rather than through any distinct heritable mechanism. Ye.tser's dual-inclination framework, where it appears, is transmitted as inherited theological anthropology rather than as an empirically documented psychological trait.

**D11.2.1 — Innate endowment vs. acquired:** Executive-function capacity underlying will, planning, and choice has a documented heritable component (general cognitive ability and self-regulatory capacity both show meaningful heritability), while the specific skills of effective goal-setting, implementation-intention formation, and commitment-device use are acquired and trainable. Ye.tser as a pre-deliberate inclination is presented in its own rabbinic-theological frame as a given structural feature of the human person rather than either purely innate temperament or purely acquired habit — a category the secular literature does not fully parallel.

**D12.1.1 — Social/behavioural manifestation:** Resolve manifests behaviourally as persistence and resistance to distraction once an intention is formed. Planning manifests as specific, sequenced, often written or declared intended action rather than vague aspiration. Choice manifests as an observable selection among alternatives, ideally consistent with a stated value structure. Vowing manifests as a public or ritually marked self-binding declaration, with genuine behavioural force only where breach carries a real, enforced cost.
