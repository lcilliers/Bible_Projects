## Science sections

### Section 1 — Despair, fainting, and psychological collapse

**Primary field:** Clinical psychology — hopelessness and despair research

**Glosses covered (representative):**
to despair (G0560 apelpizō), to despair (G1820 exaporeomai), despair (H4531A), despairing (H4523), to faint (G0674 ekluō), to faint (G1590 ekluō — variant), to faint (H3286, H5263, H6296), fainting (H5969), faint (H1742, H3544, H5889), be faint (H5888), fainthearted (G3642 oligopsuchos), to expire (G1606, G1634), to be dismayed (H0926, H0927, H0928, H1670, H2865), to pine (H1727, H3642), to languish (H1669, H1809, H3856A, H5243A)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Beck's hopelessness theory of depression (Beck, Weissman, Abelson and colleagues, *Hopelessness Scale*, 1974; Abramson, Metalsky and Alloy's hopelessness theory of depression, 1989) treats hopelessness — the stable, global expectation that desired outcomes will not occur and that nothing one does will change this — as a distinct and measurable cognitive construct with strong predictive power for depression and suicidality. Seligman's learned helplessness research (1967 onward) demonstrated that repeated exposure to uncontrollable aversive outcomes produces a characteristic profile of passivity, motivational deficit, and cognitive impairment that generalises beyond the original inescapable situation — a close psychological analogue to the biblical vocabulary of fainting and dismay under sustained adverse pressure. The vasovagal mechanism of literal fainting (a sudden parasympathetic surge producing a drop in blood pressure and heart rate, often precipitated by extreme fear, pain, or emotional shock) gives the cluster's "fainting" vocabulary a genuine physiological referent distinct from its metaphorical extension to despair.

**Emergence — what causes despair and fainting to arise:**
Despair arises from the cognitive appraisal that a negative outcome is both certain (or highly likely) and uncontrollable — the combination Abramson and colleagues identify as the hopelessness triad (the situation is seen as global, stable, and internally attributed as beyond one's capacity to change). Fainting proper (the physiological event) arises from acute autonomic dysregulation under extreme threat, pain, blood loss, or shock. The biblical vocabulary's frequent pairing of the two (fainting language used for despair, and vice versa) mirrors the genuine psychophysiological overlap: severe psychological collapse and acute bodily collapse share overlapping autonomic pathways (both can involve a sudden parasympathetic "shutdown" response rather than the more familiar sympathetic arousal of fear).

**Reduction and absence — what causes despair to diminish:**
Hope re-emerges (clinically) when the appraisal of uncontrollability is revised — new information, new resources, or a new agent capable of changing the outcome restores the sense that the situation is not fixed. Social support is among the most consistently documented protective factors against hopelessness; the presence of a trusted other who can act, or who simply remains present, reliably reduces despair even before the underlying circumstance changes. Meaning-making (Frankl) — locating significance within or beyond the adverse circumstance — is a second major route out of despair distinct from circumstantial resolution.

**Location — where despair and fainting are seated:**
Despair is primarily cognitive-affective — a stable, global expectancy with strong emotional accompaniment (a leaden, motivationally paralysing quality distinct from acute sadness). Fainting proper is somatic and autonomic. The cluster's vocabulary treats the two as continuous rather than separate — consistent with current affective-neuroscience findings that psychological collapse and physiological collapse share autonomic substrate rather than being cleanly distinct domains.

**External influences:**
*Hereditary:* Hopelessness and depressive cognitive style show moderate heritability, overlapping substantially with general depression risk. *Developmental:* Early experience of uncontrollable adversity (a core feature of learned-helplessness research) predisposes toward hopelessness in later adversity, even where the later situation is in fact controllable. *Circumstances:* Sustained, unresolved, high-stakes adversity — especially where the person has no effective agency — is the primary situational driver. *Social:* Isolation during adversity substantially increases the likelihood of despair; a witnessing, capable other is protective.

**Coverage assessment:** Good. Clinical hopelessness and learned-helplessness research map closely onto this section's vocabulary.

### Section 2 — Anguish, pain, and distress

**Primary field:** Clinical psychology / pain research

**Glosses covered (representative):**
anguish (H2479, G3600, G3601, G4928, H6695B, H7089), pain (H2256B, H3510, H3511, H4341, H6088, H6090A), distress (H4157, H4689, H4691, H6695A, H6862B, H6869B, H7451C), affliction (H6039, H6040), afflicted (H6041), to afflict (H6031B, H6033, G0085), suffering (G3804, G3805, G2552), to suffer (G3958, H3013), to suffer with (G4777, G4778, G4841), hardship (G4730, H8513), pressure (G2347)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
The biopsychosocial model of pain (Engel, 1977; Gate Control Theory, Melzack and Wall, 1965) established that pain — physical and psychological — is not a simple linear response to injury but a state shaped jointly by physiological signal, cognitive appraisal, and emotional and social context. Melzack's later neuromatrix theory extends this to argue that the brain generates the experience of pain even without proportionate peripheral input, given sufficient psychological distress. Herman's *Trauma and Recovery* (1992) and van der Kolk's *The Body Keeps the Score* (2014) document psychological suffering and anguish as embodied states, not purely cognitive ones — anguish is held physically (tension, gut distress, tightness) as much as it is narrated. Compassion and empathic-distress research ("to suffer with" — sumpaschō) addresses the genuine physiological co-activation observed when witnessing another's pain (shared neural representations of self and other pain, Singer and colleagues, *Science*, 2004).

**Emergence — what causes anguish and distress to arise:**
Anguish and distress arise from the combination of a genuinely aversive event (loss, threat, injustice, injury) and the appraisal that the person's coping resources are insufficient to meet it. Sustained rather than momentary threat produces the more chronic distress register (as against the acute arousal of fear proper, M01). The "suffering with" vocabulary names a distinct emergence pathway — distress produced not by direct threat to the self but by empathic co-suffering with another's pain, a genuine and well-documented psychological and physiological phenomenon.

**Reduction and absence — what causes anguish to diminish:**
Distress reduces when its cause is addressed, when coping resources are augmented (materially, relationally, or through reappraisal), and — for the somatic component specifically — through the same parasympathetic-activating mechanisms that reduce fear arousal generally (safety, co-regulation, rest). Chronic, unaddressed distress produces allostatic load — cumulative physiological wear from sustained stress-response activation — which is itself a further source of vulnerability rather than a neutral baseline.

**Location — where anguish is seated:**
Whole-person: cognitive (appraisal of the threat and of one's own inadequate resources), emotional (the felt quality of anguish, distinct from fear's alarm and grief's loss-orientation), somatic (anguish is bodily felt, not merely thought), and — via the "suffering with" vocabulary — relational, since a portion of the cluster's distress vocabulary describes distress that arises specifically from relationship with another's suffering rather than from one's own circumstance.

**External influences:**
*Hereditary:* Individual differences in pain sensitivity and in stress reactivity (the strength of HPA-axis response) have documented genetic components. *Developmental:* Early adverse experience sensitises the stress-response system, producing heightened distress reactivity to later adversity (a well-replicated finding across the adverse-childhood-experiences literature). *Circumstances:* Genuine, proportionate adversity is the primary trigger — this vocabulary, unlike some emotion vocabulary, is largely reactive to real external conditions rather than free-floating. *Social:* The presence or absence of a supportive witness substantially shapes whether distress becomes chronic or resolves.

**Coverage assessment:** Good. Pain research, trauma psychology, and empathic-distress research together provide strong coverage.

### Section 3 — Weariness, sluggishness, and depletion

**Primary field:** Health psychology / motivational psychology

**Glosses covered:**
weary (H3019, H3023, H3287), be weary (H3811, H3021), be sluggish (H6101), sluggish (H6102), sluggishness (H6103, H6104), to wither (H3001, H5034B), be feeble (H2826), feebleness (H7510), to enfeeble (H5848C), to slacken (H7503)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Baumeister's ego-depletion model (*Psychological Science*, 1998) proposed that self-regulatory capacity draws on a limited, exhaustible resource — a model whose specific mechanism has been substantially contested in the replication literature since (Hagger et al., 2016 multi-lab replication failure), though the general phenomenon of mental and physical fatigue reducing subsequent performance is well established through other paradigms (sleep-deprivation research, sustained-attention decrement studies). Fatigue research more broadly distinguishes peripheral fatigue (muscular, metabolic depletion) from central fatigue (neurological, motivational). Withering and enfeeblement in their biological register have a direct physiological analogue in cellular senescence and tissue atrophy under sustained deprivation or disuse. Sluggishness as a dispositional trait overlaps with low conscientiousness in personality psychology (the Big Five) and, at a clinical extreme, with the psychomotor retardation characteristic of depressive episodes.

**Emergence — what causes weariness and sluggishness to arise:**
Weariness arises from sustained output without proportionate recovery — physical, cognitive, or emotional. Withering and enfeeblement arise from sustained deprivation of a necessary resource (nutrition, water, rest, or — in the plant metaphor that underlies much of the withering vocabulary — sustaining conditions generally). Sluggishness as a settled trait, distinct from situational fatigue, arises from a combination of temperament, low motivational engagement, and — in its more pathological form — depressive psychomotor slowing.

**Reduction and absence — what causes weariness to diminish:**
Rest, adequate recovery time, and restoration of depleted resources (nutritional, relational, motivational) are the primary reducers. Recovery research (Sonnentag, Zijlstra) identifies psychological detachment from the depleting demand, not merely physical rest, as a necessary condition for full recovery — simply stopping activity without genuine disengagement produces incomplete restoration.

**Location — where weariness is seated:**
Physical (musculoskeletal and metabolic depletion), cognitive (reduced executive capacity under sustained demand), and motivational (reduced willingness to initiate or sustain effort). The withering vocabulary is drawn substantially from plant/organic-decline imagery, giving this section an unusually direct biological referent even in its metaphorical uses.

**External influences:**
*Circumstances:* Sustained demand without adequate resource or recovery is the primary driver — this is largely a situational rather than dispositional vocabulary, though sluggishness proper shades into trait territory. *Developmental:* The capacity for sustained effort and self-regulation is partly heritable (temperament) and partly formed through developmental habituation. *Hereditary:* Baseline metabolic and stress-reactivity differences affect individual vulnerability to fatigue under equivalent demand.

**Coverage assessment:** Good. Fatigue and recovery research provide solid direct coverage.

### Section 4 — Oppression, plunder, and externally imposed affliction

**Primary field:** Social psychology / trauma psychology

**Glosses covered:**
to oppress (G2559 kakoō, H2556C, H3238, H3905), oppression (H8496), coercion (H6115), constraint (H4164), to seize (H2862, H7059, G0726, G4884), plunder (H0957, H0961, H4933, H6563, G0724, G4661), to harm (G0091, G2559, G0984), to hurt (H6087A, H6088), severe (G0840, H7186), terror (H4712), dearth (H6869A), vexer (H6869C)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Seligman's learned-helplessness research (again relevant here, distinct from its application to despair proper in Section 1) applies directly to sustained coercion and oppression: repeated exposure to uncontrollable adverse treatment by an external agent produces passivity, cognitive impairment, and difficulty recognising escape even when it becomes available. Bandura's research on moral disengagement and Milgram's obedience studies address the perpetrator side of oppression — the psychological mechanisms by which one person's harm of another is enabled and sustained. Herman (*Trauma and Recovery*, 1992) documents the specific psychological profile of sustained, interpersonally inflicted harm (as against impersonal disaster), which produces more severe and durable effects than comparable impersonal adversity because it damages basic trust in the possibility of safe relationship.

**Emergence — what causes oppression and plunder to arise:**
This vocabulary, unlike much of the rest of the cluster, primarily describes an external force acting on the person rather than an internal state — oppression, plunder, and seizure are things done to a person by another agent with greater power. The inner-being response the cluster tracks is the predictable psychological consequence of being subject to such treatment: constraint, harm, injury, terror at what cannot be resisted.

**Reduction and absence — what causes this to diminish:**
Diminished only by the removal or restraint of the oppressing power, or by the intervention of a more powerful advocate on the victim's behalf. This section's content is not internally resolvable through reappraisal or coping in the way that despair or weariness can be — it names a genuinely external condition requiring external remedy, which the recovery literature on captivity and coercive control (Herman; Patterson on coercive control) treats as a structurally different problem from internally generated distress.

**Location — where this is seated:**
Structurally located between persons (the relationship of unequal power) rather than within the person, though its effects — harm, injury, terror, constraint — are registered across every inner-being domain: somatic (injury), cognitive (impaired agency and helplessness), emotional (terror, distress), and relational (damaged trust).

**External influences:**
*Social and political:* The primary driver — this vocabulary describes conditions produced by social and political arrangements (war, conquest, exploitation, unjust rule), not individual psychology. *Historical:* The biblical corpus is produced substantially within and addressed to contexts of real oppression (exile, occupation, conquest); this section's density should be read against that historical backdrop. *Developmental:* Prior experience of powerlessness shapes both vulnerability to and resilience under later oppression.

**Coverage assessment:** Moderate. The literature on coercion, captivity, and oppression provides real coverage of the inner-being consequences; it does not, and cannot, address the theological dimension of divine response to oppression (see Outside-science inventory).

### Section 5 — Toil, labour, and effortful struggle

**Primary field:** Occupational psychology / developmental psychology

**Glosses covered:**
toil (H6089A, H6093, H8383), labor (H6001A a.mel labourer, G2872 to labor, G2873 labor, G5604 labor, G5605 be in labor), breaking (H4290, H7665, H7667, H7670, G2800)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Occupational psychology (Hackman and Oldham) addresses effortful labour and what makes it bearable or destructive depending on autonomy, meaning, and proportion to reward. The childbirth-labour vocabulary in this section (G5604/G5605, ōdin) has a direct developmental-psychology parallel in research on labour pain and its distinctive profile — intense, time-limited, purposive pain oriented toward a specific productive outcome, which is psychologically processed differently from injury pain of equivalent intensity (women's retrospective ratings of labour pain intensity are high, but its meaning and outcome markedly shape both the experience and its memory — Niven and Murphy-Black's research on the recall of labour pain). "Breaking" vocabulary (sheber, shibbaron) maps onto research on the psychological experience of a prior intact state being shattered — closely related to Section 2's anguish content but specifically carrying the sense of a structural rupture rather than an ongoing painful state.

**Emergence — what causes toil and breaking to arise:**
Toil arises when sustained, effortful labour is required to meet a goal, particularly where the labour is imposed by circumstance (the curse-of-the-ground register in Genesis) rather than freely chosen. Breaking arises from a single decisive traumatic event or the accumulation of stress beyond a structure's capacity to bear it — the language implies a before-and-after discontinuity rather than gradual depletion.

**Reduction and absence — what causes this to diminish:**
Toil is relieved by the completion or reduction of the demand, by adequate rest, or by a shift in the meaning attached to the labour (purposive, productive toil — as in labour pains oriented toward birth — is experienced very differently from meaningless or punitive toil, even at equivalent physical cost). Breaking, once it has occurred, is not "reduced" so much as it requires a distinct process of repair or restoration — the literature on psychological recovery from acute rupture (post-traumatic growth, Tedeschi and Calhoun) documents that restoration can produce a genuinely different, not merely restored, state.

**Location — where toil and breaking are seated:**
Toil is physical, cognitive, and motivational — sustained effort under real cost. Breaking is more acute and structural — a single-event or threshold phenomenon rather than an ongoing state, located wherever the "structure" in question is: physically (the body), relationally (a relationship), or psychologically (a person's sense of coherence).

**External influences:**
*Circumstances:* The primary driver in both cases — imposed demand for toil, or a discrete traumatic event for breaking. *Cultural:* Cultures vary in whether toil is framed as inherently degrading, as purposive and dignifying, or as morally neutral; this shapes its psychological cost independent of the physical labour itself. *Developmental:* Capacity to sustain toil without breaking is shaped by prior formation in effortful tasks and by baseline resilience.

**Coverage assessment:** Moderate. Occupational psychology and labour-pain research provide partial coverage; the theological register of toil as consequence of the fall (Genesis 3) exceeds this.

## Outside-science inventory

- **Affliction in its covenantal-disciplinary register** — suffering as divine formation or discipline (as in Hebrews 12) is a theological category; science addresses the psychological effects of suffering, not its theological meaning as refining.
- **Toil as consequence of the fall** — the Genesis 3 aetiology of toil as a specific consequence of humanity's rupture with God exceeds occupational psychology, which treats effortful labour as a feature of any economy, not as a marked post-lapsarian condition.
- **Oppression in its covenantal register** — God's obligation and promised action as defender of the oppressed is a theological category that trauma and social psychology, addressing only the human-level dynamics of coercion, do not reach.
- **Despair addressed by divine intervention** — the biblical pattern in which despair is met not merely by improved coping but by God's own action changing the situation is outside clinical psychology's descriptive scope, which treats hopelessness as revised through cognitive or circumstantial change alone.

**Observation:** M24's outside-science content clusters at the point where each section's human-level mechanism (despair reduced by revised appraisal, oppression relieved by a stronger advocate, toil made purposive by meaning) is claimed by the biblical text to be met specifically by God's own action. Science can describe the mechanism by which relief occurs in general; it cannot adjudicate the specific theological claim that God is the advocate, the meaning-giver, or the one who lifts the fainting.

## D9.1.1 / D9.2.1 / D11.2.1 / D12.1.1 prompt reference notes

**D9.1.1 — Physiological/neural substrate:** Strong substrate across the cluster. Fainting is a genuine autonomic (parasympathetic-surge) event; anguish and distress engage the HPA axis and produce measurable allostatic load under chronicity; weariness and sluggishness have clear metabolic and central-nervous-system correlates. The oppression/plunder section is the exception — its substrate is the victim's stress physiology, but the phenomenon itself (an external agent's coercive action) has no substrate of its own; it is a social fact with a physiological consequence, not a physiological event.

**D9.2.1 — Generational transmission:** Documented across several sections. Stress-reactivity and depressive/hopelessness cognitive style show heritable components and are also transmitted through adverse early-childhood experience (the ACEs literature is directly relevant here — early uncontrollable adversity sensitises the stress-response system and predisposes to hopelessness and heightened distress reactivity in later life, a genuinely intergenerational as well as biographical pattern). Oppression's effects can also transmit across generations through both epigenetic stress-pathway changes and through transmitted narrative and behavioural adaptation (documented in descendant populations of historical oppression and captivity).

**D11.2.1 — Innate endowment vs. acquired:** Mixed. The autonomic mechanisms underlying fainting and acute distress are innate and universal. Sluggishness/conscientiousness has a documented heritable-temperament component but is substantially shaped by acquired habit. Despair's cognitive architecture (the hopelessness triad) is acquired through experience, though individual vulnerability to forming it is influenced by innate stress-reactivity. Toil-tolerance and capacity to sustain effort without breaking are predominantly acquired through formation and practice rather than innate.

**D12.1.1 — Social/behavioural manifestation:** Highly variable by section. Weariness, sluggishness, and toil are directly behaviourally observable (reduced output, visible fatigue). Despair and anguish have behavioural correlates (withdrawal, reduced initiative, psychomotor slowing) but are substantially internal states requiring self-report or inference. Oppression and plunder are, by definition, socially and structurally manifest — they are events between persons, not private inner states, even though their psychological consequences are registered internally.
