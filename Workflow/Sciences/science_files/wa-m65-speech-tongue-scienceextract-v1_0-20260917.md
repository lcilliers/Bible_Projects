## Science sections

### Section 1 — Word, language, and verbal communication

**Primary field:** Cognitive psychology / psycholinguistics

**Glosses covered:**
word (logos), lip: word (peh), to tell (na.gad), word (e.mer), word (o.mer), word (im.rah), word (da.var), word (dab.be.ret), word (mil.lah), word: speaking (da.var), word: thing (da.var), to tell (exēgeomai), to say: will say (ereō)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Vygotsky's inner-speech research (*Thought and Language*, 1934) established that language functions not only as a communicative tool but as a cognitive one — verbal thought is a primary medium through which the mind organises itself, and words are not merely labels attached to pre-formed thoughts but constitutive of thought itself. Speech-act theory (Austin, *How to Do Things with Words*, 1962; Searle's subsequent development) provides the most directly relevant framework for the da.var/logos vocabulary's dual sense of "word" and "thing/event" — Austin's central insight, that certain utterances do not merely describe reality but perform an action in the saying of them (a declaration, a promise, a naming), closely parallels the Hebrew da.var's own collapse of "word" and "deed/matter" into a single lexical item, which is not accidental but reflects a view of speech as efficacious, world-changing action rather than mere description. Exēgeomai (to tell out, expound, make known — the root behind "exegesis") specifically names authoritative, explanatory telling, distinct from ordinary report.

**Emergence — what causes verbal communication and declaration to arise:**
Speech arises from the pressure to externalise and share an internal cognitive/verbal state — Vygotsky's developmental account traces this from social speech (directed outward from birth) to private speech (thinking aloud, observable in young children) to fully internalised inner speech (silent verbal thought, the adult default). Speech-act theory adds that declarative and performative speech (naming, promising, pronouncing) arises specifically from a speaker's socially or institutionally recognised standing to make the utterance efficacious — a mere assertion and an authoritative declaration are not equivalent even when the words are identical, because only the latter carries the standing to actually effect what it states.

**Reduction and absence — what causes communication to fail or be withheld:**
Communication fails through inadequate shared framework between speaker and hearer (comprehension failure — see M63 Section 3's interpretation material), through the speaker lacking the standing needed for a performative utterance to be efficacious, and through deliberate withholding (silence, treated more fully as its own inner state in the superseded M42 review's Section 3, now associated with live M84). Word can also fail through corruption of its content — addressed in Section 2 below.

**Location — where verbal communication is seated:**
Neurologically, inner and outer speech activate substantially overlapping language networks (with reduced motor output for inner speech); cognitively, speech is constitutive of much deliberate thought, not merely its output channel; relationally, speech-act theory locates the efficacy of declarative and performative speech in the social/institutional standing of the speaker, not in the words alone.

**External influences:**
*Developmental:* Inner speech develops through the progressive internalisation of external, socially-directed speech across early childhood (Vygotsky). *Social/institutional:* What counts as an efficacious, standing-backed declaration (rather than a mere assertion) is entirely socially and institutionally constituted — relevant background for reading da.var's own weighty, event-effecting sense, especially where it names the spoken word of God.

**Coverage assessment:** Good. Psycholinguistics and speech-act theory together provide strong, directly applicable coverage for this section's core vocabulary.

### Section 2 — Proclamation and good news

**Primary field:** Communication psychology — persuasive and hope-bearing messaging

**Glosses covered:**
to speak good news (euangelizomai), to speak good news (proeuangelizomai)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Communication research on the psychological effects of good-news versus bad-news delivery (the "MUM effect" — reluctance to transmit bad news, documented by Rosen and Tesser, 1970 — and its inverse, the documented eagerness and social reward associated with delivering good news) establishes that the valence of a message shapes both the messenger's motivation to speak and the recipient's receptivity. Hope research (Snyder's hope theory, *The Psychology of Hope*, 1994) documents that a credible message of positive change to come (a "good news" announcement in the structural sense this vocabulary names) measurably increases goal-directed engagement and resilience in the recipient, distinct from mere positive mood induction — hope specifically requires the message to be believed as both desirable and genuinely attainable, not merely pleasant to hear.

**Emergence — what causes proclamation of good news to arise, and to have effect:**
Proclamation arises from a messenger possessing information of genuinely positive, significant change and the motivation (and, in this vocabulary's specifically heraldic sense, the standing or commissioning) to declare it publicly. Its psychological effect on hearers depends heavily on the messenger's perceived credibility and standing — good news from a trusted, credible source produces markedly stronger hope and behavioural response than identical content from a source perceived as unreliable or self-interested.

**Reduction and absence:** Good-news proclamation's effect is undermined by messenger incredibility, by prior disappointed hope (which research on hope and disconfirmation shows makes subsequent hope-claims harder to sustain), and by content that, on examination, does not meet the criteria of genuine desirability and attainability that hope theory identifies as necessary for a credible hope-response.

**Location:** Relational and cognitive — the message's content is cognitively evaluated for credibility and desirability, but its full effect (hope, engagement, changed orientation toward the future) is constituted in the relationship between messenger, message, and hearer, not in the propositional content alone.

**External influences:** The messenger's prior track record and social standing are the primary documented shapers of how much hope-effect a given proclamation produces.

**Coverage assessment:** Moderate. Communication and hope research provide genuine, if indirect, coverage for this small (two-gloss) section.

### Section 3 — Empty, deceptive, and flattering speech

**Primary field:** Social psychology — ingratiation and deceptive-rhetoric research

**Glosses covered:**
smooth talk (chrēstologia), to talk nonsense (fluareō)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Jones's ingratiation research (*Ingratiation: A Social Psychological Analysis*, 1964) identifies flattery, opinion conformity, and self-presentation tactics as a well-documented strategic category — speech deployed not to convey accurate information but to secure the speaker's own advantage by manipulating the hearer's favourable regard. Chrēstologia (literally "good/smooth speaking") names precisely this strategic, other-manipulating use of pleasant speech — the New Testament's own usage (Romans 16:18, warning against those who by chrēstologia and flattery deceive the naive) matches the ingratiation literature's finding that such speech is most effective, and most dangerous, against hearers who lack the social experience or critical scrutiny to detect the strategic intent behind it. Fluareō (to talk nonsense, to babble idly) names a different failure mode — speech emptied of substantive content rather than strategically manipulative — closer to communication research on idle talk and low-information discourse than to deception research proper.

**Emergence — what causes deceptive/empty speech to arise:**
Ingratiating flattery arises from a speaker's motivation to secure advantage (trust, favour, compliance) disproportionate to what honest disclosure would obtain — it is a strategic substitute for the harder work of genuinely earning trust. Idle, empty talk (fluareō) arises from various sources documented in the general discourse-quality literature: lack of substantive content to communicate, social pressure to fill silence, or — in its New Testament usage, directed at gossiping idlers — habituated unproductive social engagement in place of useful activity.

**Reduction and absence:** Ingratiation loses effectiveness against hearers with developed critical scrutiny and independent sources of information about the speaker's actual reliability — social-skills and media-literacy research both document that recognising manipulative rhetorical strategies substantially blunts their effect. Empty talk reduces where social norms and accountability structures reward substantive rather than merely voluble engagement.

**Location:** Behavioural and strategic-motivational — both phenomena in this section are located in the speaker's communicative intent (to manipulate, or to fill space without substance) rather than in an inner emotional state as such.

**External influences:** Social and institutional structures that reward genuine substance over rhetorical polish reduce both phenomena; environments that reward surface impression over verified reliability increase them.

**Coverage assessment:** Moderate. Ingratiation research provides good coverage for the flattery/smooth-talk sub-phenomenon; idle talk is more thinly covered as its own research category.

## Outside-science inventory

- **word (da.var, logos) as the spoken word of God** — where da.var/logos names God's own creative or revelatory word — efficacious, world-making speech (Genesis 1's "and God said," John 1's Logos) — the theological claim of divine speech as itself creative and self-revealing exceeds speech-act theory's account of human performative utterance, which depends on socially constituted human institutions rather than on the speaker's own inherent, world-making authority.
- **to speak good news (euangelizomai) as the gospel proclamation** — the specifically theological content of the good news proclaimed (the gospel of Christ) is a matter for the biblical pass, not for communication psychology, which can describe the mechanics of hope-inducing proclamation but not adjudicate the truth or theological significance of its content.
- **exēgeomai as authoritative divine self-disclosure** (John 1:18, of the Son making the Father known) — this specific theological usage, naming Christ's unique capacity to make God known, exceeds ordinary explanatory-telling psychology.

**Observation:** M65 has good coverage for its core word/language content (Section 1) via psycholinguistics and speech-act theory, moderate coverage for good-news proclamation and for deceptive/empty speech (Sections 2–3). As in the superseded M42 review, this cluster sits at the inner-being/outer-expression boundary — most of its vocabulary names an outward speech act rather than a directly named inner state, and Session B will need to attend to whether a given verse's inner-being content is carried by the speech term itself or by surrounding vocabulary.

## D9.1.1 / D9.2.1 / D11.2.1 / D12.1.1 prompt reference notes

**D9.1.1 — Physiological/neural substrate:** Speech production and comprehension engage well-localised language networks (Broca's and Wernicke's areas and their broader connected systems); inner speech recruits substantially the same networks with reduced motor output. No distinct substrate is documented for good-news proclamation or deceptive speech specifically beyond general language and social-cognition (theory-of-mind, relevant to strategic ingratiation) networks.

**D9.2.1 — Generational transmission:** Language itself is the paradigm case of cultural, generationally transmitted human capability; specific communicative styles (directness, use of flattery or ingratiation, valuation of substantive versus idle talk) transmit through family and community modelling rather than through any distinct heritable pathway specific to this cluster.

**D11.2.1 — Innate endowment vs. acquired:** The underlying capacity for language is innate and species-typical (a substantial linguistics and developmental-psychology consensus, however contested in its details); the specific vocabulary, register, and communicative strategies (including both good-news proclamation and deceptive flattery) are acquired.

**D12.1.1 — Social/behavioural manifestation:** Word/speech manifests as observable verbal or written communication, distinguishable by content and by performative force (mere assertion versus efficacious declaration). Good-news proclamation manifests as public, hope-inducing announcement, its effect measurable in the hearer's subsequent engagement and resilience. Deceptive/empty speech manifests as content disproportionate to, or substituting for, genuine disclosure or substance — behaviourally detectable, per ingratiation research, once hearers develop the relevant critical scrutiny.
