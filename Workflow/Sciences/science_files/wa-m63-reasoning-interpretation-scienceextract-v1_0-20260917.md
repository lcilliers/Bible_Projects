## Science sections

### Section 1 — Reasoning, deliberation, and dispute

**Primary field:** Cognitive psychology — reasoning and argumentation research

**Glosses covered:**
a question/dispute (zētēma), dispute (antilogia), dispute-loving (filoneikos), reasoning (dialogismos), to discuss (antiballō), to discuss (dialaleō), to discuss (dialogizomai), to discuss (sullogizomai), to dispute (antilegō), to dispute (dialegō), to reason (froneō), intelligent (sunetos)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Mercier and Sperber's argumentative theory of reasoning (*The Enigma of Reason*, 2017) proposes that human reasoning evolved primarily as a social tool for producing and evaluating arguments in the presence of others, rather than as a solitary truth-seeking faculty — an account that fits the strongly dialogical, discussion-heavy character of this section's vocabulary (dialegomai, sullogizomai, antiballō — all compound verbs of reasoning done between or against others) better than a purely individual-cognition model of thought would. This theory also explains a well-documented finding: individual reasoning is prone to confirmation bias and motivated reasoning, but group argumentation, when conducted under genuine mutual challenge, is more reliable at correcting error than solitary reflection — directly relevant to why the biblical wisdom tradition consistently commends discussion and multiple counsellors (see also M64's planning material) over solitary deliberation. Kuhn's research on argumentative skill (*The Skills of Argument*, 1991) documents that the capacity to genuinely engage an opposing argument (rather than merely restate one's own position) is a developed skill that varies widely across individuals and educational backgrounds. Filoneikos (contentious, quarrelsome — literally "friend of strife") names a disordered form of this capacity, discussed further in relation to M51 Section 2's love-of-dispute material.

**Emergence — what causes reasoning and dispute to arise:**
Reasoning-in-discussion arises whenever a claim is contested or a decision must be justified to others — Mercier and Sperber's account holds that this social function, not private truth-seeking, is reasoning's primary evolutionary driver. Disputes (antilogia, zētēma) arise specifically when parties hold incompatible positions and each seeks to have their view prevail or be vindicated; filoneikos names a motivational distortion in which the contest itself, rather than resolution or truth, becomes the object of engagement.

**Reduction and absence — what causes reasoning to fail or dispute to be resolved:**
Reasoning fails through motivated cognition (reasoning primarily to defend a pre-existing conclusion rather than to evaluate it), through the absence of genuine mutual challenge (echo-chamber conditions in which confirmation bias goes uncorrected), and through low argumentative skill. Dispute resolves through the introduction of a trusted arbiter (see Section 2), through one party's genuine persuasion by the other's argument, or through mutual concession — filoneikos-type contentiousness specifically resists resolution because winning the contest, not reaching agreement, is what the contentious party is actually motivated by.

**Location — where reasoning and dispute are seated:**
Primarily cognitive, with a strongly social-relational dimension — per Mercier and Sperber, reasoning of this kind is constitutively interpersonal rather than a private mental operation merely expressed outwardly afterward. Froneō (to think, to set one's mind on, to be minded) carries an additional dispositional dimension — not just an act of reasoning but a settled orientation of thought.

**External influences:**
*Educational:* Formal training in argumentation measurably improves the capacity for genuine, rather than merely defensive, engagement with opposing views (Kuhn). *Social/cultural:* Communities and cultures vary in whether open dispute is normalised as a path to truth (rabbinic and much of Greco-Roman intellectual culture, both relevant background to this vocabulary) or discouraged as disruptive to harmony.

**Coverage assessment:** Good. Argumentation theory and reasoning research provide strong, directly applicable coverage.

### Section 2 — Judgment, discernment, and decision

**Primary field:** Judgment and decision-making psychology / legal psychology

**Glosses covered:**
to decide (diaginōskō), to decide (epikrinō), to judge (krinō), to judge/doubt (diakrinō), to judge (din), to judge (sha.phat), to judge (she.phat), to decide (cha.rats), to advise (ya.ats), to advise (ma.lakh)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Kahneman's dual-process framework (*Thinking, Fast and Slow*, 2011) remains the dominant model for how judgments and decisions are formed — fast, heuristic-driven System 1 processing versus slower, deliberate System 2 evaluation — and is directly relevant to distinguishing careful krinō/shaphat-type judgment (which the biblical wisdom and legal material consistently commends as a deliberate, examined act) from snap judgment. Legal-decision psychology (Tversky and Kahneman's original framing-effect studies, extended extensively into juror-decision research) documents the systematic ways judicial and quasi-judicial judgment can be distorted by irrelevant framing, anchoring, and in-group bias — directly relevant background for the shaphat/din vocabulary's consistent biblical concern with impartial judgment. Diakrinō's dual sense (to judge/discriminate, and also to doubt/waver) is psychologically apt: the same cognitive act of weighing alternatives that produces a decision can, when the weighing does not resolve, produce sustained uncertainty instead — decision psychology documents indecision itself as a distinct and costly cognitive state (decision paralysis under high-stakes or high-ambiguity conditions).

**Emergence — what causes sound judgment and advice to arise:**
Sound judgment requires adequate information, freedom from the biasing pressures the judgment-and-decision-making literature documents (self-interest, in-group loyalty, status pressure), and — for judicial-type judgment specifically — a settled procedural commitment to impartiality independent of the parties' relative power. Advice (ya.ats) arises from a counsellor's accumulated experience or wisdom being offered to inform another's decision — group-decision research (Surowiecki's "wisdom of crowds," Janis's contrasting groupthink findings) shows that outside input improves decision quality only under specific conditions (independence of judgment among advisors, genuine diversity of perspective) and can degrade it under others (conformity pressure, premature consensus-seeking).

**Reduction and absence — what causes judgment to fail or advice to be disregarded:**
Judgment is degraded by the biases named above and by insufficient deliberative effort (over-reliance on System 1 heuristics in situations that warrant System 2 engagement). Advice-taking research (Yaniv's documented egocentric discounting of others' input, even from advisors with objectively superior information) shows that good advice is frequently underused regardless of its quality — a robust and consistent finding directly relevant to the biblical wisdom literature's recurring theme of the person who will not receive counsel.

**Location — where judgment and decision are seated:**
Cognitive, with the legal/judicial register (shaphat, din) additionally institutional and relational — judgment in this register is an act performed on behalf of, and with authority over, others, not merely a private cognitive event.

**External influences:**
*Institutional:* Formal procedural safeguards (evidentiary standards, multiple witnesses, appeal mechanisms — several of which are themselves biblical legal innovations) measurably reduce bias in judicial-type judgment relative to unstructured discretion. *Social:* Group composition and norms around dissent strongly determine whether collective judgment converges toward accuracy (wisdom of crowds) or toward premature, biased consensus (groupthink). *Access:* Access to trustworthy advisors is unevenly distributed and constitutes a genuine social advantage in decision quality.

**Coverage assessment:** Rich. Judgment-and-decision-making and legal psychology are extensively developed fields with direct application to this section's vocabulary.

### Section 3 — Interpretation and understanding

**Primary field:** Psycholinguistics / hermeneutics-adjacent cognitive psychology

**Glosses covered:**
interpretation (hermēneia), interpreter (diermēneutēs), to interpret (diermēneuō), to interpret (hermēneuō), interpretation (pe.shar), interpretation (pe.sher), interpretation (pit.ron), to interpret (pe.shar), to interpret (pa.tar), sense (aisthētērion)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Comprehension psychology (Kintsch's construction-integration model, *Comprehension: A Paradigm for Cognition*, 1998) describes interpretation as an active process of constructing a coherent mental model from textual or symbolic input by integrating it with prior knowledge — not passive decoding but active meaning-construction, closely matching the biblical vocabulary's own range from dream/vision interpretation (pit.ron, pe.sher — used of Joseph's and Daniel's interpretive acts) to translated or explained speech (hermēneuō, diermēneuō). Schema theory (Bartlett's classic *Remembering*, 1932, and its extensive subsequent development) documents that comprehension is always mediated by pre-existing interpretive frameworks — the same input can be validly or invalidly integrated depending on the schema brought to it, a finding directly relevant to why interpretation (rather than raw perception) is itself treated in this cluster as a distinct cognitive skill requiring discernment. Sense (aisthētērion — the faculty of perception, used in Hebrews 5:14 of senses "trained by practice to distinguish good from evil") names the trained-perceptual dimension of interpretive competence — that accurate interpretation is a skill developed through practice, not a passive given capacity.

**Emergence — what causes accurate interpretation to arise:**
Accurate interpretation requires an adequate schema or framework for the material being interpreted, sufficient exposure and practice (per aisthētērion's trained-senses language), and — in the specifically prophetic/dream-interpretation register of pit.ron/pe.sher — access to information or insight not available through ordinary comprehension processes alone, which is where this section shades into the outside-science content below.

**Reduction and absence — what causes interpretation to fail:**
Interpretation fails through an inadequate or mismatched schema (imposing a framework that does not fit the material), through insufficient practice or exposure (untrained aisthētērion), and through the well-documented human tendency to interpret ambiguous material in line with prior expectation (confirmation bias operating at the level of comprehension itself, not only at the level of explicit reasoning addressed in Section 1).

**Location — where interpretive capacity is seated:**
Cognitive, and specifically dependent on stored prior knowledge and trained perceptual/comprehension skill rather than being a single unitary faculty — comprehension research treats interpretation as emerging from the interaction of incoming information with an existing knowledge structure, not as a separate, isolable mental organ.

**External influences:** *Educational/developmental:* Exposure, training, and practice (aisthētērion's own language) are the primary documented shapers of interpretive skill. *Cultural:* Available interpretive frameworks (genre conventions, shared background knowledge) are culturally transmitted and shape what a given community can accurately interpret from a given text or sign.

**Coverage assessment:** Good for the general cognitive-comprehension dimension; the specifically prophetic/revelatory dream-and-vision interpretation register (pit.ron, pe.sher) is only partially addressed by comprehension psychology and shades into the outside-science inventory below.

### Section 4 — Ignorance and unwisdom

**Primary field:** Epistemology-adjacent cognitive psychology — overconfidence and metacognition research

**Glosses covered:**
be ignorant (agnoeō), ignorance (agnoia), ignorance (agnōsia), unwise (asofos)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Metacognition research (Flavell's foundational work, extended by Dunning and Kruger's overconfidence findings, 1999) distinguishes first-order ignorance (simply lacking information) from second-order ignorance (not knowing that one lacks it) — the latter being both more consequential and harder to remedy, since it removes the very signal (recognised uncertainty) that would otherwise prompt inquiry or caution. This distinction is directly relevant to reading this section's vocabulary alongside M66's madness/recklessness material and the earlier M16 folly-cluster background: ordinary ignorance (agnoia — simply not knowing) is a remediable cognitive state, while asofos (unwise, lacking sofia/wisdom as an orienting framework) names something closer to the second-order, more entrenched condition.

**Emergence — what causes ignorance and unwisdom to arise or persist:**
First-order ignorance arises from simple lack of exposure to relevant information and is remedied straightforwardly once the information is supplied. Persistent unwisdom (asofos) is more resistant, per Dunning-Kruger and related overconfidence research, because it is frequently accompanied by inflated confidence in one's own competence that blocks the self-correction ordinary ignorance would otherwise prompt.

**Reduction and absence:** First-order ignorance reduces straightforwardly with exposure to accurate information. Overconfident unwisdom reduces more slowly and typically requires either a forceful disconfirming experience or sustained, trusted external correction (echoing the counsel-resistance finding in Section 2) rather than information alone.

**Location:** Cognitive — an absence or deficiency of the same reasoning, judgment, and interpretive faculties addressed in Sections 1–3 above, rather than a separate faculty of its own.

**External influences:** Education and access to accurate information are the primary remedies for first-order ignorance; community correction and accountability are the primary remedies (where they work at all) for the more entrenched overconfident unwisdom.

**Coverage assessment:** Moderate. Metacognition and overconfidence research provide a solid, if general, framework; the section is small and overlaps substantially with the fuller cognitive-failure treatment already given under the M16 successor cluster's own folly vocabulary.

## Outside-science inventory

- **interpretation (pit.ron, pe.sher) as divinely given insight** — where dream and vision interpretation is presented as a gift given by God to a specific individual (Joseph, Daniel) rather than as an acquired comprehension skill, the claim of revelatory, God-given insight exceeds what comprehension psychology, which addresses ordinary schema-based interpretation, can reach.
- **judgment (shaphat, krinō) in its eschatological/divine register** — God's own judgment of the nations or of individuals at the last day is a theological category; legal-decision psychology addresses human judicial process, not the claim of an ultimate, omniscient divine tribunal.
- **wisdom as the ground of right reasoning (contrasted with asofos)** — the biblical wisdom tradition's claim that "the fear of the LORD is the beginning of wisdom" grounds sound reasoning and judgment in a right posture toward God, not merely in cognitive skill; this theological grounding of reasoning itself is outside what cognitive and decision psychology, as value-neutral descriptive fields, can adjudicate.

**Observation:** M63 has strong, well-developed science coverage across reasoning, judgment, and interpretation (Sections 1–3) and moderate coverage for ignorance/unwisdom (Section 4). Its outside-science content concentrates specifically at the point where reasoning is presented as either divinely gifted (prophetic interpretation) or divinely grounded (wisdom as prerequisite for sound judgment) rather than as a self-sufficient human cognitive achievement.

## D9.1.1 / D9.2.1 / D11.2.1 / D12.1.1 prompt reference notes

**D9.1.1 — Physiological/neural substrate:** Reasoning and judgment (Sections 1–2) are prefrontal-cortex-dependent executive functions, with System 1/System 2 processing corresponding to documented differences in the speed and neural pathways engaged. Interpretation/comprehension (Section 3) engages distributed language and memory-integration networks (temporal and prefrontal regions) rather than a single localised substrate. No distinct substrate is documented for ignorance/unwisdom beyond the general absence or under-recruitment of these same networks.

**D9.2.1 — Generational transmission:** Reasoning style, argumentative skill, and interpretive competence transmit generationally chiefly through education and cultural transmission of shared frameworks (schema) rather than through a direct heritable pathway specific to this cluster; access to trustworthy counsel (Section 2) is itself unevenly and generationally transmitted as a social-capital advantage.

**D11.2.1 — Innate endowment vs. acquired:** General reasoning capacity and working-memory capacity (which underlies System 2 deliberate reasoning) have a documented heritable component (general cognitive ability, g, shows substantial heritability), but the specific skills this cluster names — argumentative engagement, calibrated judgment, trained interpretive competence — are substantially acquired through practice and formation, consistent with aisthētērion's own "trained by practice" language.

**D12.1.1 — Social/behavioural manifestation:** Reasoning and dispute manifest observably in discourse — genuine argumentative engagement versus contentious point-scoring being behaviourally distinguishable. Judgment manifests in decisions rendered, with impartiality or bias observable in outcome patterns across cases. Interpretation manifests in the accuracy and coherence of the meaning constructed from a given input, testable against the material interpreted. Ignorance and unwisdom manifest behaviourally as the refusal or inability to update belief or conduct in response to available correcting information.
