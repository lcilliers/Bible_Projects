## Science sections

### Section 1 — Physical support, load-bearing, and being upheld

**Primary field:** Biomechanics / physiology (source domain) with social psychology of perceived support (target domain)

**Glosses covered:**
support (H4552 mis'ad, H4937A/B mish'an/mish'enah, H4938A mish'enet, H5979 ma'amad), to support (H5564 samakh, H5582 sa'ad)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
The literal referent of this vocabulary — a physical prop, staff, or structural support that bears weight and prevents collapse — belongs to biomechanics rather than psychology, and has limited direct application. Its psychological interest lies in how thoroughly the support/upholding metaphor structures human experience of being sustained by another: Lakoff and Johnson's conceptual metaphor theory (*Metaphors We Live By*, 1980) demonstrates that physical support schemas (an object held up, prevented from falling) are among the most basic image-schemas recruited to structure abstract experience — "support" as a psychological and relational concept is not an arbitrary borrowing from the physical domain but a cognitively natural extension of it. Cohen and Wills's stress-buffering model of social support (*Psychological Bulletin*, 1985) is the primary psychological correlate: perceived availability of support from others measurably buffers the physiological and psychological impact of stress, functioning — in the literature's own vocabulary — as a prop that prevents collapse under load.

**Emergence — what causes the experience of being supported/upheld to arise:**
Physical support arises structurally — a load-bearing element positioned to prevent collapse. The psychological experience of being supported arises when another party (or a stable internal resource) is perceived as reliably available to bear some portion of a load the person could not sustain alone — reliability and availability, more than the magnitude of help actually given, drive the stress-buffering effect. Being "propped up" in the vow-related idiom (H5375P, "to lift: vow") names a distinct act — undertaking or upholding a commitment — where the bearing is of an obligation rather than a physical or emotional weight.

**Reduction and absence — what causes the sense of being upheld to diminish:**
The withdrawal, unreliability, or perceived absence of a supporting other is the primary psychological reducer, producing the felt experience named in the cluster's counterpart vocabulary of collapse and being without support (cf. M73's weakness/illness terms and M78's burden terms). Isolation and the erosion of stable social ties are the most consistent findings in the support-deprivation literature.

**Location — where support is seated:**
Physically located wherever the literal support structure bears the load. Psychologically, "being supported" is relational rather than internal to any single faculty — it exists in the relationship between the person and the supporting other (or structure), not as a property of the person alone. This mirrors the relational location noted for M01 Section 5's coercion vocabulary and M23/M72's power vocabulary: the term describes a relationship, and the inner-being effect (steadiness, capacity to stand) is the downstream consequence of that relationship's presence or absence.

**External influences:**
*Social:* The single strongest determinant — perceived and actual availability of supportive relationships. *Circumstances:* Load (the magnitude of what must be borne) interacts with support availability; the same objective support can feel adequate or inadequate depending on the weight it is set against. *Developmental:* Early experience of reliable caregiving (a secure base, Bowlby) establishes a template for trusting that support will be available, shaping adult expectations of being upheld.

**Coverage assessment:** Thin for the literal biomechanical referent; moderate for the psychological extension via social-support research.

### Section 2 — Bearing for others: carrying burdens and carrying news

**Primary field:** Social/occupational psychology of caregiving and communication

**Glosses covered:**
to bear/lead (G5342 pherō), to bear tidings (H1319 basar)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Pherō's general sense of carrying or bearing (extended in the New Testament to bearing one another, cf. Gal 6:2) connects to caregiving and compassion-fatigue research — Figley's work on the cost of caring (*Compassion Fatigue*, 1995) documents that habitually carrying others' burdens (as caregiver, counsellor, or intercessor) produces real, measurable psychological cost alongside its relational value, requiring its own replenishment. Basar's specific sense — bearing tidings, being a messenger of news — connects to a different literature: Rosen and Tesser's "MUM effect" (*Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 1970) documents a robust reluctance to transmit bad news even when accurate transmission is important, while the bearer of good news is received with disproportionate warmth (the herald effect) — relevant background for why the biblical vocabulary treats "one who bears good tidings" (mevaser) as a role of particular honour (cf. Isa 52:7).

**Emergence — what causes the act of bearing (burden or news) to arise:**
Bearing another's burden arises from voluntary identification with their need combined with the capacity (or willingness to extend beyond current capacity) to carry some portion of it. Bearing tidings arises from possessing information significant enough, and a relationship warranting enough, to justify the act of transmission — the messenger's motivation and the social stakes of the message (good or bad) shape both the readiness to bear it and its reception.

**Reduction and absence:**
Compassion fatigue and burnout (Maslach) are the primary reducers of sustained capacity to bear others' burdens without replenishment. Reluctance to bear bad tidings (MUM effect) is the primary reducer on the messenger side of the news vocabulary — people systematically under-transmit difficult news relative to good news.

**Location — where this is seated:**
Relational and motivational — bearing for another is inherently other-directed, located in the relationship between bearer and the one borne for (or the one to whom news is carried), not in an isolated inner faculty.

**External influences:**
*Social:* Community norms around mutual burden-bearing (versus individualist self-sufficiency norms) shape how readily this capacity is exercised and replenished. *Occupational:* Roles that require habitual bearing for others (caregiving, ministry, counselling) carry known, well-documented occupational risk of depletion without structural support for the bearer.

**Coverage assessment:** Thin but genuine — compassion-fatigue and messenger-effect research each provide a real, if narrow, point of contact.

## Outside-science inventory

- **bearing/being sustained by God** — the theological register in which God himself is the one who bears, upholds, and sustains his people (a recurring biblical pattern) is a theological category; science addresses interpersonal and self-support but not the claim of divine sustaining action.
- **to lift up a vow (H5375P)** — the covenantal/cultic act of undertaking a vow before God is a theological-legal category outside science's descriptive scope; science addresses commitment psychology generally but not the vow as a binding covenantal speech-act before God.
- **bearing good tidings of the gospel (basar's NT theological extension, euangelizō family, though not itself in this cluster's gloss list)** — where the OT basar vocabulary anticipates the bearing of specifically salvific news, the content borne exceeds ordinary messenger psychology.

**Observation:** M70 is a small, thin cluster scientifically — much of its content is source-domain physical/relational vocabulary whose primary analytical value is metaphorical structuring rather than a direct phenomenon science studies in its own right. A short, honest review is appropriate rather than an inflated one.

## Catalogue prompt reference notes (D9.1.1 / D9.2.1 / D11.2.1 / D12.1.1)

**D9.1.1 — Physiological/neural substrate:** Minimal direct substrate — this cluster's content is largely relational/structural rather than a discrete inner state with its own neural signature. Where relevant, perceived social support has documented physiological correlates (reduced cortisol reactivity, buffered cardiovascular stress response under load) via the stress-buffering literature.

**D9.2.1 — Generational transmission:** The capacity to trust that support will be available, and the disposition to bear others' burdens oneself, are both substantially transmitted through early attachment experience (a secure base) and through family/community modelling of mutual burden-bearing norms — social/observational transmission rather than genetic.

**D11.2.1 — Innate endowment vs. acquired:** Almost entirely acquired/relational rather than innate — this cluster names a capacity that exists only in relationship (there is no such thing as "support" or "bearing" in isolation), so the innate/acquired frame applies more naturally to the underlying capacities it draws on (attachment security, compassion) than to the cluster's content as such.

**D12.1.1 — Social/behavioural manifestation:** Entirely social/behavioural in character — every term in this cluster describes an inherently relational act (supporting, bearing, carrying tidings) rather than a private inner state, making this cluster a useful test case for whether the programme's finding-level framework needs a distinct "enacted-relational" category alongside its inner-state categories.
