## Science sections

### Section 1 — Favour, acceptance, and goodwill

**Primary field:** Positive psychology — gratitude and social-exchange research

**Glosses covered:**
favor (chen, H2580), goodwill (eudokia), to accept (ra.tsah, H7521), to favor (G5487, G4380), to accept (G1962)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
McCullough, Emmons and Tsang's three-component model of gratitude identifies the benefit received, attribution to an intentional benefactor, and the recognition that the benefit exceeded what was owed — directly applicable to the experience of being shown favour. Social-exchange theory (Blau, 1964; Homans) treats favour and reciprocal goodwill as the basic currency of ongoing relationships, distinguishing calculated exchange from genuinely gratuitous favour extended without expectation of return. Baumeister and Leary's need-to-belong research addresses the psychological weight of social acceptance versus rejection — being shown favour is, at root, a signal of acceptance within a valued relationship or community.

**Emergence — what causes the experience of favour and acceptance to arise:**
Favour is experienced when another party — with no obligation to do so — extends goodwill, preference, or benefit toward the recipient. The psychological intensity of the response is amplified when the favour is unexpected, when it addresses a genuine need, and when the party extending it had no instrumental reason to do so. Acceptance (ra.tsah in its reception sense) specifically names the moment a person or offering is received with approval rather than merely tolerated.

**Reduction and absence — what causes the experience of favour to diminish:**
Entitlement (treating favour as owed) eliminates the psychological force of being favoured. Habituation to repeatedly received favour reduces its perceived significance over time. Social contexts governed by strict calculated reciprocity make purely gratuitous favour psychologically unusual and, per social-exchange research, sometimes uncomfortable to receive.

**Location — where the experience of favour is seated:**
Relational and emotional — favour is irreducibly other-directed, requiring a party who extends it and a party who receives it. It has a cognitive dimension (the recognition that the benefit was not owed) and, per social-exchange research, a motivational dimension: received favour typically motivates reciprocal goodwill or loyalty toward the one who showed it.

**External influences:**
*Cultural:* Norms of reciprocity and the social scripts for extending and receiving favour are strongly culturally shaped. *Developmental:* Early experience of being shown unearned favour (rather than only earned reward) shapes the capacity to recognise and receive it later. *Circumstances:* Genuine need followed by unexpected provision is the primary situational trigger for the felt experience of favour.

**Coverage assessment:** Good. Social-exchange theory and gratitude research together provide solid coverage for this section's vocabulary.

### Section 2 — Gift-giving as social and relational practice

**Primary field:** Behavioural science / social anthropology — gift theory

**Glosses covered:**
gift (dōron, shay, charisma), gift (H0814, H1890, H4976, H4978, H4979, H4991, H5078, H5083, H5379), gift (G1390, G1394), free gift (G1431, G1434), gift (G1435)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Mauss's gift theory (*The Gift*, 1925) remains the foundational account of the social dynamics of giving and receiving — gifts create relational bonds and obligations, and the ability to receive a gift without an implied obligation to reciprocate is psychologically and socially complex. Camerer's experimental work on gift-exchange in economic games demonstrates that gift-giving reliably elicits reciprocal generosity even in anonymous, one-shot interactions — evidence that gift-exchange norms are deeply ingrained rather than purely calculated. Anthropological research across cultures (following Mauss) documents gift-giving as a near-universal mechanism for establishing, marking, and maintaining relationship — a gift is rarely a purely economic transaction; it is a relational statement.

**Emergence — what causes gift-giving to arise:**
Gift-giving arises from the desire to establish, mark, or strengthen a relationship; to honour an occasion or a recipient; or to respond to a perceived need out of genuine concern rather than obligation. Mauss's analysis identifies three obligations that structure gift cultures: the obligation to give, the obligation to receive, and the obligation to reciprocate — even where a gift is formally described as free, social gift-exchange typically carries at least an implicit expectation of some return, whether material or relational (gratitude, loyalty, future favour).

**Reduction and absence — what causes gift-giving to diminish:**
Gift-giving diminishes where relationships are purely transactional and where reciprocity norms are absent or actively suppressed. It is also inhibited where the giver fears the gift will be misread as generating an unwanted obligation, or where the recipient's pride makes genuine receiving difficult (a documented tension in gift research — receiving a gift can itself be socially costly, since it implicitly acknowledges dependence on the giver).

**Location — where gift-giving is seated:**
Behavioural and relational — a gift is, by definition, an external act (an object or benefit transferred) that carries internal and relational meaning beyond its material value. It has a motivational antecedent (the giver's concern, honour, or relational intent) and a relational consequence (the bond it establishes or reinforces).

**External influences:**
*Cultural:* The form, scale, and occasions for gift-giving are entirely culturally shaped, though the underlying practice is a human universal (Mauss). *Economic:* Material scarcity constrains the material scale of gift-giving without necessarily reducing its relational significance — small gifts under scarcity can carry as much relational weight as large gifts under abundance. *Circumstances:* Life-transition events, covenant-making, and the resolution of conflict are common occasions that elicit gift-giving cross-culturally.

**Coverage assessment:** Good. Gift theory is a well-developed cross-disciplinary literature (anthropology, behavioural economics, social psychology) directly applicable to this section's substantial gift vocabulary.

### Section 3 — Moral goodness and gracious character

**Primary field:** Positive psychology / virtue psychology

**Glosses covered:**
be good (te.ev, ya.tav), be pleasing (tov), gracious (chan.nun), be gracious (cha.nan)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Peterson and Seligman's VIA character strengths (*Character Strengths and Virtues*, 2004) identify kindness and generosity among the most consistently cross-culturally endorsed character strengths. Tov (good, pleasant, beautiful) is among the most comprehensive positive evaluative terms in Biblical Hebrew, spanning aesthetic, moral, relational, and material goodness. Gracious (chan.nun, cha.nan) describes a settled disposition of generous, unmerited kindness — a character orientation rather than an episodic act.

**Emergence — what causes gracious goodness to arise:**
As a character orientation, graciousness arises through habit formation — repeated acts of generous, uncoerced kindness that consolidate into a stable dispositional tendency (the Aristotelian account: virtues are formed through practice in community). Tov as a quality of being has both aesthetic and moral dimensions — the good is also the beautiful in Biblical Hebrew's unified concept.

**Reduction and absence — what causes graciousness to diminish:**
Graciousness diminishes through self-protective withdrawal in response to repeated disappointment or betrayal, through the cultivation of entitlement (the expectation that good treatment is owed), and through the gradual hardening of disposition documented in character-formation research on habituated vice.

**Location — where graciousness is seated:**
Characterological — a stable dispositional orientation that shapes how the person perceives and responds across situations, with an affective dimension (warm positive affect toward others) and a volitional dimension (the settled choice to act generously).

**External influences:**
*Developmental:* Graciousness is cultivated in communities that model and practise it. *Social:* Reciprocity norms shape whether graciousness is expressed freely or is constrained by obligation structures.

**Coverage assessment:** Moderate. Virtue psychology provides coverage for graciousness as a character trait; the theological character of divine graciousness now sits primarily in M50's territory.

## Outside-science inventory

- **goodwill (eudokia) as divine pleasure/will** — God's settled disposition of delight toward his people in Christ — is a theological category exceeding social-exchange theory's account of favour.
- **gracious (chan.nun) as a divine attribute** — where this term describes God's own character rather than a human disposition, it names God's settled nature, which virtue psychology cannot adjudicate as ontological fact.
- **charisma as Spirit-gift**, where present in this cluster's material, would name the gifts of the Spirit distributed sovereignly for the building of the church — a theological category beyond gift theory's account of social gift-exchange. (Note: the noun *charisma* itself is not present in the live M39 gloss list; flagged here in case verse evidence surfaces related forms.)

**Observation:** With grace and blessing content now assigned to M50 and M80 respectively, M39's remaining outside-science content is smaller and more tightly focused on divine disposition (goodwill, graciousness as God's character) rather than the fuller soteriological grace vocabulary the original v1_0 review addressed. The cluster now reads more cleanly as "gift and favour" — a genuinely more science-tractable territory than its predecessor.

## D9/D11/D12 prompt reference notes

> These four catalogue questions (`D9.1.1`, `D9.2.1`, `D11.2.1`, `D12.1.1`) replace the retired `T7.3` prompt as of the 2026-09-17 catalogue realignment (`catalogue_version='v2-dimension-realign-20260917'`). Each is answered here from this document's science sections as the pre-analytical data input; the verse-evidence side of each question is completed at the characteristic-level answer stage, not here.

**D9.1.1 — Physiological/Neural Substrate:** Moderate. Received favour and gift-giving both activate the same social-bonding physiology documented elsewhere in this programme's reviews — oxytocin release and parasympathetic engagement on receiving genuine kindness (paralleling M05's findings). Gift-exchange specifically activates reward-system responses in both giver and recipient in experimental economic-game studies (Camerer). Graciousness as a character trait (Section 3) has no distinct neural-substrate literature of its own beyond general trait-agreeableness findings.

**D9.2.1 — Generational Transmission:** Moderate. The capacity to receive gifts and favour without excessive obligation-anxiety is shaped by early experience (Section 1's developmental findings); graciousness as a character trait is documented to be cultivated across generations through community modelling (Section 3). Gift-giving norms themselves (Section 2) are strongly culturally transmitted generation to generation, per Mauss and subsequent anthropological gift research.

**D11.2.1 — Innate Endowment:** Partial. Agreeableness, the personality dimension most closely associated with graciousness and generosity, has documented heritable components. Favour-seeking and reciprocity instincts (the pull to reciprocate a gift) appear to be near-universal and possibly evolutionarily grounded in cooperative social-exchange dynamics (Camerer's cross-cultural experimental consistency), suggesting the capacity for gift-exchange itself may be closer to an innate endowment than most of this programme's characteristics — worth testing directly against D11.1's created-design finding.

**D12.1.1 — Social/Behavioural Manifestation:** Strongly cooperative. Every section of this cluster is inherently relational and prosocial: favour and acceptance signal and build relational bonds, gift-giving is one of the best-documented cooperative and relationship-establishing behaviours across cultures (Mauss; Camerer), and gracious character disposes a person toward consistent other-directed generosity. This cluster shows the clearest cooperative-manifestation profile of the clusters reviewed in this batch.
