## Science sections

### Section 1 — Physical strength and physiological capacity

**Primary field:** Physiology / exercise science

**Glosses covered:**
strength (ischus, ul, on, e.yal, e.ya.lut, o.mets, am.tsah, do.ve, che.zeq, cho.zeq, chez.qah, cha.yil, ko.ach, e.zuz, oz, o.tsem, ots.mah), be strong (ischuō, a.zaz), be vast (a.tsom), mighty (ul, cha.sin, a.tsum, taq.qiph), strong (ischuros, stereos, e.tan, am.mits, az, cha.zaq-family, cha.son), stronger (cha.zeq), to strengthen (endunamoō, sthenoō, stereoō, a.mets, cha.zaq-family), vigor (H3624, H3893), force (choz.qah, H0153)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Exercise physiology addresses physical strength as muscular capacity — the ability to exert force against resistance. The psychology of physical strength is addressed through self-efficacy research (Bandura — the belief in one's capacity to perform physical tasks). Most of this section's glosses function metaphorically in biblical context — strength vocabulary applied to God, to character, and to resilience far more than to literal muscular capacity — so physiology functions here mainly as the source domain of the metaphor rather than its primary referent.

**Emergence — what causes a sense of strength and physical capacity to arise:**
Physical strength increases through exercise, adequate nutrition, rest, and developmental maturation. The psychological sense of strength is produced by successful performance experiences, social encouragement, and the observation of models (Bandura's four sources of self-efficacy). The strengthening terms in this section (endunamoō, cha.zaq) are frequently relational — strength that comes from outside the individual, through encouragement or divine empowerment.

**Reduction and absence — what causes strength to diminish:**
Physical strength diminishes through illness, injury, age, fatigue, deprivation, and sustained high output without recovery. The psychological sense of strength diminishes through failure experiences, social discouragement, and the withdrawal of relational support. Fear, grief, and shame are among the most powerful psychological strength-reducers — they suppress the motivation to act even when physical capacity remains.

**Location — where physical strength is seated:**
Primarily musculoskeletal — physical strength resides in muscles, tendons, bones, and the neuromuscular system. The psychological sense of strength is more diffuse: it involves the whole person's self-assessment of capacity. This cluster's vocabulary spans both, with most terms naming an inner quality of being capable, resolute, and effective that transcends literal muscularity.

**External influences:**
*Developmental:* Physical strength is shaped by nutrition, health, and physical activity across development. *Social:* The psychological sense of strength is heavily shaped by relational support — "strengthening one another" (cha.zaq applied relationally) is a consistent biblical pattern with direct parallels in social support research. *Hereditary:* Physical capacity has significant genetic components (muscle fibre type, baseline hormonal levels).

**Coverage assessment:** Limited for direct physical application; moderate for psychological application. Most value is in how the physical source domain maps metaphorically onto the cluster's wider content.

### Section 2 — Energy, active power, and efficacy

**Primary field:** Motivational psychology / positive psychology

**Glosses covered:**
active energy (energeia), be active (energeō), effective (energēs enargēs), working (energēma), power (dunamis, kratos, ma.a.mats, shil.ton, ta.a.tsu.mah, to.qeph), might (ge.vu.rah, te.qaph, H1369, H6978, H8632A), growing (ga.del), to empower (dunamoō), to enlarge (ra.chav), to grow great (re.vah, H7236, H7679, H7680), to grow strong (te.qeph, H8631), to prevail (katischuō, ga.var, ta.qeph, H1396, H8630), able (G1415), have power (G1840)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Bandura's self-efficacy theory (*Self-Efficacy: The Exercise of Control*, 1997) is the primary framework — the belief in one's capacity to perform specific tasks is among the strongest predictors of actual performance and persistence. Ryan and Deci's self-determination theory addresses psychological energy (vitality) as produced by environments that support autonomy, competence, and relatedness. The energeia vocabulary in this section maps onto these motivational constructs — active, effective working as a psychological as well as physical state.

**Emergence — what causes active energy and efficacy to arise:**
Psychological energy and efficacy arise through successful performance experiences, encouragement from credible others, physiological arousal, and positive emotional states. Empowerment (dunamoō) is relational — it arises through another's action on the individual, producing increased capacity. This is structurally significant for the programme: the strengthening and empowering terms in this cluster are often explicitly relational or divine — capacity that comes from outside the self.

**Reduction and absence — what causes energy and efficacy to diminish:**
Failure experiences, social discouragement, physiological depletion, and the withdrawal of relational support all reduce efficacy and energy. Ego depletion (Baumeister — decision fatigue and self-control depletion) reduces the capacity for effective action even when no physical constraint exists.

**Location — where energy and efficacy are seated:**
Primarily motivational and cognitive, with physiological expressions. Efficacy is a belief state (cognitive); energy is experienced as a felt capacity (emotional and physiological). The energeia terms describe effective working as an observable outcome, not merely a subjective feeling.

**External influences:**
*Social:* Encouragement, modelling, and social support are primary producers of efficacy. *Cultural:* The domains in which efficacy is valued and cultivated vary by culture. *Developmental:* Early mastery experiences are foundational to adult efficacy beliefs.

**Coverage assessment:** Good. Self-efficacy and motivational psychology provide direct coverage for this section's vocabulary.

### Section 3 — Courage and boldness

**Primary field:** Positive psychology — courage research

**Glosses covered:**
bold man (G5113), be bold (G0662, G5111), boldness (G3954), firm (G0949), firmness (G4733), to preach boldly (G3955), to establish: right (H3559K)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Courage research within positive psychology (Rate, Clarke, Lindsay and Sternberg, *Journal of Positive Psychology*, 2007; Peterson and Seligman's VIA character strengths, 2004, which lists courage as one of six core virtues) treats courage as the capacity to act on a valued goal despite the presence of fear, risk, or uncertainty — distinct from fearlessness, which is the absence of fear altogether. Rachman's classic research on courage in bomb-disposal operators demonstrated that courageous actors typically do experience fear physiologically but act despite it — courage is a behavioural and volitional category, not an emotional one. Putman's typology distinguishes physical, moral, and psychological courage; the Greek parrēsia-family vocabulary in this section (boldness, to preach boldly) maps most directly onto moral/social courage — the willingness to speak or act openly despite anticipated social cost.

**Emergence — what causes courage and boldness to arise:**
Courage arises when a person values a goal or conviction highly enough to act on it despite perceived risk, and possesses (or is given) sufficient confidence in their capacity to act that the fear does not become paralysing. Boldness in speech (parrēsia) specifically arises from confidence in one's standing or relationship with the audience or authority being addressed — the term's root sense is "all-speech," the freedom to say everything without concealment, which requires a secure enough relational position to risk direct speech.

**Reduction and absence — what causes courage to diminish:**
Courage diminishes when perceived risk rises without a corresponding rise in perceived capacity or support, when prior attempts to act boldly have been punished or humiliated, and when the valued goal itself becomes uncertain or contested. Boldness in speech is silenced by the withdrawal of relational security or standing — parrēsia requires a position from which one can speak without excessive cost.

**Location — where courage is seated:**
Volitional and behavioural, with an emotional substrate (fear is typically present, not absent) and a relational dimension for boldness in speech specifically (parrēsia is inherently social — it describes a manner of speaking to another, not a private inner state). Firmness (G4733, G0949) names the stable, settled quality that underlies sustained courageous action rather than a single brave act.

**External influences:**
*Social:* Encouragement, modelling of courageous action by others, and secure relational standing are primary supports for boldness. *Developmental:* Early experiences of being heard and taken seriously build the confidence that underlies later boldness in speech. *Circumstances:* High-stakes, high-risk situations are the proving ground for courage; low-stakes environments do not develop or reveal it.

**Coverage assessment:** Moderate. Courage research is a smaller but well-defined area of positive psychology; the specifically verbal/social register of parrēsia has less direct coverage than physical courage.

### Section 4 — Subjection and the exercise of force

**Primary field:** Social psychology — coercion and compliance

**Glosses covered:**
to subject (hupotassō), to compel (anankazō, H0597), to conquer (katagōnizomai, G2610, G3528, G5245)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Cialdini's compliance research (*Influence*, 1984) addresses the mechanisms by which people are moved to comply with requests or demands. Seligman's learned helplessness research addresses the psychological consequences of prolonged coercive control.

**Emergence — what causes coercive force to arise:**
Coercion arises when the controlling party possesses greater power and is willing to use it to compel compliance regardless of the subject's preference — the exercise of power without the consent dimension that would distinguish legitimate authority (now M72's territory) from coercion.

**Location — where this characteristic is seated:**
Like M01's Section 5, the glosses here primarily describe relationships and actions rather than inner states. The inner experience of the coerced or conquered party sits in the same physical-somatic and emotional domain as M01's coercion vocabulary.

**External influences:**
*Social and political:* The conditions that enable coercive force are structural — conflict, conquest, and asymmetric power create the contexts in which these terms are deployed.

**Coverage assessment:** Thin. This section's content is now much smaller than in v1_0 since the governance/authority material moved to M72; what remains is narrowly the exercise and resisting of raw compelling force.

## D9/D11/D12 prompt reference notes

> These four catalogue questions (`D9.1.1`, `D9.2.1`, `D11.2.1`, `D12.1.1`) replace the retired `T7.3` prompt as of the 2026-09-17 catalogue realignment (`catalogue_version='v2-dimension-realign-20260917'`). Each is answered here from this document's science sections as the pre-analytical data input; the verse-evidence side of each question is completed at the characteristic-level answer stage, not here.

**D9.1.1 — Physiological/Neural Substrate:** Physical strength (Section 1) has a direct and well-understood physiological substrate (musculoskeletal and neuromuscular systems). Courage (Section 3) is notable for research showing fear's physiological signature is typically *present*, not absent, in courageous action (Rachman's bomb-disposal studies) — courage is a volitional override of an intact fear response, not its neural absence. Efficacy and energy (Section 2) are more motivational-cognitive than narrowly neural, though self-efficacy beliefs correlate with prefrontal engagement in goal pursuit.

**D9.2.1 — Generational Transmission:** Moderate. Physical strength has an obvious hereditary/constitutional component (muscle fibre type, hormonal baseline) but is not "transmitted" in a generational-pattern sense beyond ordinary genetics. Courage and boldness show generational modelling effects — children of visibly courageous or vocally confident parents show elevated boldness — closer to social learning than strict heredity.

**D11.2.1 — Innate Endowment:** Partial. Baseline physical strength potential and temperamental fear-reactivity (which sets the "difficulty level" courage must overcome) both have heritable components. Courage itself, efficacy, and boldness in speech are not treated as innate endowments in the literature — they are built through experience, encouragement, and repeated successful action taken despite risk, on top of an inherited temperamental substrate.

**D12.1.1 — Social/Behavioural Manifestation:** Mixed. Physical strength and energy/efficacy (Sections 1–2) are manifestation-neutral — they can serve either cooperative ends (strengthening one another, per the relational reading of cha.zaq) or self-directed ends. Courage and boldness (Section 3) are frequently manifested socially and relationally — parrēsia by definition addresses another party. Subjection/coercion (Section 4) is manifested competitively and adversarially by definition — it names the imposition of one party's will over another's.
