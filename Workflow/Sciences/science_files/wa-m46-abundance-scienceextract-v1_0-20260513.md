## Science sections

### Section 1 — Goodness and abundance

**Primary field:** Positive psychology — wellbeing research

**Glosses covered:**
goodness (agathōsunē), abundance (mar.veh, rov, H3502, H4766, H7230)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Diener's subjective wellbeing research (*Psychological Bulletin*, 1984) defines wellbeing as the combination of positive affect, low negative affect, and high life satisfaction. Seligman's PERMA model (*Flourish*, 2011) extends wellbeing beyond subjective happiness to include Positive emotion, Engagement, Relationships, Meaning, and Achievement. Goodness (agathōsunē — moral goodness, generosity) maps onto the VIA character strengths (kindness, generosity) and their wellbeing correlates. The abundance vocabulary (mar.veh, rov — abundance, multitude) names a quantitative fullness that positive psychology treats as necessary but not sufficient for wellbeing.

**Emergence — what causes goodness and a sense of abundance to arise:**
Psychological flourishing arises when the conditions for need satisfaction are met — relationships, meaningful activity, basic security, and growth. Positive psychology research consistently shows that flourishing is not produced by material abundance above a moderate threshold (the Easterlin paradox) but by the quality of relationships, meaning, and engagement. Goodness (agathōsunē) as a character quality produces flourishing both in the person who possesses it and in those around them — character strengths are positive-sum in their exercise.

**Reduction and absence — what causes goodness and the sense of abundance to diminish:**
Flourishing diminishes through the deprivation of relational connection, meaningful activity, basic security, or the absence of growth. Material scarcity below a threshold reduces flourishing through the cognitive tax of scarcity (Mullainathan and Shafir, *Scarcity*, 2013); above the threshold, additional material abundance does not produce proportional increases in wellbeing.

**Location — where goodness and abundance are seated:**
A property of the whole person's life rather than any single faculty — experienced as a felt quality of aliveness, engagement, and rightness that is simultaneously emotional, cognitive, relational, and motivational. Goodness (agathōsunē) as a character quality is seated in the will and habituated disposition; abundance as felt fullness is more circumstantial and material.

**External influences:**
*Social:* Relationships are the single strongest predictor of subjective wellbeing across cultures and populations (Diener and Oishi). *Cultural:* The definition of flourishing varies by cultural context. *Circumstances:* Basic security is a prerequisite; above this threshold, material circumstances add comparatively little to flourishing.

**Coverage assessment:** Good. Positive psychology wellbeing research is directly relevant to this section's vocabulary, though the section is now smaller than v1_0's since general "greatness" content is no longer part of the live cluster.

### Section 2 — Material wealth, security, and the psychology of money

**Primary field:** Behavioural economics / social psychology

**Glosses covered:**
riches (ke.vud.dah, H3520B, H6239, G4149), wealth (cho.sen, H2633, G3126, G5094, H1991, H5232, H5233, H2428H), to gain (kerdainō), luxury (ta.a.nug, H8588), to prosper (da.shen, H1878, H7951, H6743B, H6744, G2141), prosperity (H3574, H7959, G2142), substance (H1952), rich (H6223, H8082, G4145, G3045), to enrich (H6238, G4148), richly (G4146), self-sufficient (autarkeia), secure (H7600), fatness (H4924B), inheritance (H5159), to hoard (H2630), to grow fat (H8080), money (G0694, G5536), money-loving (G5366), to luxuriate (H5727)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Kahneman and Deaton's research (*PNAS*, 2010) established that emotional wellbeing increases with income up to a moderate threshold and plateaus above it. Kasser's materialism research (*The High Price of Materialism*, 2002) demonstrates that strong financial goals are negatively correlated with wellbeing even when achieved. Thaler's behavioural economics (mental accounting, endowment effects) addresses the psychological distortions in how people value, hoard, and manage money. Loss-aversion research (Kahneman and Tversky) is directly relevant to hoarding — the documented tendency to weight potential losses roughly twice as heavily as equivalent gains, which motivates the retention of accumulated resources beyond rational need. Security-seeking through material accumulation is addressed in attachment-adjacent research on financial anxiety (Klontz and Britt's money-scripts research), which identifies "money avoidance" and "money vigilance" as distinct psychological orientations toward accumulated wealth.

**Emergence — what causes the pursuit, hoarding, and enjoyment of wealth to arise:**
The pursuit of wealth arises from basic security-seeking (wealth as a buffer against deprivation), social status-seeking (wealth as a marker of social standing), cultural conditioning of desire toward material objects, and the genuine utility of financial resources for meaningful goals. Hoarding specifically arises from loss aversion and from anxiety about future scarcity that outpaces present need — the accumulated store functions psychologically as a guarantee against an uncertain future, and that guarantee-function tends to expand faster than the objective risk it addresses. Self-sufficiency (autarkeia) as a stated goal arises from the desire to be independent of others' provision or goodwill — a security strategy pursued through personal accumulation rather than relationship.

**Reduction and absence — what causes the pursuit of wealth and hoarding to diminish:**
Reduced by genuine material security (removing the scarcity-driven motivation), by the reorientation of goals toward intrinsic rather than extrinsic values (Kasser), by gratitude and sufficiency practices, and by the experience that wealth does not deliver the wellbeing it promises. Money-vigilance and hoarding specifically are reduced by interventions that address the underlying anxiety about future scarcity rather than the material shortfall itself — behavioural-finance research finds that hoarding driven by anxiety does not resolve merely by increasing the hoarded amount.

**Location — where wealth-orientation, security-seeking, and hoarding are seated:**
Primarily cognitive and motivational — a goal structure and valuation system — with a significant emotional dimension (the anxiety of insufficiency that drives accumulation and hoarding, and the pleasure of acquisition that reinforces it) and an identity dimension (wealth and self-sufficiency as sources of self-worth and independence). The psychology of material prosperity as an inner-being orientation is more about what wealth-seeking does to the inner being than about wealth as an external fact.

**External influences:**
*Cultural:* Consumer capitalism creates cultural conditions that systematically elevate material goals; cultures with strong communal-sharing norms and sufficiency values produce less wealth-oriented and less hoarding-prone individuals. *Developmental:* Childhood material deprivation is one of the most consistent predictors of adult over-valuation of financial security and hoarding-type money scripts (Klontz and Britt). *Social:* Social comparison with wealthier peers amplifies material aspiration.

**Coverage assessment:** Good. Behavioural economics and materialism research provide strong coverage; the security/hoarding dimension newly confirmed in this cluster is well covered by loss-aversion and money-scripts research specifically.

## D9/D11/D12 prompt reference notes

> These four catalogue questions (`D9.1.1`, `D9.2.1`, `D11.2.1`, `D12.1.1`) replace the retired `T7.3` prompt as of the 2026-09-17 catalogue realignment (`catalogue_version='v2-dimension-realign-20260917'`). Each is answered here from this document's science sections as the pre-analytical data input; the verse-evidence side of each question is completed at the characteristic-level answer stage, not here.

**D9.1.1 — Physiological/Neural Substrate:** Moderate. Material reward and acquisition activate dopaminergic reward circuitry in a manner well-documented in behavioural economics and neuroeconomics (overlapping with the general reward-processing literature); loss aversion (relevant to hoarding) has documented neural correlates in amygdala and insula activation during anticipated loss. Goodness/character (Section 1) has no distinct substrate literature of its own beyond general trait research.

**D9.2.1 — Generational Transmission:** Well documented. Money scripts (Klontz and Britt) — including hoarding and security-anxiety orientations toward wealth — are explicitly shown to be transmitted across generations through family financial modelling and childhood experience of scarcity or abundance. Inheritance itself (H5159) is the most literal possible instance of generational transmission in this programme's entire vocabulary set — material wealth passed directly from one generation to the next — worth cross-referencing against any verse evidence using inheritance language generationally.

**D11.2.1 — Innate Endowment:** Not strongly supported. Loss aversion and status-seeking (which underlie wealth pursuit and hoarding) appear to have some evolutionary/innate grounding as general cognitive biases, but wealth-seeking, hoarding, and self-sufficiency as specific orientations are predominantly shaped by developmental experience and cultural context (Section 2's external-influences findings) rather than functioning as a specific innate endowment.

**D12.1.1 — Social/Behavioural Manifestation:** Mixed, leaning individualistic/competitive. Wealth pursuit, hoarding, and self-sufficiency (autarkeia) are oriented toward individual security and independence rather than cooperative exchange — autarkeia by definition names a stance of not needing others. Social comparison research shows wealth-orientation is also inherently comparative/competitive (status-seeking relative to peers). Goodness (Section 1) is the cluster's one clearly cooperative/prosocial element, contrasting with the largely self-directed orientation of Section 2.
