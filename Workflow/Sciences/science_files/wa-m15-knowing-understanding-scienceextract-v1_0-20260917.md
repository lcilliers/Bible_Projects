## Science sections

### Section 1 — Knowledge and knowing

**Primary field:** Cognitive psychology / epistemology

**Glosses covered:**
knowledge (H1844, H1843, H1847, H4093, H4486, G1108, G1922), to know (oida-family G6063, G0143, G1097, G1987, G2467, G4267, H3045, H3046, H3321, H8175C), be aware (G4894)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Memory and learning research (Tulving's episodic/semantic memory distinction; Baddeley's working-memory model) addresses the acquisition and retention of knowledge as a cognitive process. The distinction between knowing-that (propositional knowledge) and knowing-how (procedural knowledge) is foundational in cognitive psychology and epistemology alike. The Hebrew ya.da carries a distinctively relational and experiential dimension — knowing through direct involvement and relationship — that exceeds propositional knowledge, and parallels the philosophical distinction between propositional knowledge and acquaintance knowledge (Russell).

**Emergence — what causes knowledge and knowing to arise:**
Knowledge develops through sensory experience and its mental encoding, instruction and transmission from others, inference from existing knowledge, and — for experiential knowing specifically — direct involvement and relationship. The relational dimension of ya.da suggests that genuine knowledge of persons requires a form of participation that purely observational knowledge cannot achieve.

**Reduction and absence — what causes ignorance:**
Ignorance arises from lack of exposure to relevant information, motivated avoidance of information that threatens existing beliefs, and cognitive capacity limits. Where the biblical material frames ignorance as morally significant (the suppression of knowledge one could and should have, Romans 1:18–20), this names an act of will rather than merely an epistemic gap — a category the science of memory and learning does not itself adjudicate.

**Location — where knowledge is seated:**
Primarily cognitive — stored in memory and activated through recall. Experiential knowing (ya.da) has a broader location: it is seated in the whole person's history of relationship and engagement, not merely in propositional mental representations.

**External influences:**
*Education and culture:* Knowledge is primarily transmitted through formal and informal education within cultural traditions. *Access:* Differential access to knowledge — including social and economic barriers to education — is a significant circumstantial shaper of who comes to know what.

**Coverage assessment:** Good. Memory and learning research provides extensive coverage of propositional knowledge; the relational dimension of ya.da has thinner direct science engagement.

### Section 2 — Understanding, perception, and discernment

**Primary field:** Cognitive psychology — comprehension and insight research

**Glosses covered:**
understanding (G4907, G5428, H8394, H0998, H0999, H7922), to understand (suniēmi-family H0995, G1492G, G3539, G3543), to perceive (G0143, G2529, H2938, G1492I), discerning (G2924), discernment (diakrisis, G1253), to contemplate (H7920)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Comprehension research addresses how humans make sense of complex information through the construction of mental models (Johnson-Laird) and the role of prior knowledge in determining what can be understood. Insight research (Metcalfe and Wiebe; Schooler and colleagues) distinguishes insight problem-solving (sudden, felt-as-discovery) from analytical, step-by-step problem-solving. The Hebrew bi.nah-family vocabulary (understanding, H0998/H0999/H7922) carries a depth-discernment quality — understanding that goes beneath the surface to underlying structure rather than merely registering surface information.

**Emergence — what causes understanding and discernment to arise:**
Understanding arises from adequate background knowledge that provides a framework for integrating new information, the cognitive capacity to detect structural patterns beneath surface variation, and — for insight specifically — periods of relaxed, non-analytical processing that allow unconscious pattern recognition to surface. Discernment adds an evaluative dimension: not merely comprehending what something is, but assessing what it means and what response it calls for.

**Reduction and absence — what causes understanding to fail:**
Understanding fails through inadequate background knowledge, cognitive overload, and motivated reasoning that protects existing beliefs against new information. Where understanding is present as raw comprehension but the evaluative-discernment dimension is absent — information without the capacity to act rightly on it — the biblical vocabulary's senselessness terms (H5034A, and M16's folly vocabulary more broadly) name this specific failure mode.

**Location — where understanding and discernment are seated:**
Primarily cognitive, though discernment adds a practical/evaluative dimension oriented toward action rather than pure comprehension — closer to Aristotle's phronesis (practical wisdom) than to theoretical knowledge alone.

**External influences:**
*Education and exposure:* Depth of engagement with a domain is the primary precondition for genuine understanding. *Mentorship:* Understanding is substantially transmitted through guided engagement with complexity rather than through information alone.

**Coverage assessment:** Good. Cognitive psychology provides solid frameworks for comprehension, insight, and discernment.

### Section 3 — Thought, mind, and inner cognition

**Primary field:** Cognitive psychology — thought and consciousness research

**Glosses covered:**
thought (H6248, H6250, H7454, H7808, G1270, G1771, G1963, G3053, H7476, H4609A), thinking (G5424, G5429 thoughtful), mind/thought (G3540, G1271, G3510, G3563, H1079), to think (G1380, G3543, H2803J, H6245B), to reflect on (G1760), reflection (G1761), resolution (gnōmē, G1106), to set: consider (H7896K, H7760K), to mean (G3177), to resemble (G1503), to yield (G1502), hard to understand (G1425), be of sound mind (G4993), natural (psuchikos, G5591), be senseless (H5034A), to change mind (G3328)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Cognitive science of thought (Baars's global workspace theory; Dehaene's consciousness research) addresses the nature and content of conscious thought and its neural correlates. The automatic-versus-deliberate distinction (System 1/System 2 — Kahneman) is directly relevant to this section's broad "thought" vocabulary, which spans both spontaneous, intrusive mental content and deliberate reasoning. In Hebrew and Greek anthropology, thought is frequently located in the heart (lev, kardia) rather than exclusively the head — a whole-person, embodied cognitive account that anticipates contemporary embodied-cognition research's rejection of a purely head-bound model of thinking.

**Emergence — what causes particular thought-content and mental states to arise:**
Thought-content arises from deliberate direction of attention, the automatic activation of associated memories and schemas, and emotional states that prime particular content (emotion-congruent cognition). "Natural" (psuchikos) as a specific Pauline category names unaided, unregenerate human cognition — a theological-anthropological distinction addressed further in the outside-science inventory below.

**Reduction and absence — what causes thought to be redirected or fail:**
Deliberate thought can be redirected through attentional control (a well-documented executive-function capacity); a fundamental change of mind (metanoia-adjacent "to change mind," G3328) requires a deeper restructuring of the frameworks and values that generate thought-content, not merely a single redirection of attention.

**Location — where thought and mind are seated:**
Cognitive — distributed across the neural networks constituting working memory, long-term memory, and executive control — but this section's own vocabulary (mind/heart language) reflects an ancient whole-person account that resists locating thought exclusively in a single organ or faculty.

**External influences:** The emotional and motivational state of the person substantially shapes what thought-content arises; cultural and community environments shape the concepts and frameworks available for thought.

**Coverage assessment:** Moderate to good. Cognitive science provides solid frameworks for most of this section; the specific theological-anthropological categories (natural/psuchikos) extend beyond it.

### Section 4 — Meditation, contemplation, and instruction

**Primary field:** Cognitive psychology / psychology of mindfulness and education

**Glosses covered:**
meditation (H1900, H1901, H1902H), to muse (H7878), meditation (H7881), to ponder (H0239), to contemplate (G0333), to teach (G1321), discipline (G3809, H4561), to train (G1128, G4994), arbiter (G3312)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Mindfulness research (Kabat-Zinn; Baer; Williams and Teasdale) has extensively documented the benefits of sustained, non-judgmental attention to present experience. Rumination research (Nolen-Hoeksema) distinguishes adaptive reflection (constructive engagement with difficult material) from maladaptive rumination (repetitive, passive cycling through negative content) — a distinction directly relevant to this cluster's meditation vocabulary, which biblically leans toward the sustained, active, vocalised register (ha.gut — to mutter, to meditate) closer to lectio divina than to contemporary silent mindfulness practice. Instructional and training research (deliberate-practice literature — Ericsson) addresses how sustained, guided teaching and discipline build durable cognitive and practical competence.

**Emergence — what causes meditation, contemplation, and instructional learning to arise:**
Reflective cognition arises when a person creates space and time for slow, sustained engagement with content, when there is sufficient psychological safety to tolerate the discomfort of genuine self-examination, and when the reflection is oriented toward understanding and action rather than anxious cycling. Instruction and discipline (teaching, training) build understanding through structured, guided repetition rather than through unguided reflection alone — the two registers of this section (self-directed meditation and other-directed teaching) are complementary pathways to the same end.

**Reduction and absence — what causes meditation and instructional formation to diminish:**
Genuine reflection is crowded out by the pace and interruption-density of daily life and by the preference for information over understanding; the slide from adaptive reflection to rumination is a documented clinical risk. Instructional formation fails without sustained, structured repetition — deliberate-practice research consistently finds that unstructured exposure alone produces far weaker competence than guided, corrected practice.

**Location — where meditation and instruction are seated:**
Cognitive and affective for meditation — sustained reflection involves both deliberate attention-direction and emotional engagement with material, with this cluster's vocalised-meditation register (muttering, speaking to oneself) suggesting a partly bodily, partly private-social location rather than a purely silent inner event. Instruction and discipline are inherently social — teaching and training require another party.

**External influences:**
*Practice:* The capacity for sustained reflection is cultivable through deliberate practice — a consistent finding across both mindfulness research and the wisdom traditions. *Cultural:* Contemplative traditions that embed meditation and structured teaching in daily life cultivate these capacities; cultures that prioritise productivity and speed suppress them. *Circumstances:* Silence, solitude, and unhurried time are circumstantial prerequisites for deep meditation; consistent access to a teacher or trainer is the corresponding prerequisite for instructional formation.

**Coverage assessment:** Good. Mindfulness/reflection research and deliberate-practice/instructional research are both well developed and directly relevant.

## Outside-science inventory

- **natural (psuchikos) as Pauline anthropological category** — psuchikos (the natural, un-Spirit-directed person) is a Pauline theological category distinguishing two modes of human existence before God, not a psychological trait distinction cognitive science can generate or adjudicate.
- **to know (ya.da) in its most relational-covenantal register** — where "to know" names God's own knowing of his people, or the intimate covenant relationship between God and Israel, the theological depth of that knowing exceeds the relational-knowledge psychology addressed in Section 1.
- **arbiter (meristēs)** in its specifically eschatological/judicial register, where it names Christ's refusal to act as a worldly arbiter of inheritance disputes (Luke 12:14) — a theologically loaded refusal that a generic cognitive-psychology account of arbitration and judgment does not capture.

**Observation:** M15 remains one of the programme's broadest clusters even after wisdom and counsel content moved to M16. Its outside-science content is comparatively modest and concentrated at the relational-covenantal and Pauline-anthropological ends; the great bulk of the cluster's knowing, understanding, thinking, and meditating vocabulary is well covered by cognitive psychology, memory research, and mindfulness/instructional research.

## D9/D11/D12 prompt reference notes

> These four catalogue questions (`D9.1.1`, `D9.2.1`, `D11.2.1`, `D12.1.1`) replace the retired `T7.3` prompt as of the 2026-09-17 catalogue realignment (`catalogue_version='v2-dimension-realign-20260917'`). Each is answered here from this document's science sections as the pre-analytical data input; the verse-evidence side of each question is completed at the characteristic-level answer stage, not here.

**D9.1.1 — Physiological/Neural Substrate:** Good coverage. Knowledge, understanding, and thought (Sections 1–3) are grounded in well-characterised memory and executive-function neuroscience (hippocampal/medial-temporal memory systems, prefrontal executive control); meditation and contemplative practice (Section 4) have an extensive neuroimaging literature documenting measurable, trainable changes in attention-network and default-mode-network function.

**D9.2.1 — Generational Transmission:** Moderate to strong. Instruction, discipline, and training (Section 4) are, almost by definition, generationally transmitted — teaching is an inherently intergenerational act. Cognitive style and general reasoning capacity also show documented parent-to-child transmission through both shared environment (educational access, modelling of reflective habits) and heritable cognitive-ability components.

**D11.2.1 — Innate Endowment:** Partial. Baseline cognitive capacities (working memory, general reasoning ability) have well-documented heritable components and represent genuine innate endowment; however, the specific content of knowledge, understanding, and the capacity for sustained meditation and disciplined learning are built through experience, education, and practice on top of that innate cognitive substrate rather than being themselves innate.

**D12.1.1 — Social/Behavioural Manifestation:** Mixed. Knowledge and thought (Sections 1, 3) can be manifested privately, but a great deal of this cluster's vocabulary is inherently social — understanding and discernment are frequently exercised in relation to others' situations, and Section 4's teaching/training vocabulary is explicitly other-directed and cooperative by nature (instruction requires a teacher and a learner in relationship). No section of this cluster shows an adversarial/competitive manifestation pattern.
