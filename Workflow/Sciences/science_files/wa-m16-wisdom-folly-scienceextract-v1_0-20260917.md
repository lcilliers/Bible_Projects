## Science sections

### Section 1 — Folly and foolishness

**Primary field:** Cognitive psychology — judgement and decision-making

**Glosses covered:**
folly (H0200, H5529, H5039, H5531A, H5531B, H6580, H8604), fool (H3684, H5530, G4469), foolish (H0196, H5036, G0878, G3474, G0453, H0191), foolishness (afrosunē, G0877, G3472, G0454), be foolish (H1984C, G3471, H2973, H5528), foolish talk (G3473), stupid (H1198), be stupid (H3688), stupidity (H3687), be brutish (H1197B), senseless (asunetos, G0801), simple (H6612A), to be dull (H3513J)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Kahneman's dual process theory (*Thinking, Fast and Slow*, 2011) provides the primary cognitive-science framework: foolish decisions typically arise from the uncritical application of fast, automatic (System 1) heuristics to situations that require slow, deliberate (System 2) reasoning. Cognitive-bias research (Tversky and Kahneman) documents the systematic ways human judgment departs from rationality. Dunning-Kruger research (Kruger and Dunning, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 1999) demonstrates that incompetent individuals systematically overestimate their own competence — the fool characteristically does not know that they are foolish, a finding with direct bearing on why folly, biblically, is so resistant to correction (Proverbs 26:12).

**Emergence — what causes foolishness to arise:**
Foolishness arises from the overconfident application of heuristics without adequate deliberation, insufficient knowledge and experience in the relevant domain, and motivational biases that distort judgment toward self-interest or immediate gratification. Brutishness (H1197B) and stupidity name a more thoroughgoing, characterological deficit than a single poor decision — a settled incapacity for reflective judgment rather than an episodic lapse.

**Reduction and absence — what causes foolishness to diminish:**
Reduced by education in the specific biases that distort judgment (debiasing — though genuinely harder to achieve than commonly assumed), by deliberate cultivation of slow, careful deliberation, by the development of genuine domain expertise, and by the accountability of a wise community that names and corrects poor judgment. Dunning-Kruger's own finding implies a structural difficulty here: the more foolish the judgment, the less able the person typically is to recognise it as such without external correction.

**Location — where foolishness is seated:**
Both cognitive and characterological. Cognitive folly is located in deficient judgment processes — the wrong deployment of heuristics, overconfidence in one's own cognition. Characterological folly (the settled fool, na.val-type) is located in the person's fundamental orientation — a moral-epistemic stance that refuses correction, not merely a cognitive deficit.

**External influences:**
*Education:* Formal education in critical thinking and decision-making partially reduces cognitive foolishness, though it does not reliably touch characterological folly. *Social environment:* Environments that reward confident assertion over careful deliberation amplify foolishness. *Developmental:* Adolescence and early adulthood show elevated risk-taking and impulsivity linked to prefrontal cortex immaturity.

**Coverage assessment:** Good. Judgment and decision-making research provides rich coverage of cognitive foolishness; the moral-theological dimension of characterological folly is addressed further in the outside-science inventory below.

### Section 2 — Wisdom as capacity and character

**Primary field:** Developmental psychology / positive psychology — wisdom research

**Glosses covered:**
wisdom (sofia, chokh.mah, H2451, H2452, H8454), wise (sofos, chak.kim, cha.kham, H2445, H2450), to make wise (sofizō, G4679), prudent (a.rum, H6175), be prudent (H7919A), be shrewd (H6191), craftiness (G3834, H6195), self-discipline (G4995)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
The Berlin Wisdom Paradigm (Baltes and Staudinger, *American Psychologist*, 2000) defines wisdom as expert knowledge about the fundamental pragmatics of life — factual and procedural knowledge, lifespan contextualism, relativism of values, and management of uncertainty. Sternberg's balance theory of wisdom (2003) defines wisdom as the use of intelligence and creativity toward a common good, balancing intrapersonal, interpersonal, and extrapersonal interests. Grossmann's situated-wisdom research demonstrates that wisdom is context-dependent and can be cultivated through perspective-distancing practices, not merely accumulated passively with age. Prudence and shrewdness sit at a documented boundary in the literature: practical intelligence (Sternberg's tacit-knowledge research) can be deployed either toward genuine goods (prudence, wisdom) or toward self-interested manipulation (craftiness) — the same cognitive skill, differently oriented, which is directly relevant to this section's pairing of prudent/shrewd with craftiness/cunning vocabulary in the same semantic field.

**Emergence — what causes wisdom to develop:**
Wisdom develops through significant life experience (particularly the navigation of adversity), structured reflection on experience (experience alone is insufficient without integration through reflection), the guidance of wise mentors, and exposure to wisdom traditions. Grossmann's research shows wisdom can be deliberately cultivated through practices of perspective-distancing — viewing one's own situation from a third-person vantage.

**Reduction and absence — what causes wisdom to fail to develop, or to curdle into mere craftiness:**
Wisdom fails to develop when experience is not reflected on and integrated, when access to wisdom traditions and mentors is absent, and when self-interest overrides the broader perspective-taking wisdom requires. The prudent/crafty boundary documented above suggests a specific failure mode worth testing in the verse evidence: the same cognitive capacity for foresight and strategic thinking, oriented toward self-interested manipulation rather than genuine good, is what the crafty/cunning vocabulary names — wisdom's skill without wisdom's proper end.

**Location — where wisdom is seated:**
Primarily cognitive and experiential, but with strong integrative dimensions — wisdom involves emotional, relational, and moral intelligence alongside factual and procedural knowledge, not any single isolable faculty.

**External influences:**
*Developmental:* Wisdom is typically associated with later life stages, though not exclusively so; early access to wisdom traditions and mentors accelerates development. *Cultural:* Cultures with living wisdom traditions transmit wisdom more effectively than those with fragmented or lost wisdom heritage. *Circumstances:* Adversity navigated well is among the most powerful wisdom-generators.

**Coverage assessment:** Rich. Wisdom research is one of the most developed areas of positive and developmental psychology.

### Section 3 — Counsel and counsellors

**Primary field:** Social psychology / organisational psychology — group decision-making and advice research

**Glosses covered:**
counsel (H4156, H4431, H5475, H5843, H6098, H8458, sumboulion G4824), counsellor (paraklētos, G3875, H3272B, H1907), counselor (sumboulos, G4825), to counsel (H3272A)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Group decision-making research (Janis's groupthink, 1972; Surowiecki's wisdom of crowds, 2004) examines how collective deliberation can both enhance and impair judgment quality depending on the conditions under which it occurs. Advice-taking research (Yaniv's egocentrism-in-advice-use studies) documents a robust and consistent tendency to discount others' advice relative to one's own judgment, even when the advisor demonstrably has superior information — directly relevant to the biblical wisdom literature's recurring contrast between the wise who seek counsel and the fool who refuses it. The counsellor relationship in organisational contexts (mentoring, coaching, advisory relationships) shows consistent positive effects on decision quality and goal achievement across a substantial applied literature.

**Emergence — what causes counsel-seeking to arise:**
Counsel-seeking arises from the recognition that one's own judgment is insufficient for the decision at hand, trust in the counsellor's wisdom and benevolence, and the humility to receive information that may challenge existing plans. Resistance to counsel — a consistent pattern in the biblical folly literature — arises from overconfidence and pride, the refusal to acknowledge the limits of one's own perspective (directly paralleling Section 1's Dunning-Kruger finding).

**Reduction and absence — what causes counsel-seeking to diminish:**
Diminished by overconfidence in one's own judgment, the absence of trustworthy advisors, and the pride that treats advice-seeking as weakness. Yaniv's egocentrism-in-advice-use finding is pervasive and robust across experimental settings — the tendency to discount good counsel is not a rare failure but a well-documented default.

**Location — where counsel is seated:**
Relational — it exists in the between-space of the counsellor-counselled relationship, not in either party alone. It has cognitive products (improved plans, better decisions) but is constituted by the relationship itself.

**External influences:**
*Access:* Access to wise counsellors is a significant social advantage — differential access to quality advisory relationships tracks broader social and economic inequality. *Cultural:* Cultures with strong mentorship traditions and elder-respect transmit counsel more effectively.

**Coverage assessment:** Moderate. Group decision-making and advice research provide genuine, directly relevant frameworks.

### Section 4 — Instruction, discipline, and the origin of wisdom

**Primary field:** Cognitive and developmental psychology — deliberate practice and moral formation

**Glosses covered:**
to teach (G2567 "teaching good", H8637), discipline (H4148G), beginning (G0746, G2602, H8462, H3246, H7221), cause (H1701, H1700), to collect (H6960B, G4816), to gain (kerdainō, H3276)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Deliberate-practice research (Ericsson and colleagues) demonstrates that durable expertise is built through structured, corrected, effortful practice rather than through unguided experience or raw exposure — directly relevant to the biblical wisdom literature's insistence that wisdom is instructed and disciplined into a person, not simply absorbed. The "beginning" vocabulary in this section maps onto the foundational claim of the wisdom tradition (Proverbs 9:10 — the fear of the LORD is the beginning of wisdom): developmental and educational psychology consistently find that foundational orienting commitments, established early, structure everything subsequently learned in a domain — a general finding (not specific to religious commitment) about how initial framing conditions shape the trajectory of a whole body of subsequent learning.

**Emergence — what causes instructed wisdom to develop, and what "beginning" orientations produce:**
Instructed wisdom develops through structured teaching, correction, and discipline sustained over time — the deliberate-practice literature's central finding is that this structured, corrected process reliably outperforms unguided experience alone. A foundational orienting commitment ("beginning") shapes how all subsequent instruction is received and integrated — learners do not process new information neutrally but through the frame their foundational commitments provide.

**Reduction and absence — what causes instructional formation to fail:**
Instructional formation fails without sustained, structured repetition and correction; unguided exposure alone produces markedly weaker competence than guided, corrected practice (Ericsson). Where the foundational orienting commitment is absent or wrongly placed, the wisdom tradition's own logic (and the general finding about framing conditions) predicts that everything built on it will be correspondingly distorted.

**Location — where instructed wisdom is seated:**
Inherently social and developmental — teaching and discipline require another party and unfold over time, in contrast to Section 2's wisdom-as-integrated-capacity, which, once formed, is seated more diffusely in the whole person.

**External influences:**
*Social:* Consistent access to a capable, invested teacher or disciplinarian is the primary precondition for instructional formation. *Developmental:* Early instruction and discipline shape the trajectory of all later learning in a domain, per the framing-conditions finding above. *Cultural:* Societies and communities vary substantially in how seriously they take structured instruction and correction as opposed to unguided experience.

**Coverage assessment:** Good. Deliberate-practice and educational-psychology research are well developed and directly applicable, though the theological specificity of "the fear of the LORD" as the beginning is addressed in the outside-science inventory below.

## Outside-science inventory

- **folly (na.val/ne.va.lah) in its moral/theological register** — the fool who says in his heart "there is no God" (Psalm 14:1) names a theological, existential stance (the denial of God as the ground of all reality and wisdom), not merely a cognitive deficit. Science addresses cognitive and judgment failure; it does not address the metaphysical folly of refusing the Creator.
- **the fear of the LORD as the beginning of wisdom** — the specific theological claim that wisdom's foundational orienting commitment must be reverential relationship with God exceeds what developmental psychology's general finding about foundational framing conditions can establish; the psychology explains why *a* beginning shapes everything after it, not that *this* beginning is the right one.
- **counsellor (paraklētos) as the Holy Spirit** — where this cluster's counsellor vocabulary includes the Paraclete (John 14–16), it is a christological/pneumatological category — the divine Counsellor — not addressed by the social psychology of human advisory relationships in Section 3.

**Observation:** M16's outside-science content is concentrated at the moral-theological and foundational-commitment ends. The cognitive and social dimensions of folly, wisdom-as-capacity, counsel-seeking, and instructional formation are all well covered by science; the deepest biblical claim — that wisdom's beginning is a right relationship to God specifically — is a theological claim psychology can describe the general shape of (foundational commitments shape everything after) without being able to adjudicate its content.

## D9/D11/D12 prompt reference notes

> These four catalogue questions (`D9.1.1`, `D9.2.1`, `D11.2.1`, `D12.1.1`) replace the retired `T7.3` prompt as of the 2026-09-17 catalogue realignment (`catalogue_version='v2-dimension-realign-20260917'`). Each is answered here from this document's science sections as the pre-analytical data input; the verse-evidence side of each question is completed at the characteristic-level answer stage, not here.

**D9.1.1 — Physiological/Neural Substrate:** Moderate. Folly/poor judgment (Section 1) is well characterised in dual-process theory as a System 1/System 2 imbalance with known prefrontal-cortex correlates; wisdom (Section 2) as an integrative capacity is not localised to any single substrate but draws on distributed executive, emotional, and social-cognitive networks. Counsel (Section 3) and instruction (Section 4) are inherently social/behavioural rather than substrate-specific.

**D9.2.1 — Generational Transmission:** Strong, especially for Section 4. Instruction and discipline are, by definition, an intergenerational transmission mechanism; wisdom traditions are explicitly transmitted across generations through mentorship (Section 2's developmental findings); foundational orienting commitments (the "beginning" vocabulary) are characteristically instilled early, generation to generation, within a family or community.

**D11.2.1 — Innate Endowment:** Partial. General intelligence and openness to experience (preconditions for wisdom development) have documented heritable components, and impulsivity/risk-tolerance (relevant to Section 1's foolishness) also shows heritability — but wisdom itself, prudence, counsel-seeking, and instructed formation are, across the literature, overwhelmingly developed rather than innate, built through experience, reflection, and structured teaching on top of an inherited cognitive substrate.

**D12.1.1 — Social/Behavioural Manifestation:** Mixed. Wisdom and counsel (Sections 2–3) are strongly cooperative/relational — wisdom's exercise typically serves a common good (Sternberg's balance theory explicitly defines it this way) and counsel is inherently a two-party relational act. Craftiness (part of Section 2) is the clear exception — the same cognitive skill redirected toward self-interested manipulation, a competitive/adversarial manifestation of otherwise-cooperative capacity. Folly (Section 1) is manifestation-neutral in itself but frequently damages cooperative relationships as a side effect (the fool who will not take counsel, per Section 3's findings).
