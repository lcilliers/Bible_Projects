## Science sections

### Section 1 — Hatred as emotional state

**Primary field:** Social psychology / affective science

**Glosses covered:**
hating (sin.ah), hated (sa.ni), to hate (sa.tam), to hate (sa.ne), to hate (se.na), hatred (mas.te.mah), hated (H8146), hating (H8135)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Sternberg's duplex theory of hate (*The Psychology of Hate*, 2003) proposes that hate is composed of three elements: negation of intimacy (disgust and revulsion that pushes the other away), passion (anger and fear in the presence of the hated object), and devaluation/diminution (contempt — cognitive devaluation of the other's worth). Different combinations produce different types of hate, from cool disdain to burning rage. Intergroup hatred research (Mackie, Devos, Smith) treats group-based hate as arising from perceived threat to in-group values, identity, or welfare. Unlike anger, which is typically activated and action-oriented, hatred is often a sustained, dispositional orientation toward its object.

**Emergence — what causes hatred to arise:**
Hatred emerges from sustained or extreme exposure to what is perceived as deeply wrong, threatening, or repugnant. It differs from anger in being more enduring and less responsive to the resolution of specific incidents — hatred becomes a stable orientation rather than a reactive episode. It typically develops through repeated aversive encounters with the hated person or group, the interpretation of those encounters through a devaluing framework, and the social reinforcement of hate within a group context. Dehumanisation is frequently a co-condition — the hated other is deprived of full human status.

**Reduction and absence — what causes hatred to diminish:**
Hatred is among the most resistant of the negative emotions to reduction. Interventions that work for anger (reappraisal, empathy induction, conflict resolution) are less effective once the negative orientation has crystallised into stable hate. Contact theory (Allport) — structured positive contact between hated groups — shows some effectiveness under conditions of equal status, cooperative goals, institutional support, and personal acquaintance. Genuine transformation of hatred typically involves a significant reappraisal of the hated person or group's humanity.

**Location — where hatred is seated:**
Primarily emotional and cognitive, with a strong motivational dimension (hatred inclines toward avoidance of or aggression toward its object). It is less somatically acute than rage but more cognitively entrenched — it involves a stable evaluative framework that organises perception of the hated other. The relational dimension is significant: hatred is always directed toward someone and shapes all perception of and interaction with that person.

**External influences:**
*Social and group context:* Hatred is rarely generated in isolation — it is typically sustained and amplified by group membership, shared narrative about the hated other, and social reward for expressing hate. *Developmental:* Parental and community modelling of hatred toward specific groups shapes children's hate formation from early childhood (social learning). *Circumstances:* Perceived threat (to identity, welfare, or status) by the hated group is the primary circumstantial driver. *Hereditary:* No specific heritability for hatred as such; underlying traits (authoritarianism, low openness, high disgust sensitivity) that predispose toward hatred have some heritable components.

**Coverage assessment:** Moderate. Hatred is less researched as a primary emotion than love, fear, or anger. Sternberg's duplex theory is the most systematic framework available.

### Section 2 — Contempt, disgust, and derision

**Primary field:** Moral psychology / emotion research

**Glosses covered:**
contempt (buz), contempt (biz.za.von), contempt (ne.a.tsah — two entries), derision (shim.tsah), derision (H3933, H7048, H7047), scorn (she.at), scorn (H7589), to despise (ba.zah), to despise (shut), to spurn (na.ats), to stink (ba.ash, H2186B), detestation (she.qets), abhorrence (de.ra.on), to abhor (bdelussomai), to abhor (ta.av, H1602, H8581, G0655), to loathe (ta.av, H6973, H8374, H2092), mockery (H2049, H4426), mocking (H3934, H4485), scorning (H3944), loathing (H1604)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Contempt and disgust are distinct emotions with different elicitors, expressions, and relational consequences. Contempt (Gottman, *Why Marriages Succeed or Fail*, 1994) is among the most destructive emotions in close relationships — it communicates the perception of the other as fundamentally inferior, beneath consideration, and not worthy of engagement. Disgust (Haidt, Rozin, *Disgust: The Body and Soul Emotion*, 2007) is rooted in contamination aversion and extends from bodily disgust to moral and social disgust. Public derision and mockery add a performative, audience-directed dimension studied in the social psychology of humiliation and ridicule (Miller, *Humiliation*, 1993) — ridicule functions as a public status-lowering act distinct from private contempt. The cluster's vocabulary spans all three registers: cold private contempt, visceral disgust, and public mockery.

**Emergence — what causes contempt, disgust, and derision to arise:**
Contempt arises from a hierarchical social evaluation in which the other is placed below the contemptuous person on a scale of worth, competence, or virtue; it does not require anger and can be entirely cold. Disgust arises from contact with, or perceived contact with, contaminating objects, people, or acts — moral disgust extends the contamination model to violations of purity norms or basic decency. The stink vocabulary (ba.ash) reflects the olfactory-disgust basis of the emotion before it extends into the moral domain. Mockery and derision add the social-performance dimension — they require an audience and are aimed at public status-lowering, not merely private evaluation.

**Reduction and absence — what causes contempt, disgust, and derision to diminish:**
Contempt is particularly resistant to reduction because it involves a stable evaluative hierarchy rather than a situational reaction; it is reduced by information that revises the hierarchical assessment or by deliberate empathy practice. Disgust reduces through habituation (the basis of exposure therapy for disgust-based phobias); moral disgust is more cognitively resistant because it is maintained by moral frameworks that define certain acts or persons as impure. Public derision ceases when the mocker loses either the audience or the social standing needed to mock credibly.

**Location — where contempt, disgust, and derision are seated:**
Contempt is primarily cognitive and social — a stable evaluative stance expressed in the face and in interaction patterns, with limited somatic intensity but profound relational consequences. Disgust is more somatically rooted, beginning in the gut and extending outward into the cognitive and moral domain. Derision and mockery are inherently relational and public — they exist in the space between the mocker, the mocked, and an observing community.

**External influences:**
*Cultural:* Contempt norms and their social targets are heavily culturally determined — who is considered beneath consideration varies across social structures (caste, class, honour-shame systems), and honour-shame cultures give public derision exceptional social force. Disgust sensitivity varies cross-culturally, though core disgust elicitors are cross-culturally consistent. *Developmental:* Contempt and derision are largely learned through social modelling; high-contempt households produce children with high contempt orientation. *Circumstances:* Status hierarchies and competition between groups increase contempt and mockery along power lines.

**Coverage assessment:** Good. Contempt and disgust are well-researched in moral psychology and emotion science; humiliation/ridicule research is thinner but directly relevant to the mockery vocabulary.

### Section 3 — Hostility, enmity, and oppression

**Primary field:** Clinical and social psychology — hostility and intergroup conflict research

**Glosses covered:**
be hostile (a.yav), hostility (G2189, H7147), opponent (antidikos, ya.riv), be an opponent (G0480), adversary (qim, H7009), to oppose (H7853, G0436, G0475, G1758), to mistreat (hubrizō, G1908), to disown (H5010), severity (H6531, G0663), irreconcilable (G0786), enemy (G2190, H0341, H6145, H6862C, H7790, H8324), to oppress (G4912, G2616, G2669), oppression (H4835), to oppress (H5065), hostile (G1727)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Aggression research (Berkowitz, *Aggression*, 1993; Bandura's social learning theory) distinguishes reactive aggression (hot, impulsive, triggered by provocation) from proactive aggression (cold, instrumental, used to achieve goals). Hostility as a personality trait — the cognitive component of the Type A behaviour pattern (Dembroski, Williams) — involves a chronic negative orientation toward others: the expectation that others will frustrate, deceive, or harm one. Intergroup conflict research (Sherif's Robbers Cave studies, 1954/1961; Tajfel and Turner's social identity theory) demonstrates that enmity between groups arises readily from competition over scarce resources and from the mere categorisation of "us" versus "them." Structural oppression research (power and social dominance theory — Sidanius and Pratto) addresses sustained, institutionalised hostility exercised by a dominant group against a subordinated one, distinct from episodic interpersonal hostility.

**Emergence — what causes hostility, enmity, and oppression to arise:**
Hostility arises from chronic frustration and unmet needs, a dispositional negative attribution style (attributing hostile intent to others' ambiguous actions), experiences of victimisation that prime vigilance for threat, and social environments that reward aggressive dominance. Enmity between defined parties (enemy vocabulary) typically consolidates through repeated conflict, competing claims over scarce resources or territory, and the categorisation of the other as belonging to a threatening out-group. Oppression specifically arises where one party holds structural power advantage and uses it to constrain, exploit, or degrade another over a sustained period — the power differential, not any single hostile act, is the defining feature.

**Reduction and absence — what causes hostility and enmity to diminish:**
Hostility reduces through secure, trustworthy relationships that revise the expectation that others are threatening, cognitive restructuring of the hostile attribution bias, and community and accountability structures that sanction aggressive behaviour. Sherif's superordinate-goals finding (Robbers Cave) remains the classic intergroup result: enmity between competing groups reduced sharply once they were given a shared goal that neither could achieve alone. Oppression ends only through the removal or redistribution of the structural power advantage that sustains it — goodwill alone does not resolve a power-based hostility.

**Location — where hostility, enmity, and oppression are seated:**
Hostility is primarily characterological — a stable cognitive orientation (hostile attribution bias, negative expectation of others) with motivational and behavioural consequences. Enmity and oppression, unlike private hatred (Section 1), are inherently relational and often structural: they name a standing relationship between two parties (or a dominant and a subordinated group) rather than a purely internal state. The internal experience they produce in the party on the receiving end — vigilance, fear, degradation — sits in the same domains as M01's coercion vocabulary.

**External influences:**
*Developmental and parenting:* Harsh, punitive, and abusive parenting is among the strongest predictors of hostile and aggressive character development. *Hereditary:* Genetic variants associated with the MAOA gene influence aggression reactivity, particularly in the context of childhood abuse. *Cultural:* Cultures with norms of honour, retaliation, and aggressive dominance show higher rates of reactive hostility. *Circumstances:* War, occupation, economic competition, and social hierarchy with no legitimate routes to status increase hostile, adversarial, and oppressive behaviour; much of the biblical material's enemy and oppression vocabulary is produced in and addressed to such contexts.

**Coverage assessment:** Good. Aggression, intergroup conflict, and structural oppression are all well-studied, though the political-science dimension of oppression exceeds psychology's individual-level frame.

### Section 4 — Cursing and covert malice

**Primary field:** Social psychology — indirect aggression and speech-act research

**Glosses covered:**
to curse (H0779, G2653, G2672, H5344B, H6895, G2551), to plot (G1917, G4945, H2050, H2665, H2790A, H4209, H4284, H5230)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Indirect and relational aggression research (Björkqvist, Lagerspetz, and Kaukiainen, 1992; Crick and Grotpeter, *Child Development*, 1995) identifies a distinct category of covert hostility — aggression pursued through concealment, manipulation, and third parties rather than direct confrontation — that is behaviourally and developmentally separable from overt physical or verbal aggression. Speech-act theory (Austin, Searle) treats cursing as a performative utterance: an act that attempts to bring about harm through the speech itself, not merely a description of ill-will. Anthropological and psychological research on imprecation and ritual cursing (Malinowski's classic fieldwork; contemporary cross-cultural surveys) documents cursing as a near-universal human practice for attempting to direct harm at an adversary when direct action is unavailable or too costly.

**Emergence — what causes cursing and plotting to arise:**
Covert hostility (plotting, scheming) arises typically where the plotter perceives themselves as unable to confront the target directly — because of a power disadvantage, social cost, or the wish to preserve deniability — and instead pursues harm indirectly, through concealment, alliance-building against the target, or manipulation of circumstance. Cursing as a speech-act arises from the same disadvantage structure: it is the recourse of one who cannot compel an outcome by force and instead invokes a hoped-for external agency (a god, fate, the community's moral order) to bring about the desired harm.

**Reduction and absence — what causes cursing and plotting to diminish:**
Covert aggression diminishes when the underlying power disadvantage is resolved (the plotter no longer needs concealment), when the scheme is exposed (removing its principal advantage), or when direct, legitimate channels for grievance become available. Cursing as a practice diminishes with the loss of belief in its efficacy and with the availability of legitimate recourse (legal, relational, or restorative) that renders invoking a third-party agency unnecessary.

**Location — where cursing and plotting are seated:**
Cursing is a speech-act — located at the boundary between the inner state (malicious intent) and its external expression (uttered words believed to carry causal force). Plotting is cognitive and behavioural — sustained, deliberate cognitive planning directed toward another's harm, concealed from its target. Both differ structurally from Sections 1–3 in being active, planned, and typically concealed rather than felt and expressed.

**External influences:**
*Social and power structure:* Covert hostility is disproportionately the recourse of the relatively powerless; open aggression is more available to those with power or social sanction to use it directly. *Cultural:* Belief in the efficacy of cursing, and the social legitimacy of ritual imprecation, vary widely across cultures and historical periods; the ancient Near Eastern and biblical worlds took curse and blessing as having real, not merely expressive, force. *Circumstances:* Prolonged rivalry, unresolved grievance, and the absence of legitimate recourse are the primary situational drivers of sustained plotting.

**Coverage assessment:** Thin to moderate. Indirect-aggression research is well developed for interpersonal and childhood/adolescent contexts; its direct application to ritual cursing and biblical imprecation is limited, and this section leans more heavily on the outside-science content noted below than most of the cluster.

## Outside-science inventory

- **adversary (qim) and opponent (antidikos) in their legal and covenantal registers** — where these terms describe a party to a covenant lawsuit or a legal adversary in the divine court, the theological structure (God as prosecuting adversary, the adversary who brings accusation before God) is outside science's sociological account of opposition.
- **hatred as divine response to evil** — science addresses hate as a human psychological phenomenon; the biblical material includes divine hatred of sin and evil as a holy, ontologically grounded response. Science does not adjudicate holy hatred.
- **cursing (H0779 and related) in its covenantal register** — where cursing names covenant curses pronounced by God or invoked as covenant sanction (Deuteronomy 27–28), the theological structure of covenant blessing-and-curse exceeds the speech-act psychology of ordinary imprecation.

**Observation:** M06's outside-science content remains concentrated at the divine-attribute and covenantal ends. Human hatred, contempt, hostility, enmity, and covert malice are all covered by science, often more thoroughly for their pathological manifestations than for the moral-relational analysis the biblical material provides. The narrowing of this cluster's scope since v1_0 (cruelty and reproach moved out; cursing and plotting moved in) shifts its centre of gravity slightly further from individual emotional pathology and further toward relational and structural enmity — a shift Session B should note when comparing this review against the cluster's sub-group structure.

## D9/D11/D12 prompt reference notes

> These four catalogue questions (`D9.1.1`, `D9.2.1`, `D11.2.1`, `D12.1.1`) replace the retired `T7.3` prompt as of the 2026-09-17 catalogue realignment (`catalogue_version='v2-dimension-realign-20260917'`). Each is answered here from this document's science sections as the pre-analytical data input; the verse-evidence side of each question is completed at the characteristic-level answer stage, not here.

**D9.1.1 — Physiological/Neural Substrate:** Neuroscience and social psychology converge on hatred and hostility sharing much of the same threat/arousal circuitry as fear and anger (amygdala, sympathetic activation), with contempt distinguished by its comparatively low somatic intensity and high cognitive/evaluative stability (Gottman). Cursing and plotting have no direct neural-substrate literature of their own — they are better characterised as planned, cognitively-mediated acts than as arousal states. Verse evidence should be tested for whether the cluster's vocabulary carries the hot, aroused register (hatred, hostility) or the cold, calculated register (contempt, plotting, cursing).

**D9.2.1 — Generational Transmission:** Well-attested. Parental and community modelling of hatred, contempt, and enmity toward specific groups shapes children's orientations from early childhood (social learning); MAOA-gene variants interact with childhood abuse to elevate aggression reactivity across generations. Intergroup enmity in particular is transmitted through shared narrative and inherited grievance rather than through any single generation's direct experience — worth testing against the cluster's own generational/inherited-enmity language (e.g. enemy status inherited across generations in the biblical material).

**D11.2.1 — Innate Endowment:** Underlying dispositional traits that predispose toward hatred and hostility (low openness, high disgust sensitivity, authoritarianism, trait hostility) have documented heritable components, but hatred, contempt, enmity, cursing, and plotting as such are not treated in the literature as innate endowments — they are constructed through experience, social learning, and structural conditions acting on a heritable temperamental substrate. This bears on D11.1's created-design/fallen-condition question: science does not support treating malice itself as part of the original created endowment, only the capacity for strong evaluative and defensive response that malice distorts.

**D12.1.1 — Social/Behavioural Manifestation:** Overwhelmingly competitive rather than cooperative. Hatred, contempt, hostility, enmity, oppression, cursing, and plotting are, across every section of this review, other-directed and adversarial — they function to exclude, degrade, subordinate, or harm a target, whether openly (Sections 1–3) or covertly (Section 4). The one partial exception is Sherif's superordinate-goals finding: shared cooperative purpose is the documented mechanism by which intergroup enmity is reduced, which may be worth testing against any biblical material in this cluster that shows enmity yielding to shared purpose.
