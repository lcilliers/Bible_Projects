## Science sections

### Section 1 — Contamination, impurity, and disgust psychology

**Primary field:** Moral psychology — contamination and disgust research

**Glosses covered:**
defilement (miasmos, molusmos, G0234, G3393, H1352), impurity (akatharsia, nid.dah, H5079), to defile (molunō, ta.me, G3435, G4510, H2930A, H1351), unclean (akathartos, ta.me, H2931), refuse (H6292)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Rozin and Haidt's disgust research (*Disgust: The Body and Soul Emotion*, in Lewis and Haviland-Jones eds., 2008) establishes contamination sensitivity as an evolved response originally tied to pathogen avoidance (rotting food, bodily waste, disease vectors) that has been extended, across cultures, into the moral and social domain — "moral disgust," where acts or persons are perceived as contaminating even without literal biological contagion risk. Haidt's moral foundations theory identifies purity/sanctity as one of the primary foundations of moral judgment across cultures, operating partly independently of harm-based moral reasoning — people frequently judge an act as wrong because it is "impure" or "degrading" even when they cannot identify a victim it harmed. Douglas's classic anthropological account (*Purity and Danger*, 1966) treats ritual purity systems as ways that a culture orders and stabilises its conceptual categories — impurity names what falls outside or between a culture's ordering categories, which is directly relevant to the biblical purity system's own logic of ordered versus disordered categories.

**Emergence — what causes the perception of defilement and impurity to arise:**
Contamination perception arises through contact (real or symbolic) with a source classified as impure — bodily fluids, corpses, disease, or, in the extended moral-disgust register, acts and persons perceived as violating purity norms. Douglas's anthropological account adds that impurity classifications frequently mark boundary-crossing or category-violation specifically — matter or behaviour that does not fit a culture's ordering system is what gets classified as unclean, more than any single objective property of the thing itself.

**Reduction and absence — what causes the sense of defilement to diminish:**
Contamination sensitivity reduces through ritual or physical cleansing (a near-universal human practice — washing, purification rites), through habituation to previously disgusting stimuli (the basis of exposure-based treatment for contamination-focused OCD), and, for moral disgust specifically, through reframing that removes the perceived category violation. Purity systems characteristically provide a specified mechanism for restoration to cleanness — the psychological function of such mechanisms is well documented: without a path back to "clean" status, contamination anxiety tends to become chronic and generalising (a pattern clinically visible in contamination-based OCD, where no ritual ever feels sufficient).

**Location — where the sense of defilement is seated:**
Disgust in its core form is somatic (gut-level, often literally nauseating) before it is cognitive; moral disgust extends this bodily-rooted response into evaluative judgment about persons and acts. It therefore spans body and mind more directly than most of this programme's characteristics — the biblical purity vocabulary's insistence on both physical and moral impurity within a single semantic field (ta.me covers both) is consistent with the science finding that these two registers of disgust share a common psychological root rather than being unrelated phenomena that happen to use similar language.

**External influences:**
*Cultural:* What counts as defiling, and the specific rituals for its removal, are extensively culturally shaped, though the underlying disgust response and its capacity for moral extension appear to be human universals (Rozin, Haidt). *Developmental:* Disgust sensitivity and its specific triggers are substantially shaped by childhood socialisation — parental modelling of disgust responses teaches children what counts as contaminating within their culture. *Circumstances:* Contact with death, disease, and bodily waste are near-universal disgust elicitors; the specific social and religious extensions vary.

**Coverage assessment:** Good. Contamination psychology and moral-foundations research on purity/sanctity are both well developed and directly applicable to this cluster's core vocabulary.

### Section 2 — Profanation of the sacred

**Primary field:** Psychology of religion / sociology of the sacred

**Glosses covered:**
to pollute (H2610), profaneness (H2612, H2613)

**Landmark findings and key researchers:**
Durkheim's foundational sacred/profane distinction (*The Elementary Forms of Religious Life*, 1912) treats the sacred as categorically set apart from ordinary (profane) use — profanation is specifically the act of treating what belongs to the sacred category as though it were ordinary, or introducing the ordinary/contaminating into the sacred space. Pargament's sanctification research (psychology of religion) documents that objects, places, relationships, and activities perceived as sacred are treated with a distinct psychological seriousness — violation of their set-apart status produces a qualitatively different, often more severe, response than an equivalent violation of an ordinary object or space.

**Emergence — what causes profanation to arise:**
Profanation arises when the boundary between the sacred and the ordinary is treated as unimportant or is deliberately transgressed — through carelessness, ignorance of the boundary, or intentional violation. Because sacred status is itself a socially and religiously constituted category (Durkheim), profanation is possible only within a community that recognises and maintains that category in the first place.

**Reduction and absence — what causes profanation to diminish:**
Reduced by clear community maintenance of the sacred/ordinary boundary (instruction, ritual reinforcement, visible consequence for violation) and by the psychological seriousness with which sanctified objects and spaces are held by the community (Pargament's sanctification research finds that perceived sanctification is one of the strongest predictors of protective, careful treatment of the object or relationship in question).

**Location — where profanation is seated:**
Social and relational at root — it depends on a shared, community-held category (the sacred) rather than existing as a purely private psychological state, though the individual act of profaning or the individual distress at witnessing profanation is, of course, personally experienced.

**External influences:**
*Religious and cultural:* The existence and strength of a sacred/profane distinction is entirely dependent on a community's religious and cultural framework; without shared maintenance of the category, "profanation" ceases to be a meaningful violation. *Developmental:* Formation within a religious community teaches what is sacred and what response its violation calls for.

**Coverage assessment:** Thin to moderate. Two glosses only; the sociology and psychology of religion's sacred/profane framework provides a genuine but not extensively developed literature for this specific vocabulary.

## Outside-science inventory

- **unclean (ta.me) in its cultic-ritual register** — the specific mechanics of the Levitical purity system (what defiles, for how long, and by what rite it is removed) is a theologically and legally structured system that contamination psychology can illuminate at the level of underlying human disgust response but cannot generate or adjudicate as a specific divine instruction.
- **defilement in its moral-covenantal register** — where impurity names Israel's covenant unfaithfulness specifically (the land "defiled" by sin, per prophetic usage), the covenantal-causal structure is a theological category beyond contamination psychology's account of moral disgust.
- **profaneness (H2612/H2613)** where it names the desecration of what God himself has specifically set apart (holy objects, the sanctuary, the divine name) — the theological ground of that set-apart status (God's own declared holiness) is not a category sociology of the sacred can establish; it can only describe how communities that hold such a belief behave.

**Observation:** M10c's outside-science content is proportionally large for its size — a genuinely small cluster whose core vocabulary sits close to a specifically cultic-legal system. The underlying human psychology of contamination, disgust, and sacred/profane boundary-maintenance is nonetheless well covered by science and directly illuminates why the biblical purity vocabulary has the shape, severity, and remedy-structure that it does.

## D9/D11/D12 prompt reference notes

> These four catalogue questions (`D9.1.1`, `D9.2.1`, `D11.2.1`, `D12.1.1`) replace the retired `T7.3` prompt as of the 2026-09-17 catalogue realignment (`catalogue_version='v2-dimension-realign-20260917'`). Each is answered here from this document's science sections as the pre-analytical data input; the verse-evidence side of each question is completed at the characteristic-level answer stage, not here.

**D9.1.1 — Physiological/Neural Substrate:** Well documented for core disgust: the insula is consistently identified across neuroimaging studies as the primary substrate for both physical and moral disgust, and the facial expression of disgust is cross-culturally universal (Ekman). This is one of the more neurally well-characterised characteristics in this programme's science coverage. Profanation (Section 2) has no distinct substrate literature beyond the general disgust/insula finding, since it is best characterised as a socially-mediated violation rather than a direct contamination-contact response.

**D9.2.1 — Generational Transmission:** Well documented. Disgust sensitivity and its specific cultural triggers are substantially transmitted through childhood socialisation and parental modelling (Rozin's developmental disgust research); what a community treats as sacred, and therefore what counts as profanation, is likewise transmitted generationally through religious formation (Section 2).

**D11.2.1 — Innate Endowment:** Strong support for the substrate, weaker for the specific content. The core disgust response itself is a well-evidenced evolved, innate endowment (pathogen-avoidance function, present cross-culturally and cross-species in related forms); its extension into moral and sacred/profane territory is culturally and religiously shaped rather than innate. This is a useful case for D11.1: the underlying capacity is created/innate, but its specific object (what counts as unclean or profane) is not built in — it must be taught.

**D12.1.1 — Social/Behavioural Manifestation:** Primarily boundary-maintaining rather than clearly cooperative or competitive. Defilement and profanation name violations of a shared category boundary (clean/unclean, sacred/ordinary) that a community collectively maintains; the behavioural response (avoidance, exclusion, ritual remedy) functions to protect the community's shared categories rather than to compete with or cooperate toward an external goal in the way most other clusters' D12.1.1 answers do.
