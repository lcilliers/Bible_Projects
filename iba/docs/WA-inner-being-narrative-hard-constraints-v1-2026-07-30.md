## The task

Build a narrative — a piece of writing for a general, non-technical, non-scholarly reader — about
how the human inner being works and interacts: with itself, with other people, with non-human
beings, and with the objects and situations around it, **as shown in the book named below, across
every passage supplied.**

This is not a summary of the book's plot and not a theological essay. It is an account of the
*inner life* the text actually depicts — what moves people, what they turn toward or away from,
what overwhelms them and what restores them, what they say about their own hearts or others say
about their hearts, what fear and courage and self-importance and understanding look like when the
text names them directly, and where the text simply says nothing at all.

## The one and only source of material

Every passage-debate document supplied below, covering the book named, already contains the
finished analysis this narrative must be built from. Read all of them in full before writing
anything — they are long, and the material worth weaving into the narrative is distributed across
the whole of each document, not just its headline verses.

Each debate document has the same internal shape:

- A per-verse walkthrough: what the text states, who is doing or undergoing what, where it comes
  from and where it's directed, and a decision on whether it was kept as real inner-being content,
  set aside, or logged as a deliberate silence (the text names a candidate but says nothing about
  their interior).
- A **"Passage-level linkages"** section — the threads and echoes the chapter's own vocabulary makes
  *within itself* (and sometimes back to earlier chapters).
- An **"Insufficiencies register"** — places where the underlying word-data genuinely can't settle a
  question.
- An **"Emergent questions log"** — open questions the chapter itself raised, explicitly left
  unresolved.
- An **"Open decisions"** section, including any known gaps in the underlying verse data for that
  chapter.

Everything the narrative draws on should trace back to one of these. **Do not draw on the raw
lexical/Strong's-number extract files** (the similarly-named `-verse-span-meaning.md` files) — those
are raw word-level data the debate documents have already digested; going back to them risks
introducing claims the debates themselves didn't make. Only the debate documents supplied are the
source.

## Hard constraints — read carefully, these are not optional

1. **Nothing invented.** Every claim in the narrative must be traceable to something a debate
   document actually states — either as retained content, an explicitly named silence/absence, an
   insufficiency, or an emergent question. Do not add psychological interpretation, historical
   background, or connections between chapters that the debate documents didn't already draw
   themselves.
2. **Open threads stay open, in the writing.** Where a debate flagged something as uncertain,
   unresolved, or silent, say so plainly in the narrative itself — as a real "we don't know" or "the
   text doesn't say," not smoothed over, not quietly dropped, and not resolved by guessing. These
   moments are as much a part of the narrative as the clear ones.
3. **Contradictions stand.** If different passages (or different figures within one passage) show
   the inner being working in tension or outright opposite ways, let both stand side by side. Do not
   force a resolution or a single moral.
4. **No forced unity.** The finished piece does not need to be one integrated argument or a single
   throughline. It is allowed to be organized as a set of distinct threads, observations, or
   fragments, in whatever order or grouping best serves the reader. A fragmented result is an
   acceptable, expected result — not a failure to synthesize.
5. **Nothing is off the table.** Minor or marginal threads deserve inclusion too, even if only a
   sentence — the instruction is not to select only the most dramatic material.
6. **General-reader language.** Write as if for someone who has never studied biblical Hebrew, never
   heard of Strong's numbers, and has no interest in how the analysis was produced. Plain, direct,
   accessible prose.
7. **No self-reference to the method or the project.** Do not write "this study," "this project,"
   "the debates," "the passage-debate documents," "our analysis," "we found," or any internal
   vocabulary from the source documents (action-type, Q7, recorded silence, insufficiency register,
   node, Strong's code, root, etc.). The narrative should read as an account of what the book's text
   shows — full stop — not as a report about how it was studied. Where you need to signal
   uncertainty, use plain language ("the text doesn't say," "it's left unclear," "we're not told
   why").

## Suggested approach (guidance, not a rigid procedure)

1. Read every supplied debate document in full, in passage order.
2. While reading, keep track of recurring threads that cut across passages — what happens when
   someone's heart is described as lifted up or exalted; what happens when someone turns their face
   toward or away from another; what collapse and restoration look like, and what triggers each;
   what fear does and what follows it; what it means for understanding to be granted, withheld, or
   admitted absent; how flattery and deception operate on people; what non-human figures (named or
   unnamed) do to or for the humans around them; what objects and settings act upon a person's inner
   state — not an exhaustive or prescriptive list, just a starting point.
3. Also track what's genuinely singular — a moment in one passage only, not part of a bigger
   pattern — since those deserve a place too, not just the recurring threads.
4. Decide on a structure that serves the material rather than imposing one — thread-by-thread is one
   reasonable option, but it's a judgment call, not a requirement.
5. Write the narrative in plain language, folding in open questions, absences, and contradictions as
   they arise rather than appending them as a separate "limitations" section at the end (though a
   closing note on what's left genuinely unresolved is reasonable if it helps the reader).
6. Close with a `## Scope self-check` section per `WA-inner-being-narrative-guidance-v1-2026-07-28.md`
   §3 — required, not optional, on top of everything above.

## What "done" looks like

A piece of writing a general reader could pick up and follow without any prior knowledge of the
book, of Hebrew or Greek, or of how this material was produced — that shows them, honestly and
without padding, what the book says and doesn't say about how a person's inner life moves, connects,
and is acted upon.
