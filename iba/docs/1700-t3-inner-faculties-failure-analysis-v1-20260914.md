# Escalation #1700 — T3 (Inner Faculties): understanding a failed question, not just that it failed

**Scope note:** all 33 T3 questions are retired (`deleted=1`), per the researcher's own verdict,
escalation #1598 Phase D, 2026-09-08 — six days before this session. This is not a live-quality
check; it's reading the actual historical answers to understand *how* the retirement verdict plays
out in real content, at the researcher's request ("I am also interested on getting an
understanding of the failed questions").

**Method:** full read of every distinct non-blob answer to T3.1.1 (132 answers, `Perception`
component, the first and largest question in the section) — not sampled.

## The retirement verdict, verbatim

> *"Faculties are real but not directly derivable from any verse; the terminology is foreign to
> the Bible; no individual faculty exists or operates on its own — it may only emerge from a
> collection of characteristics' own operations."*

## What that looks like in the actual answers

**1. Near-universal "yes," reached only through heavy interpretive construction.** Of 132 answers,
the overwhelming majority conclude the characteristic "engages the perceptive faculty" — but
getting there requires treating almost any use of a sense-adjacent word or concept as evidence:
"the eyes going whoring after idols" (Eze 6:9, CHAR-4 idolatry), "the bad eye... is the perceptive
faculty corrupted at its root" (Matt 6:23), "God's perception... is one of moral revulsion" (Eze
36:17), "the palate as discernment metaphor" (Job 6:30) applied to *justice*, not taste. The
question doesn't discriminate between characteristics that genuinely turn on sensory/perceptual
language and ones where a perceptual reading has to be built on top of the verse rather than found
in it. When a category can be made to fit almost anything with enough interpretive effort, it isn't
doing real analytical work — this is the "not directly derivable from any verse" problem, visible
directly in the answers rather than just asserted.

**2. Explicit admissions the fit doesn't hold, scattered throughout.** Several answers say so
plainly rather than forcing a yes: *"Silent — gentleness is not perceptively triggered... no
dominant perceptive trigger evidenced"* (M05); *"the despair vocabulary does not directly engage
the perceptive faculty"* (M20); *"the discouragement vocabulary does not engage the perceptive
faculty explicitly"* (M20). These are the honest cases — and they're evidence the category itself,
not the answerer, is the problem: a well-formed analytical question shouldn't need this many
legitimate "doesn't apply" verdicts across ordinary characteristics.

**3. Faculty boundaries bleed into each other constantly — the clearest direct evidence for "no
individual faculty exists on its own."** *"M46-C does not explicitly engage the perceptive faculty.
The wisdom tradition operates through the understanding and evaluative faculties (T3.2) rather than
through perception directly"* — the answerer has to actively adjudicate which of several
overlapping faculty categories a verse belongs to, inconsistently, verse by verse. *"M15-D IS a
form of perception — discernment is constitutively perceptive"* directly contradicts the M46-C
answer's own separation of perception from discernment/understanding. *"M15-H is a diagnostic of
perception, not itself perception"* — a third, still different boundary drawn for a related case.
Three different lines drawn in three different places for what should be the same conceptual
boundary is exactly what "no individual faculty exists or operates on its own" predicts.

**4. The answers are, on their own terms, well-written.** This isn't a quality-of-writing problem —
every one of these is verse-cited, specific, and often genuinely insightful as biblical-theological
commentary (e.g. the M08 cluster's consistent "pride closes a specific perceptive channel" reading
across five characteristics is a real, coherent interpretive thread). The failure is at the
category level: a well-executed analytical process was pointed at a category that doesn't cleanly
carve the material, and the effort produced sophisticated prose papering over an unstable
distinction rather than a wrong distinction poorly described.

## What this suggests, not decided here

If "was the question well-posed" is a separate axis worth tracking alongside "was the answer
well-argued" — T3.1.1 is a case where every individual answer could score well on the second axis
while the section as a whole rightly failed on the first. That's a real, useful distinction if the
researcher wants a template for evaluating other sections (including ones not yet retired) going
forward — not proposed as a ready-made rubric here, just named as what this reading surfaced.
