# What Joel Shows Us About the Inner Life

Joel is a short book, but it moves through nearly every kind of inner weather a person can pass through — dread, denial, grief commanded and grief simply felt, hollow bravado, genuine hope held without guarantee, jealousy and pity in the same breath, terror and shelter from the very same voice. It also, just as tellingly, goes silent at exactly the points where a reader might most want to know what someone felt. Both the noise and the silence are part of what the book actually shows.

## A word arrives, and a voice goes quiet until the very end

The book opens with almost no ceremony: "the word of the LORD... came to Joel." What that felt like to Joel — receiving it, being singled out for it — is never said. He disappears into being simply the voice through which everything else is spoken. He does not speak again in his own first-person voice until the seventeenth verse of a very long crisis, when, watching fire consume even the wild pastures, he finally says: "To you, O LORD, I call." That's the only time in the whole opening chapter that a single named person turns to God on his own, rather than as part of a crowd being told what to do. And immediately, in the very next line, the wild animals do something startlingly similar — they "pant for you," using the same word of direct address Joel had just used of himself. The chapter's last two verses put a human being and an animal side by side, both crying out to the same God out of the same devastation. Nothing more is said about either cry; it's left to stand as it is.

## A catastrophe passed down, and nothing left to pass down

Before the book even describes what happened, it commands that it be told and retold: tell your children, and let your children tell their children, and their children another generation — four generations, named one after another. Then, in the very next verse, the same four-link chain structure is used again, but for the opposite purpose: what one wave of locusts left, the next wave ate, and what that one left, the next ate, until nothing remained. The text builds the same shape twice — once for memory being handed on, once for a harvest being erased — and lets the contrast do its own work. There will be children to tell. There may be nothing left over to show them.

## Grief assigned to whoever owns the loss

Rather than describing one general sorrow, the opening chapter moves from group to group, giving each its own reason to grieve. The drunkards are told to wake up and wail because the wine itself has vanished from their mouths. The farmers and vinedressers are told to feel ashamed because the wheat and barley they tended have failed. The priests are shown actually mourning — not commanded to, but simply doing it — because there is no longer any grain or wine to offer at the temple; this is the chapter's first grief that is narrated as already happening rather than only demanded. Later they're commanded to intensify it further: put on sackcloth, go in, spend the whole night in mourning dress. Each group's sorrow is tied to what specifically belongs to them — their drink, their crop, their worship — not to one shared, undifferentiated feeling.

## The ground itself is said to mourn

One of the stranger and more striking moves in the book is that the land is given the same word used of the priests' grief. "The priests mourn" (1:9), and one verse later, "the ground mourns" (1:10) — the very same verb, applied first to human beings and then, without any comment on the strangeness of it, to soil. Fields, vines, fig trees, pomegranate, palm, and apple are all said to "dry up" or "languish," and then, in the same sentence and the same breath, so does something plainly human: "gladness dries up from the children of man" (1:12). The text doesn't pause to explain how a feeling and a crop can wither by the same word — it simply lets that word do both jobs. Whether this is meant as more than a literary habit, the text itself doesn't say.

## The beasts, left unexplained but not left unfelt

The animals get real attention in their own right, not only as property lost to their owners. "How the beasts groan! The herds of cattle are perplexed... even the flocks of sheep suffer" (1:18) — described with the same kind of emotional language elsewhere used of human distress. Later, when the crisis lifts, they are told directly: "Fear not, you beasts of the field," because the pastures have turned green again (2:22) — the very same reassurance just given to "the land" and, before that, to the human community. The text never explains what an animal's fear or relief consists of; it simply states that they happen, and treats them as real.

## An alarm, a trembling commanded, and the cosmos that later does it for real

The second chapter opens with a trumpet blast and a command: "let all the inhabitants of the land tremble." At that point it's only a command — an anticipated fear, not yet a described fact. Later, in that same chapter, "the earth quakes" (2:10), using the identical word that had been used to command the people's trembling. It's as though the cosmos itself goes on to do, as plain fact, what the people had only been told to brace themselves for. In between, "peoples are in anguish; all faces grow pale" (2:6) — the clearest moment yet of a human crowd's fear being stated outright rather than commanded or inferred, though exactly who these "peoples" are — the same inhabitants named earlier, or some wider set of onlooking nations — the text doesn't clarify.

## A house entered, and no one inside given a voice

At the most intimate point of the invasion described in chapter two — soldiers or locusts (the text keeps this identity genuinely unclear) climbing into houses and slipping in through windows "like a thief" — nothing at all is said about what it felt like to be inside. This is the single clearest silence of the whole passage: an image built entirely around the violation of someone's home, and yet the people who lived there are never given so much as a line.

## Hearts, not garments

After all that devastation, the tone changes sharply. God speaks directly: "return to me with all your heart... rend your hearts and not your garments" (2:12-13). The line draws a hard, explicit line between an inward turning and a merely outward show of grief — torn clothing was the customary sign of mourning, and the text specifically says that isn't what's wanted. Alongside the command comes an unusually direct description of God's own character — "gracious and merciful, slow to anger, and abounding in steadfast love" — offered as the very reason a person might dare to hope. And the hope that follows is careful not to presume too much: "who knows whether he will not turn and relent" (2:14) — not a guarantee, but an admitted uncertainty held onto anyway.

## Even a wedding interrupted, and no word of how that felt

Once the community is called together to fast and assemble, the summons is remarkably specific about who must come: elders, children, even nursing infants — and, pointedly, "let the bridegroom leave his room, and the bride her chamber" (2:16). Of everyone named in that list, the bridegroom and bride are singled out because their situation is the furthest thing imaginable from public mourning — a wedding, private joy — and they are told to leave it behind for the sake of the whole community's crisis. What that cost them, or how willingly they gave it, is never once mentioned.

## The priests weep, and also argue

The priests are given the fullest, most complete moment of stated inner life in the whole book. Positioned "between the vestibule and the altar," they weep — but they also reason, out loud, giving God a specific argument for why he should act: "why should they say among the peoples, 'Where is their God?'" (2:17). It's grief and argument together, not one standing in for the other. And directly after it — though the text never uses the word "because" to connect the two — God's own disposition changes: "then the LORD became jealous for his land and had pity on his people" (2:18). Whether that sequence means the priests' weeping caused God's response, or simply came right before it, is something the text lets the reader wonder about without settling it.

## The same word for turning toward and turning back

Something quietly deliberate seems to be happening with the word for "return." The people are told to return to God (2:12); immediately after, the possibility is raised that God himself might "turn and relent" (2:14) — the identical word, first commanded of the humans, then held open, uncertain, of God. That same word turns up again in the following chapter, twice: once for God restoring Judah's fortunes (3:1), and then, remarkably, for a threat — God promising to "return" the nations' own cruelty onto their own heads (3:4, repeated word for word at 3:7). One word, doing entirely different work depending on who it's aimed at. The text doesn't explain the connection; it just keeps using the word.

## Everyone, by name

When the promised restoration finally comes, it arrives in a torrent of specific, individually named recipients: sons and daughters will prophesy, old men will dream dreams, young men will see visions (2:28) — and then, almost as an afterthought marked "even," male and female servants too (2:29), the people with the least standing of anyone in the list. Nothing is left as one vague "everyone." Each group is named, deliberately, the same way chapter one had assigned each group its own particular grief. What it is actually like to prophesy, or to dream in this way, is never described from the inside — only promised as something that will happen to them.

## A boy for a prostitute, a girl for wine

Then, in the final chapter, the book turns to describe what the surrounding nations did to Judah, and it names, in passing, two children: "they have traded a boy for a prostitute, and have sold a girl for wine and have drunk it" (3:3). Nothing else is said about either child — not a name, not a single word about what they felt or endured. They appear in the text only as the currency of someone else's appetite. This is, of the whole book's many silences, the starkest: two real children, mentioned and then left with no interior at all, only a fate. Later, God promises to do the very same thing back — sell the nations' own sons and daughters into distant slavery (3:8) — using the identical word for "sold." The punishment is shaped exactly like the crime. Whether the text means for us to feel any unease that Judah itself is named as the hand carrying this reversal out, it never says; it simply states the reversal and moves on.

## Terror for some, shelter for others, from the same voice

Toward the book's end, the LORD "roars from Zion... and the heavens and earth quake" — and in the very next clause, without any qualification: "but the LORD is a refuge to his people, a stronghold to the people of Israel" (3:16). One voice, one source, two entirely different effects depending on who is standing where. The book doesn't resolve or soften this — it simply states both as true at once.

## Nothing escapes, and then, someone does

Right at the start of the invasion, "nothing escapes them" (2:3) — total, inescapable ruin. By the book's close, that word returns, reversed: "there shall be those who escape" (2:32). But in that same verse another line sits close beside it, unreconciled: "everyone who calls on the name of the LORD shall be saved," and then, just after, "among the survivors shall be those whom the LORD calls" — the same word, "calls," used first of anyone's own free appeal and then of God's own act of choosing. The text never explains how those two ideas — anyone who calls, and those specifically called — fit together. It leaves them standing side by side.

## What the book itself leaves open

Across all three chapters, certain things are simply never settled, and the text doesn't pretend otherwise. Whether the destroying force throughout is meant as a literal army, a locust plague, or deliberately both at once is never resolved, even after God calls it "my great army, which I sent" (2:25). One verse — 1:15, the very hinge between the people being told to cry out and their cry beginning — is entirely missing from the available text; its content (about "the day of the LORD" drawing near) is known from elsewhere, but its absence here means the exact moment of that turn can't be traced. Whether the priests' weeping caused God's change of heart, or merely preceded it, is left for the reader to wonder about. And the fate and feeling of the besieged household, the bridegroom and bride pulled from their private joy, and above all the trafficked boy and girl, are none of them given so much as a line describing what any of it was like from the inside. The book is willing to name devastation and restoration in enormous, sweeping detail — and just as willing, at its most personal moments, to say nothing at all.

## Scope self-check

- **Non-human ↔ human:** God's Spirit is described as poured out directly "on all flesh," producing specific, named interior effects in specific human groups — sons and daughters prophesying, old men dreaming dreams, young men seeing visions, and finally even male and female servants (Joel 2:28-29). This is a non-human being's action landing directly inside human experience, not merely an example set for humans to reason about.
- **Human ↔ human:** The opening command to "tell your children... let your children tell their children" (Joel 1:3) has one generation's memory of the catastrophe deliberately transferred, link by link, into the next — a chain of human beings each becoming the source of what the next one carries, continuing across four named generations.
- **Physical world ↔ human:** The devastation of crops and trees is described as producing, in the very same sentence and by the same verb, an interior loss in people themselves: "gladness dries up from the children of man" (Joel 1:12) — the physical world's ordinary collapse shown determining a human being's own felt joy, not standing apart from it.
