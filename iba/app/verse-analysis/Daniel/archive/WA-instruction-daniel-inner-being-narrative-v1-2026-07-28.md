**Purpose:** self-contained brief for a new session. Read this file, then execute the task described below. No other context from any prior session is needed or assumed.

## The task

Build a narrative — a piece of writing for a general, non-technical, non-scholarly reader — about how the human inner being works and interacts: with itself, with other people, with non-human beings, and with the objects and situations around it, **as shown in the book of Daniel, chapters 1 through 12.**

This is not a summary of the book's plot and not a theological essay. It is an account of the *inner life* the text actually depicts — what moves people, what they turn toward or away from, what overwhelms them and what restores them, what they say about their own hearts or others say about their hearts, what fear and courage and self-importance and understanding look like when the text names them directly, and where the text simply says nothing at all.

Together these cover Daniel 1:1 through 12:13 with no gaps. Read all sixteen in full before writing anything — they are long, and the material worth weaving into the narrative is distributed across the whole of each document, not just its headline verses.

Each debate document has the same internal shape:

- A per-verse walkthrough: what the text states, who is doing or undergoing what, where it comes from and where it's directed, and a decision on whether it was kept as real inner-being content, set aside, or logged as a deliberate silence (the text names a candidate but says nothing about their interior).
- A **"Passage-level linkages"** section — the threads and echoes the chapter's own vocabulary makes *within itself* (and sometimes back to earlier chapters).
- An **"Insufficiencies register"** — places where the underlying word-data genuinely can't settle a question.
- An **"Emergent questions log"** — open questions the chapter itself raised, explicitly left unresolved.
- An **"Open decisions"** section, including any known gaps in the underlying verse data for that chapter.

Everything the narrative draws on should trace back to one of these. **Do not open the underlying lexical/Strong's-number extract files** (the similarly-named `dan-N-...-verse-span-meaning.md` files) — those are raw word-level data the debate documents have already digested; going back to them risks introducing claims the debates themselves didn't make.

## Hard constraints — read carefully, these are not optional

1. **Nothing invented.** Every claim in the narrative must be traceable to something a debate document actually states — either as retained content, an explicitly named silence/absence, an insufficiency, or an emergent question. Do not add psychological interpretation, historical background, or connections between chapters that the debate documents didn't already draw themselves.
2. **Open threads stay open, in the writing.** Where a debate flagged something as uncertain, unresolved, or silent, say so plainly in the narrative itself — as a real "we don't know" or "the text doesn't say," not smoothed over, not quietly dropped, and not resolved by guessing. These moments are as much a part of the narrative as the clear ones.
3. **Contradictions stand.** If different chapters (or different figures within one chapter) show the inner being working in tension or outright opposite ways — hearts lifted up in pride versus hearts set to understand and humbled, understanding granted versus understanding withheld, the same word used for opposite ends — let both stand side by side. Do not force a resolution or a single moral.
4. **No forced unity.** The finished piece does not need to be one integrated argument or a single throughline. It is allowed to be organized as a set of distinct threads, observations, or fragments, in whatever order or grouping best serves the reader. A fragmented result is an acceptable, expected result — not a failure to synthesize.
5. **Nothing is off the table.** Minor or marginal threads deserve inclusion too, even if only a sentence — the instruction is not to select only the most dramatic material.
6. **General-reader language.** Write as if for someone who has never studied biblical Hebrew, never heard of Strong's numbers, and has no interest in how the analysis was produced. Plain, direct, accessible prose.
7. **No self-reference to the method or the project.** Do not write "this study," "this project," "the debates," "the passage-debate documents," "our analysis," "we found," or any internal vocabulary from the source documents (action-type, Q7, recorded silence, insufficiency register, node, Strong's code, root, etc.). The narrative should read as an account of what Daniel's text shows — full stop — not as a report about how it was studied. Where you need to signal uncertainty, use plain language ("the text doesn't say," "it's left unclear," "we're not told why").

## Suggested approach (adjust as needed — this is guidance, not a rigid procedure)

1. Read all sixteen debate documents in full, in chapter order.
2. While reading, keep track of recurring threads that cut across chapters — for example (not exhaustive, not prescriptive): what happens when someone's heart is described as lifted up or exalted; what happens when someone turns their face toward or away from another; what collapse and restoration look like, and what triggers each; what fear does and what follows it; what it means for understanding to be granted, withheld, or admitted absent; how flattery and deception operate on people; what non-human figures (named or unnamed) do to or for the humans around them; what objects and settings (a written scroll, a throne, a furnace, a den, a garment) act upon a person's inner state.
3. Also track what's genuinely singular — a moment in one chapter only, not part of a bigger pattern — since those deserve a place too, not just the recurring threads.
4. Decide on a structure that serves the material rather than imposing one — thread-by-thread is one reasonable option, but it's a judgment call, not a requirement.
5. Write the narrative in plain language, folding in open questions, absences, and contradictions as they arise rather than appending them as a separate "limitations" section at the end (though a closing note on what's left genuinely unresolved is reasonable if it helps the reader).
6. Save the finished piece as a single markdown file. Suggested location: `iba/app/verse-analysis/Daniel/` alongside its source material, with a filename that identifies it as the narrative output (exact name is your call, following the folder's existing naming conventions).

## What "done" looks like

A piece of writing a general reader could pick up and follow without any prior knowledge of Daniel, of Hebrew, or of how this material was produced — that shows them, honestly and without padding, what the book of Daniel says and doesn't say about how a person's inner life moves, connects, and is acted upon.
