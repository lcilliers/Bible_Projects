# Hosea 1-14 — individual-named and individual-unknown beings (Layer 1 pass)

Source: `verse` table, osisId `Hos.%` — 194/197 rows in DB. Three gap-by-design verses: **Hos 1:3,
Hos 3:2, Hos 5:12** (each chapter is otherwise complete). Note: **Hos 1:3 is where Gomer is named**
("he took Gomer the daughter of Diblaim") — its absence is why the wife in 1:2/3:1/3:3 reads as
unnamed below; a full text would very likely move that case from (b) to (a).

Same method as the Nahum pass: mark only what the text itself decides — a personal name (a), or a
singular unnamed person explicitly described by role/title/apposition (b). Bare pronouns are left
open, not forced. God/Lord/Spirit/angel/animals excluded throughout.

**Headline difference from Nahum:** Hosea is far more heavily personified. Nahum had a handful of
individual-vs-collection cruxes (the scatterer, the prostitute). Hosea runs a sustained metaphor —
the nation as an unfaithful wife (ch.1-3) and as a wayward son (ch.11, and "Ephraim/Israel...he/
him" throughout ch.4-14) — where a **named collection is grammatically treated as one person**
almost continuously. That pattern is called out separately below rather than tagged verse-by-verse,
because it isn't a handful of isolated cruxes — it's close to the book's default mode.

## Structural crux, book-wide: nation-names personified as one being

Ephraim, Israel, Judah, Samaria, Bethel, and Jacob-as-nation are named collections by definition —
but Hosea routinely gives them singular pronouns and person-only predicates ("he," "him," "his,"
"my son," "an unwise son," "he was exalted... he died"). Examples: Hos 4:16-17 (Israel/Ephraim,
"leave him alone"); 8:9 (Ephraim "has hired lovers"); 10:1, 10:11, 10:15 (Ephraim/Israel/Judah/
Jacob in one breath, mixed singular/plural); **11:1** ("Israel was a child... I called my son" —
the most individual-coded instance in the book); 11:3 (Ephraim taught to walk, then "them" — singular
and plural in the same sentence); 13:1, 13:9, 13:13 (Ephraim "he... died," "an unwise son").

This is not treated as a set of (b)/(e) hits above, because doing so would mean re-litigating the
same call dozens of times through the book. It's flagged once, here, as a standing question for
the referent-resolution layer: **does grammatical personification of a named collection ever
promote it to being read as an individual for this study's purposes, or is it always kept as (c)
named collection regardless of the singular grammar?** That's a rule decision, not a per-verse one.

## Sharper crux: "Jacob" / "Israel" as the patriarch, not the nation (Hos 12:2-4, 12:12)

Everywhere else in the book, "Israel" and "Jacob" denote the nation (a named collection). In
12:2-4 and 12:12, the text recounts the patriarch's own life story (wrestling in the womb with his
brother, striving with the angel, fleeing to Aram, serving for a wife) — here "Jacob" and "Israel"
are the same one historical person, not the nation. This is a genuine lexical ambiguity internal to
the book, not just a personification convention, and it sits right next to the general crux above
in the same chapter — worth resolving deliberately rather than by default.

## Observation

Same pattern as Nahum held for the confident (b) hits: they cluster on explicit role/title tags
("O priest," "the great king," "Samaria's king," "a prophet"). What's different here is scale and
kind — Nahum's open cases were a handful of local ambiguities; Hosea's central difficulty is a
book-wide convention (nation-as-person) plus one sharp internal ambiguity (Jacob/Israel,
patriarch-or-nation) that a verse-by-verse pass can flag but can't resolve on its own.
