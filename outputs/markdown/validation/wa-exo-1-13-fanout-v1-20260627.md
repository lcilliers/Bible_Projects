The biblical record depicts ruthless behavior across multiple contexts—from political oppression to economic exploitation to violence against the vulnerable. The Egyptian enslavement of Israel represents one instance of a broader pattern Scripture condemns.

**Oppression of the poor and vulnerable** emerges as a recurring theme. A ruler lacking understanding becomes a cruel oppressor (Prov 28:15–16), and God confronts those who crush His people and grind the faces of the poor (Isa 3:14–15). Those who trample the poor and exact grain taxes while building fine houses face judgment; they afflict the righteous, take bribes, and turn aside the needy (Amos 5:11–12). Israel’s transgressions included selling the righteous for silver and the needy for sandals, trampling the poor into dust (Amos 2:6–7).

**Economic exploitation and wage theft** feature prominently. Workers’ withheld wages cry out against those who kept them back by fraud (James 5:1–6), and those who make neighbors serve without payment and refuse to give wages face condemnation (Jer 22:13–17). Merchants who trample the needy, use false balances, and buy the poor for silver exemplify this ruthlessness (Amos 8:4–6).

**Violent and predatory conduct** appears in vivid imagery. Leaders who tear skin from people, eat their flesh, and break their bones like meat in a pot represent the extreme of such brutality (Micah 3:1–3). The wicked pursue the poor in arrogance, lurk like lions to seize them, and crush the helpless through their might (Ps 10:2–10).

**Institutional injustice** also receives attention. Extortion, robbery, and oppression of sojourners without justice characterize systemic wrongdoing (Ezek 22:29). Those who decree unjust laws to rob the poor and make widows and orphans their prey face divine judgment (Isa 10:1–2).

The pattern reveals that ruthlessness—whether Pharaoh’s forced labor or the exploitation condemned by the prophets—violates God’s character and invites His judgment.

Scripture identifies multiple sources of ruthless behavior, tracing it to fundamental human corruption and specific vices that drive oppressive conduct.

**The root lies in human depravity.** The human heart harbors wickedness, with “every intention of the thoughts of his heart” oriented toward evil (Gen 6:5), and the heart is “deceitful above all things, and desperately sick.” (Jer 17:9) Evil emerges from within—murder, coveting, wickedness, and deceit all originate internally and corrupt the person (Mark 7:21–23). This inclination toward wrongdoing characterizes humanity “from youth.” (Gen 8:21)

**Specific vices generate ruthless action.** Internal conflicts driven by desires that go unsatisfied lead to murder, fighting, and quarreling (James 4:1–2)—passion overrides conscience. Greed for gain produces a rejection of God (Ps 10:2–11), creating moral permission for exploitation. Wealthy individuals who accumulate resources develop a mindset where they believe nothing lies beyond their reach; they become accustomed to using their means to create opportunities regardless of ethics[1].

**Power without restraint enables ruthlessness.** When humans exercise power over one another, they frequently use it to cause harm (Eccles 8:9). The phrase “because it is in the power of their hand” reveals the core dynamic—possessing the ability to act becomes justification for acting, driven by the mentality “I want it and I’m going to get it”[1].

**Spiritual rebellion compounds the problem.** The fundamental cause involves an antipathy of evil toward good and wicked people opposing God and rejecting His precepts[2]. When people refuse to acknowledge God, they become filled with unrighteousness, covetousness, and malice—ultimately becoming “ruthless” (Rom 1:28–32).

Ruthlessness, then, emerges not from external circumstances alone but from the convergence of unchecked desire, available power, and spiritual alienation from God’s moral order.

## 1. The same Hebrew word (the precise lexical answer)

The term carrying "ruthlessly" in Exodus 1:13 is **פֶּרֶךְ (*perek*, Strong's H6531)** — harshness, rigour, severe/crushing labour. It's a rare word, appearing only **six times** in the Hebrew Bible:

- **Exodus 1:13** — the Egyptians work Israel *bə-farek* (with rigour)
- **Exodus 1:14** — repeated in the very next verse, framing the whole oppression
- **Leviticus 25:43** — "you shall not rule over him *ruthlessly*"
- **Leviticus 25:46** — the same prohibition, brother over brother
- **Leviticus 25:53** — again, of a hired/bondservant
- **Ezekiel 34:4** — the shepherds of Israel ruled the flock *with harshness*

The theological shape here is striking and probably the most interesting part of your question: **the three Leviticus uses deliberately forbid Israel from doing to one another exactly what Egypt did to them.** The word that names Egypt's cruelty in Exodus becomes the word for the cruelty Israel is *commanded not to imitate*. Ezekiel 34:4 then turns the indictment inward — Israel's own leaders are charged with *perek*, the Egyptian sin.

Worth noting for your own method: the ESV renders *perek* as "ruthlessly" in Exodus and Leviticus but as "**harshness**" in Ezekiel 34:4. Same lemma, different English string — so the English word won't reliably track the Hebrew if you search by translation.

*Source/basis: standard concordance data for the lemma פֶּרֶךְ. I'm confident in these six occurrences; the renderings I've given are ESV.*

The Bible doesn't lay out a single systematic "theory of cruelty," but it names several roots fairly explicitly, and they cluster in an interesting way. Let me separate what the text states directly from what is synthesis on my part, since that distinction matters for your kind of work.

## Start with Exodus 1 itself — the motive is given

Before going wide, it's worth noting that the *perek* of 1:13 isn't unexplained. The narrative supplies a causal chain in the preceding verses:

- 1:8 — a new king "who did not know Joseph" (severed relationship / lost memory of covenant kindness)
- 1:9 — he perceives Israel as "too many and too mighty" (perceived threat)
- 1:10 — "Come, let us deal shrewdly with them... lest they multiply, and if war breaks out, join our enemies" (**fear of the other + self-preservation + political calculation**)
- 1:11–14 — *therefore* taskmasters and crushing labour

So in the originating text, ruthlessness springs from **fear of a perceived threat to power and security, joined to a refusal to honour an existing relationship**. That's a tight, defensible reading of the passage's own logic.

## The recurring causes the Bible names

**1. The heart / inner being as the wellspring (the most fundamental answer).**
Scripture locates the source *inside* the person, not in circumstance. Genesis 6:5 — every intention of the heart "only evil continually"; Jeremiah 17:9 — the heart "deceitful... and desperately sick"; and Jesus most explicitly in Mark 7:21–23 — "from within, out of the heart... come evil thoughts, murder..." This is the closest thing to a stated origin: cruelty flows *out of* the inner being rather than being imposed from without. (Given your WA work, note this is an explicit movement — the heart as antecedent/source, the act as effect.)

**2. Absence of the fear of God (the most repeated explicit causal link).**
This thread is unusually direct — the text actually states the connection:
- Genesis 20:11 — Abraham reasons, "There is no fear of God at all in this place, and they will kill me." (No fear of God → expectation of violence.)
- Deuteronomy 25:18 — Amalek struck the faint and weary stragglers, "and he did not fear God." (Ruthlessness toward the weak *explicitly* tied to not fearing God.)
- Psalm 36:1 — "there is no fear of God before his eyes," introducing a portrait of the wicked; quoted at Romans 3:18 as a summary of human corruption.

This is the strongest *stated* causal pattern: the fear of God functions as the restraint, and its absence removes the brake.

**3. Fierce anger / wrath.**
Two texts make this link explicitly:
- Genesis 49:7 — of Simeon and Levi (the Shechem massacre): "Cursed be their anger, for it is fierce, and their wrath, for it is cruel."
- Proverbs 27:4 — "Wrath is cruel, anger is overwhelming."

**4. Greed / covetousness.**
- 1 Timothy 6:10 — love of money "a root of all kinds of evils."
- Micah 2:1–2 — they "covet fields and seize them," oppressing households; the Naboth's-vineyard episode (1 Kings 21) is the narrative case study — coveting leads to judicial murder.

**5. Pride / arrogance / self-sufficiency.**
The "ruthless" (*arits*) of Isaiah are typically the arrogant (e.g. Isaiah 13:11 pairs "the arrogant" and "the ruthless"). Habakkuk 1:7, 11 portrays a nation whose "justice and dignity go forth from themselves" and "whose own might is their god" — cruelty as the fruit of self-deification, might-makes-right.

## The synthesis the New Testament offers

Romans 1:28–31 ties several threads together: when people suppress the truth about God, "God gave them up to a debased mind," and the resulting vice list includes "heartless, ruthless" (Greek *aneleēmōn*). Here ruthlessness is framed as a *symptom* of being handed over once God is pushed out of view — which loops back to the "no fear of God" thread above. Galatians 5:19–21 similarly files "enmity, strife, fits of anger" among the "works of the flesh," i.e. native products of fallen human nature.

## My honest framing of the assumptions

Pulling it together, the Bible's picture is roughly: cruelty originates in **a disordered heart** (the seat), is **unrestrained by the fear of God** (the missing brake), and is **triggered/fuelled by** fear-of-threat, wrath, greed, or pride (the occasions). That four-part shape is *my* synthesis of the texts, not a sentence you'll find in scripture — so treat it as an organising frame, not a proof-text.

Where I'm on solid, explicit ground: the heart-as-source texts (Gen 6:5; Jer 17:9; Mark 7:21–23), the no-fear-of-God texts (Gen 20:11; Deut 25:18; Ps 36:1 / Rom 3:18), and the anger-cruelty texts (Gen 49:7; Prov 27:4). Where I'm inferring more: arranging greed, pride, and fear as "occasions," and the overall four-part structure.
