## 5. How each characteristic works

Where the previous chapter mapped where these characteristics live in the inner person, this chapter sets them in motion — tracing how each one moves between God and the human person and between persons, how it is formed and deepened over time, how it is tested, and where it is going.

### 5.1 Reverential Fear / Fear of God as Governing Orientation

Reverential fear of God moves along multiple channels simultaneously, and the multiplicity is itself significant. God commands it — Deuteronomy 6:13 and 10:20 require the reverential posture directly. He forms it through encounter — Deuteronomy 4:10 records that God intended the Horeb encounter to produce lasting fear in those who stood there, and that intention was to run forward to their children. He implants it as a covenant gift — Jeremiah 32:40 records God pledging to put his fear into the hearts of his people as a permanent disposition preventing them from turning away. And he rewards it — Exodus 1:21 shows God giving families to the midwives whose fear of him governed their disobedience to Pharaoh. The characteristic flows toward the human person through command, encounter, and implantation; the basis is covenantal rather than transactional.

The human person's movement toward God through reverential fear is correspondingly rich. Worship is its most direct expression: Psalm 5:7 shows fear drawing the psalmist into the temple in a posture of bowing, and Hebrews 12:28 names "reverence and awe" as the fitting inner disposition for approaching God in worship, "prompted by gratitude for receiving an unshakeable kingdom." Prayer is another movement: Daniel 9:4 shows reverential fear as the inner disposition from which his confession and petition arise; Jeremiah 26:19 records Hezekiah's fear producing active prayer that averted national disaster. Covenant is a third movement: Malachi 2:5 reveals that God structured an entire covenant relationship around this response — "My covenant with him was one of life and peace, and I gave them to him. It was a covenant of fear, and he feared me. He stood in awe of my name."

The horizontal movement between persons is primarily pedagogical. The psalmist offers in Psalm 34:11 to teach fear of the Lord — it can be transmitted through instruction and example. Deuteronomy 31:12–13 shows the designed mechanism: public reading of the law, generation after generation, cultivating fear in those who hear, including children who have not yet heard. The formation chain is pedagogical rather than biological — Psalm 103:17 shows the covenantal benefit of the fear-posture running across generations, but the transmission is through teaching and practice, not constitutional deposit.

Opposition acts against reverential fear primarily through displacement rather than direct assault. The 2 Kings 17 corpus shows reverential fear systematically replaced by fear of local gods — competing objects of fear occupying the inner space where God alone should reign. Isaiah 8:12 shows social fear threatening to displace God-fear: the prophet must resist adopting the crowd's conspiratorial dread, actively maintaining the fear of God against the current of surrounding anxiety. Hollow fear is another form of corruption: Isaiah 29:13 names the inner condition in which the lips perform reverence while the heart stands at a distance — a formal external compliance that has displaced the genuine inner orientation.

Reverential fear is formed by multiple mechanisms depending on the canonical tradition. In the wisdom tradition, it is the fruit of earnest seeking — Proverbs 2:5 presents it as an inner understanding arrived at through diligent pursuit. In the Deuteronomic tradition, regular communal hearing of Torah produces it over time. In the prophetic tradition, God implants it directly in the heart (Jer 32:40). In the narrative tradition, witnessing God's acts produces it suddenly: Exodus 14:31 records that the sight of God's victory at the sea produced immediate fear and belief in Israel. Haggai 1:12 shows the same mechanism: hearing the prophetic word produces the reverential response immediately. The mechanism is context-dependent; all pathways converge on the same inner disposition.

Reverential fear is tested by suffering and shown to hold. Job 1:1 and 2:3 both record God affirming Job's character — "blameless, upright, fearing God, turning from evil" — after and through the intensity of his suffering. The affliction does not destroy the reverential posture; it reveals whether it is genuine. The eschatological arc is the most developed of any characteristic in the family. Revelation 11:18 names fearing God's name as the defining characteristic of those who receive reward at the final judgment; Revelation 14:7 commands fear of God in view of the hour of his judgment; Revelation 15:4 and 19:5 show fearing God as the universal posture of the consummated community. The fullness of reverential fear is not its abolition but its universalisation — every creature responding as the God-fearer now does in part, the present partial and contested experience reaching its permanent and uncontested form.

### 5.2 Acute Fear and Alarm

Acute fear and alarm moves primarily from the outside in and from God downward. Its dominant movement is the encounter — the sudden overwhelming of the inner person by something that exceeds its normal categories. God produces this movement through theophanic encounters: angelic appearances that fill the shepherds with great fear (Luk 2:9), Jesus's acts that leave the disciples "filled with great fear" asking "Who then is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?" (Mar 4:41), the Transfiguration that leaves Peter unable to know what to say because "they were terrified" (Mar 9:6). Angels mediate this movement bidirectionally — they both produce acute fear through their appearances and command its release ("do not be afraid," Luk 1:13; Luk 2:10; Mat 28:5). The angel stands at the interface between divine holiness and human inner life, producing the fear that correctly registers divine reality, then releasing it.

The most structurally significant movement is God's repeated address to the frightened person. "Fear not, for I have redeemed you; I have called you by name, you are mine" (Isa 43:1) — the release of acute fear is grounded not in circumstances changing but in relational identity being named. The "you are mine" is the operative mechanism, not reassurance in general. What holds the release in place is not the disappearance of the threatening circumstance but the named belonging that makes the circumstance secondary. The same structure governs Jesus's calming of his disciples: "Rise, and have no fear" (Mat 17:7) follows his touch — the command is grounded in the relationship and the proximity. Reception of this release requires a will open to the divine word; distrust blocks it.

The human person's movement toward God through acute fear follows the theophanic pattern. When the storm fear and the testimony converge, the sailors in Jonah 1:16 move from threat-fear to reverential worship — they "feared the Lord greatly, and offered a sacrifice to the Lord and made vows." Acute fear, rightly redirected, is a gateway into worship. Matthew 27:54 shows the centurion's fear at the crucifixion producing confession — the experiencing of fear as the appropriate response to what has been witnessed moves directly into theological statement. The movement is not always smooth: the disciples in the Upper Room, after the resurrection, have locked the doors "for fear of the Jews" (Joh 20:19), and it is Jesus's appearance, his showing of his hands and side, that turns fear into joy.

Between persons, the "do not fear" command operates as a giving of release. David to Mephibosheth (2Sa 9:7), Elisha to his servant (2Ki 6:16), Jesus to the disciples — in each case the one who commands release of fear does so from a position of authority or covenant assurance relative to the one who is afraid. The giving of this release is not the giving of the fear itself but the provision of the ground on which the fear can be let go.

Formation in the disordered direction is the characteristic's main developmental path when acute fear is left unaddressed. First Samuel 18:29 shows Saul's fear of David intensifying into a permanent inner root of sustained enmity — what begins as an episodic reaction calcifies into a settled state approaching the dispositional character of anticipatory dread. The disciples' doors locked in John 20:19 show the same calcification. Against this, the "do not fear" corpus shows that when the fear is addressed — when the divine word penetrates and the person receives it — the result is trust, courage, and the capacity for mission (Mat 28:10).

Acute fear's eschatological orientation is freedom from all disordering fear. Revelation 2:10 commands the church at Smyrna not to fear the coming suffering — faithfulness through eschatological trial is the direction. Luke 21:26 identifies cosmic signs as producing fainting from fear, naming the eschatological context as precisely the place where the "do not be terrified" command applies (Luk 21:9). The fullness is a state in which no legitimate ground for acute alarm remains — not because the person has become insensate to reality but because all threatening realities have been resolved.

### 5.4 Terror as Overwhelming Force

Terror as overwhelming force moves almost entirely in one direction: from God or powerful entities outward and downward, landing upon peoples and inner lives as an imposed condition. The movement from within the human person toward God is largely absent. This characteristic does not document a human-to-God movement in the evidence — the terror is imposed, not offered; received involuntarily, not given.

God's movement through this characteristic is purposive and covenantal. "I will send my terror before you and will throw into confusion all the people against whom you shall come, and I will make all your enemies turn their backs to you" (Exo 23:27). The basis is conditional on enemy opposition to God's covenantal purposes — terror is his instrument of historical governance in response to resistance. Genesis 35:5 shows the same mechanism in the patriarchal narrative: a terror from God falls upon the surrounding cities so that they do not pursue Jacob's sons. The initiative is entirely divine; the inner states of the enemy peoples are the point of impact. Deuteronomy 11:25 generalises the pattern: God placing the dread of Israel in the hearts of peoples across the land, so that no one can withstand Israel.

The sustained effect of terror-as-weapon is condition-change in its recipients. Genesis 35:5 shows hostile intent becoming paralysis; Esther 9:2–3 shows opposition to the Jewish community converting under the dread of Mordecai into active assistance from provincial officials. The characteristic transforms the internal disposition of those it strikes, turning potential enemies into passive or cooperative subjects. This is its function in the covenantal narrative — it makes human history legible as a domain governed by God, where resistance to his purposes dissolves from within.

Eschatologically, the direction is toward the stripping of all such terror-power. The Ezekiel 32 corpus shows nations whose terror was their power lying in Sheol with that power entirely removed — Assyria, once spreading terror "in the land of the living" (Eze 32:23), lies powerless in the pit. The eschatological fullness for this characteristic is not an intensification but a termination: a world in which no human power deploys terror as a weapon over others, because the only legitimate dread is the fear of God himself.

The characteristic is largely silent on formation. Terror-as-force does not produce formative growth in those who experience it; it imposes a condition. No mechanism for developing terror-endurance or resilience is documented in the evidence. The exception, perhaps, is the transformation of those who receive God's covenantal dread: the paralysed enemy who cannot pursue becomes the defeated people through whom God's sovereignty is demonstrated. But even that is a political-historical transformation, not a character-forming one.

### 5.5 Dismay and Inner Collapse

Dismay and inner collapse moves from God outward as a judicial instrument, and from catastrophic events inward into the person. God produces dismay in his judicial capacity: Psalm 2:5 shows his wrath producing dismay in rebel kings; Jeremiah 49:37 records God declaring, "I will terrify Elam before their enemies" — the inner collapse of the enemy people is the instrument through which divine judgment breaks their resistance. The basis is conditional on their defiance; dismay is not arbitrary but responsive to opposition to God's covenantal purposes.

The same God who produces dismay in enemies also commands against it in his servants. Jeremiah 1:17 holds both movements together: "Do not be dismayed by them, lest I dismay you before them." The prophet is commanded not to let inner collapse grip him when facing opponents, with the sobering implication that if he yields to dismay, God himself will produce in him the very state he fears. Dismay is thus a characteristic over which God has sovereign authority in both directions — he can impose it and he can forbid it. The existence of the command against dismay implies that the person retains agency: the characteristic can be resisted, though only from the ground of divine commission and word.

The human-to-God movement through dismay is limited but present. Psalm 6:3's soul-level dismay drives the anguished cry to God — "But you, O Lord — how long?" The dismay is not a turning away from God but a turning toward him in extremity. The lament psalms show this pattern repeatedly: the inner collapse becoming the occasion of prayer, the dissolution of human capacity becoming the ground for divine address. Dismay does not produce structured worship but it can produce the cry that is itself a form of movement toward God.

Between persons, dismay has no substantial giving direction. It is not transmitted person-to-person in the way reverential fear is instructed or acute fear is released. Its movement is primarily individual — the person is struck by catastrophe, news, or divine action, and the inner collapse follows. The contagion evidenced in 1 Samuel 14:15 (panic spreading through the Philistine garrison as a wave) is the nearest approach — dismay propagating through a group — but even this is not the giving of a characteristic so much as the social amplification of a shared experience.

Formation is largely absent for dismay as positive growth. The evidence does not document a mechanism for developing dismay-resilience or terror-endurance; these are states experienced rather than formative processes. The commanded-against-dismay material (Jer 1:17; Jer 30:10: "salvation and quiet rest" as the antidote) implies that resistance to dismay is possible and commanded, but the mechanism is the divine word rather than a developmental process. The person who does not yield to dismay when God has spoken stands on the ground of divine commission; the formation involved is the formation of trust in that commission, not of dismay-endurance as such.

Suffering is the primary producer of dismay. Job 4:5 shows Eliphaz noting that when affliction reached Job personally, dismay overtook him; Psalm 6:2–3 shows physical suffering producing inner collapse. The characteristic arises directly from catastrophic contexts — it is the inner registering of what cannot be contained. Its eschatological direction is the promise of no-more-dismay: Jeremiah 30:10 names the antidote as "salvation and quiet rest," the eschatological state of undismayed composure grounded in God's deliverance.

### 5.6 Trembling / Fear Made Somatic

Trembling moves in two quite different patterns depending on which form is in view. Theophanic trembling — the body's involuntary response to divine presence — moves from the encounter outward, simultaneously inner and somatic. "On the morning of the third day there were thunders and lightnings and a thick cloud on the mountain and a very loud trumpet blast, so that all the people in the camp trembled" (Exo 19:16). There is no interval between encounter and trembling; the divine presence and the somatic response arrive together. "Moses trembled and did not dare to look" (Act 7:32) — the same simultaneity, with God's self-identification producing the trembling before any deliberation. This form of trembling has no instruction pathway, no formation mechanism, no human-to-human transmission. It is involuntary and unmediated.

Trembling at God's word operates from a different constitutional depth and through different pathways. Isaiah 66:2 names the posture: "he who is humble and contrite in spirit and trembles at my word." The trembling-at-word tradition is formed in community and sustained through covenant engagement. Ezra 9:4 and 10:3 show those "who tremble at the commandment of our God" gathering as a distinct community around covenant decision-making — the trembling posture leading to covenant action, the somatic gravity expressing the community's reverential attentiveness to God's communication. The formation sequence is: before (covenant unfaithfulness discovered), during (the community assembling in appalled grief and trembling), after (a covenant decision made from the posture of reverent gravity). The mechanism is community formation in the context of God's word, not sudden encounter.

The human movement toward God through trembling operates differently in the two forms. Theophanic trembling is not a movement toward God but a response to his proximity — the body's registration of what the inner person has encountered. Trembling at God's word, by contrast, is precisely the posture of receptive engagement with divine communication: the person who trembles at God's word is the person God looks for, the one to whom he turns. Isaiah 66:5 promises vindication to the community that trembles at his word. Psalm 2:11 shows trembling as the somatic qualifier of joy in service to God — "rejoice with trembling" — where the trembling marks the gravity that prevents casual approach.

Between persons, trembling has almost no giving direction. It is not transmitted or extended; it is experienced. The one near-exception is the reverential weight that Ephraim's speech once commanded (Hos 13:1: "when Ephraim spoke, there was trembling" in others) — an authoritative presence that produced trembling in those who heard. But this is the body's response to perceived authority rather than a transmitted characteristic.

The eschatological direction of trembling splits along the same two-form line. For the theophanic and panic forms, the eschatological fullness is freedom from them: Ezekiel 34:28 and Micah 4:4 promise that "none shall make them afraid" — the restoration as a state where the threat-driven trembling that afflicts the present has entirely ceased. For the trembling-at-word tradition, no corresponding elimination is promised; the posture of reverential somatic gravity before God's communication is not one of the things the eschaton removes. The community defined by trembling at God's word is the one Isaiah 66:5 vindicates. Philippians 2:12 — "work out your own salvation with fear and trembling" — shows the trembling-gravity continuing as the appropriate inner-bodily tone of earnest eschatological engagement.

### 5.7 Anticipatory Dread and Anxiety

Anticipatory dread and anxiety is a characteristic that God imposes in its disordered form and releases in its resolution. Deuteronomy 28:66–67 shows covenantal-curse dread as a divine imposition — "night and day you shall be in dread and have no assurance of your life" — the chronic anxiety as a direct covenant consequence of unfaithfulness. The basis is conditional on covenant violation; God does not impose chronic dread arbitrarily but as a fitting consequence of the relationship's rupture. God will also bring upon the disobedient the very fears they have harboured (Isa 66:4) — turning their own anticipatory anxieties into the instrument of judgment.

But God also stands as the primary source of release from this characteristic. The sequence in Psalm 34:4 is one of the clearest in the evidence: "I sought the Lord, and he answered me and delivered me from all my fears." The mechanism of release is relational — seeking God produces encounter with God, and encounter with God produces deliverance from the inner terrors that had gripped the person. First John 4:18 names the ultimate mechanism: "perfect love casts out fear, because fear has to do with punishment." The love of God, when it is received in its fullness, displaces the punitive-anticipatory dimension of fear. God as judge is what makes anticipatory dread of judgment rational; God as perfect love is what displaces it.

The movement from person to person through this characteristic is limited and specific. Paul's pastoral anxiety in 2 Corinthians 12:20 — his anticipatory dread of what he may find when he visits his churches — flows from love already invested. The anxiety is relationally motivated; his concern for the community generates the forward-looking dread of discovering disorder. Philippians 2:26 shows the same relational structure in Epaphroditus's distress because the Philippians had heard of his illness — the anxiety arising from concern about how his condition has troubled those he loves. This form of anticipatory dread moves from the one who loves toward the ones loved; it is anxiety-as-care.

Formation and transformation in anticipatory dread follow no positive arc. Chronic anxiety is not a characteristic that deepens beneficially through exercise — it colonises. Ezekiel 12:18–19 shows it saturating every moment of eating and drinking; Hebrews 2:15 names the fear of death as something that held people in lifelong inner slavery. The characteristic is a condition produced by suffering and threat (siege conditions in Ezekiel, mortality itself in Hebrews), and its resolution is not through habituation but through deliverance. The partial deliverances the evidence documents — Psalm 34:4's seeking and being answered, Isaiah 12:2's declaration that dread no longer has a hold — are foretastes of the eschatological fullness in which perfect love has entirely displaced the fear of punishment (1Jo 4:18). Christ's defeat of death is the ground of Hebrews 2:15's liberation from lifelong fear-of-death slavery; the eschatological fullness of this characteristic's resolution is tied directly to his work.

The imagination is the primary operating theatre of anticipatory dread, and this makes it the one characteristic in the family that works most distinctively through the mind's forward-projecting capacity. Job 3:25 shows dread as an anticipated scenario held in the imagination before it materialised — the dreaded thing sitting within him as an inward anticipation before it arrived as an outer event. Paul's pastoral dread (2Cor 12:20) operates the same way: the feared relational disorder is projected and held in the mind before the visit. The characteristic distorts cognition not by shutting it down, as dismay does, but by fixing it on anticipated negative futures. Its resolution requires that the mind be redirected — not suppressed but given a different object. The promise "I sought the Lord" is the turn from the feared future to the reliable present reality of God's response.

### 5.8 Timidity and Cowardly Shrinking

Timidity and cowardly shrinking does not come from God. Second Timothy 1:7 states the negative directly: "God gave us a spirit not of fear but of power and love and self-control." The spirit of timidity is what God has not given; by implication it has a different source — a failed or corrupted inner constitution that stands in the place where God's gift should be. The characteristic does not move from God toward the person in any positive sense; God's movement toward the person in relation to this characteristic is the negation of it, the giving of its opposite.

The command against it operates through the relational ground that Christ provides. John 14:27: "Let not your hearts be troubled, neither let them be afraid." The command is to the heart — to the site of volitional decision — and it is grounded in the peace Christ gives ("my peace I give to you"). The mechanism is not willpower alone but will engaging with what has already been given. Second Timothy 1:6 adds the context: "stir up the gift of God that is in you." The transformation from timidity to the spirit of power, love, and self-control is not passive but actively engaged — the person must stir up what God has already placed within them.

The movement between persons is almost entirely absent for this characteristic. Timidity has no giving direction and no receiving direction between persons; it is not instructed or transmitted. What moves between persons is the command against it and the ground that releases it — Jesus commanding the disciples, Paul encouraging Timothy — but the characteristic itself is not a given or received thing. It is a condition of the inner constitution.

Opposition is what this characteristic most directly engages. Matthew 8:26 — "Why are you afraid, O you of little faith?" — shows Jesus naming the disciples' storm panic as timidity, linking it to the failure of trust in his presence. The opposition that produces timidity is specific: the storm, the master's perceived harshness, the threat of persecution (Rev 21:8 context). The characteristic arises as a response to pressure and threat, suppressing the volitional capacity that faithful action requires.

Formation is the characteristic's primary movement, but in a negative direction. What begins as a momentary failure of nerve can accumulate into a settled disposition of cowardly shrinking and ultimately into apostasy. Matthew 25:25–26 shows the buried talent — a single act of fearful self-preservation — producing the master's judgment of the servant as "wicked and slothful." Revelation 21:8 places cowardice alongside idolatry and murder as the failure state of those who apostatise under pressure. The trajectory of unaddressed timidity is not merely personal weakness but full covenant abandonment.

The transformation arc runs from timidity toward the spirit of power, love, and self-control (2Ti 1:7). The mechanism is stirring up the gift — an active engagement with what God has already given, not a passive waiting for sudden transformation. The eschatological stakes are explicit: the negative terminus is Revelation 21:8's judgment on the cowardly; the positive terminus is the company of those who fear God and receive his reward (Rev 11:18; 19:5). The formation arc for timidity is one of the most urgently motivated in the study — the eschatological distance between the coward and the God-fearer is the full distance between the lake of fire and the consummated community of the redeemed.

## 5. How each characteristic works

How each characteristic works — how it moves between God and the person and between persons, how it is formed and deepened, how it sequences through the inner being, and what it leaves behind — is the subject of this chapter, taken one characteristic at a time.

---

### 5.1 Weeping and Tears

Weeping and tears moves in one primary direction: outward from within. The inner grief overflows through the body and becomes visible to others — and, as the evidence consistently shows, to God. Joseph's weeping is the paradigmatic instance of this overflow: "And he wept aloud, so that the Egyptians heard it, and the household of Pharaoh heard it" (Gen 45:2). Years of suppressed grief break through in a sound that cannot be contained. The weeping is an act — the act of grief becoming audible — but it arises from an inner condition that has long preceded it.

The temporal dimension of weeping is also evidenced. Gen 50:4 — "And when the days of weeping for him were past, Joseph spoke to the household of Pharaoh" — frames weeping as a bounded period: it has a beginning, it runs its course, and it concludes. After the days of weeping are complete, the will re-engages with practical life. This arc of weeping — from onset to completion — is the characteristic's most basic temporal movement.

**Movement toward God**

The most theologically significant movement of weeping and tears is upward. When tears are directed toward God in penitential honesty or prayer, they function as the somatic form of petition — a physical evidence of genuine inner state that God himself receives and responds to. God declares to Hezekiah: "I have heard your prayer; I have seen your tears. Behold, I will heal you" (2Ki 20:5). The tears are seen; they carry weight before God; they are answered. Josiah's weeping before the Lord is framed similarly: God hears him precisely because the weeping is the outward expression of a genuinely humbled and penitent heart (2Ki 22:19).

The evidence shows a limiting condition on this movement. Weeping that is performative — that covers the altar with tears without genuine inner repentance behind it (Mal 2:13) — does not produce the same divine response. Similarly, the weeping that follows disobedience without genuine repentance goes unanswered (Deu 1:45). The inner posture required for tears to reach God is not spiritual perfection but genuine Godward orientation — the absence of performance and the presence of real inner movement toward him.

**Movement between persons**

Weeping and tears also moves between persons in several modes. Within existing relational bonds, weeping is the most direct evidence of the bond's depth. David's grief for Absalom — "O my son Absalom, my son, my son Absalom! Would I had died instead of you, O Absalom, my son, my son!" (2Sa 18:33) — reveals parental love at its most unguarded. The weeping discloses the inner life's real investment. Across relational distance, weeping requires a deliberate volitional act: "weep with those who weep" (Rom 12:15) is a command, not a description. Empathic weeping must be chosen; the will must extend itself toward another's grief, entering it as an act of love rather than as an automatic overflow.

Prophetic weeping moves in yet another register: the prophet weeps on behalf of a people or nation as an act of identification. Jeremiah's longing for inexhaustible tears — "Oh that my head were waters, and my eyes a fountain of tears, that I might weep day and night for the slain of the daughter of my people!" (Jer 9:1) — is grief extended beyond personal bond toward communal solidarity. The prophet's inner life is shaped by the grief of those he serves; his tears are given for others.

Grief also crosses covenantal boundaries. The Egyptians weep for Jacob; the prophet weeps for Moab. Weeping is a human phenomenon that does not respect the borders of the covenant community, though the most theologically generative weeping — penitential, Godward, and transformative — operates distinctively within the covenantal relationship between God and his people.

**Formation and deposit**

Bereavement weeping, communal national grief, and the weeping of exile do not produce evidenced transformation in the verse evidence. They describe grief experienced and expressed — real and legitimate, but not narrated as a force that reshapes the inner person. The penitential weeping of the Godward register does produce transformation, but this belongs primarily to the Sorrow and Inner Grief characteristic's evidence.

What weeping does leave is somatic deposit. Sustained weeping marks the body: Job's face reddened and eyes darkened (Job 16:16); the psalmist's bones wasted, bodily strength spent (Psa 31:10). The body carries the history of what the inner person has wept. This deposit is not permanent — God turns mourning into dancing, tears are wiped away — but while grief runs its course, the body accumulates its evidence.

---

### 5.2 Mourning and Lamentation

Mourning and lamentation is governed — in a way no other characteristic in this family is — from outside the person as well as from within. God commands it, withholds it, and reverses it. This governance is the defining feature of how mourning works: it is not simply what the person does in response to loss but a mode of inner and social life that God himself regulates.

The primary movement of mourning is willed and outward. Abraham "went in to mourn for Sarah and to weep for her" (Gen 23:2): the mourning is a deliberate entry into the grief-act, directed toward a specific person. Jacob "tore his garments and put sackcloth on his loins and mourned for his son many days" (Gen 37:34): the mourning is embodied, sustained, and directed against all comfort. Jacob then names his mourning as a permanent inner posture he intends to carry: "No, I shall go down to Sheol to my son, mourning" (Gen 37:35). The will has chosen to mourn indefinitely; mourning is not merely grief's expression but the form of the inner life going forward.

**Movement from God**

God extends mourning toward the person as discipline or judgment. Lamentations records God multiplying mourning in Judah as an act of judgment; God commands entire communities to mourn as a moral and penitential response; he forbids mourning when the relational context is broken — Jeremiah is told not to enter the house of mourning because God has withdrawn his peace from the people (Jer 16:5). God also reverses mourning by direct divine action: "You have turned for me my mourning into dancing; you have loosed my sackcloth and clothed me with gladness" (Psa 30:11). The reversal is not the natural expiry of grief but a divine transformation of the inner condition.

**Movement between persons and in community**

Mourning's most distinctive social dimension is communal mourning as a shared event. When Amos commands mourning to penetrate every square and street — "In all the squares there shall be wailing, and in all the streets they shall say, 'Alas! Alas!' They shall call the farmers to mourning and to wailing those who are skilled in lamentation" (Amo 5:16) — mourning is a whole-community act in which individual grief is given shared form. The house of mourning is a social institution; professional lament-makers are a social function; mourning garments and periods are social norms. Mourning is the characteristic that most fully extends grief into the communal and public life of a people.

In the New Testament, mourning narrows toward the individual's inner life. Jesus declares the mourning person blessed: "Blessed are those who mourn, for they shall be comforted" (Mat 5:4). James commands mourning as the appropriate inner displacement of levity: laughter must yield to mourning when the situation morally demands it (Jam 4:9). The Corinthians' mourning is a sign of genuine repentance that restores damaged relational bonds (2Cor 7:7). In each case the mourning is an inner condition that God or the community can witness and that God responds to with comfort.

**Transformation and formation**

Mourning for the dead and communal national mourning do not produce evidenced inner transformation in their specific verse evidence — they describe grief expressed and endured rather than grief as a transforming force. The transformation evidence belongs to the penitential and NT registers. There, mourning participates in the formation of moral seriousness: the wise heart chooses the house of mourning over the house of feasting (Ecc 7:2, 7:4) because regular encounter with grief and mortality shapes wisdom. Mourning also has an eschatological formation trajectory: it is the mark of the not-yet, the condition that will be replaced by divine comfort when the fullness comes. Its presence names where the person is; its promised reversal names where they are going.

The failure modes of mourning name what the characteristic looks like when it is absent where it should be present. Arrogance, moral numbing, and self-deception are the three inner states Scripture names for those who do not receive the grief that their situation requires — the Corinthians' arrogance (1Cor 5:2), the complacency of those not grieved over Joseph's ruin (Amo 6:6), and Babylon's boast that she will never see mourning (Rev 18:7). Each is the inner alternative to genuine grief reception: the failure of the relational and moral responsiveness that mourning requires.

---

### 5.3 Supporting characteristics

The supporting terms gathered at the boundary of this study do not share a single working pattern — their diversity is precisely what places them at the edge of the grief vocabulary rather than its centre. What can be said of them collectively is how they arrive and what they do in their most concentrated instances.

The Gen 3:16 vocabulary establishes the governing frame for the terms at the fall-consequence edge of the group. "I will surely multiply your pain in childbearing; in pain you shall bring forth children" — the pain (*e.tsev*) is divinely multiplied and imposed at the most intimate bodily act. The second term in the same verse (*its.tsa.von*) names the same imposition as toil. Both move from God to the person as consequences of the fall; they are not sought or formed but received as the condition of life after Eden. The movement is entirely from outside: God multiplies them, the person bears them.

The hardship vocabulary moves differently. Joseph names the burden God has released: "God has made me forget all my hardship and all my father's house" (Gen 41:51). The hardship (*a.mal*) was a sustained inner burden carried over time — years of suffering and estrangement held in memory — and its release is also from outside: God acts, and the person is freed. The direction of movement here is God resolving what God had permitted.

The hostile-provocation terms move horizontally: "The archers bitterly attacked him, shot at him, and harassed him severely" (Gen 49:23). Here the movement is from adversary to person — the embittering pressure is externally imposed through violent hostility rather than arising from inner grief over loss. The term marks a different edge of the grief vocabulary.

These terms are held at the edge of this study precisely because each of them requires more examination before its inner home, movement pattern, and relational direction can be settled. They inform the study's understanding of grief's outer edges without yet yielding to full analysis.

---

### 5.4 Sorrow and Inner Grief

Sorrow and inner grief moves through the inner person in three distinct directions: from God to the person, from person to God, and from person to person. Of all the characteristics in this family, this one has the richest and most varied movement evidence — because it is the settled inner condition from which all other forms of grief arise.

**Movement from God**

God extends sorrow to the person as discipline, correction, and sovereign dealing. Deu 28:65 names the divine bestowal directly — the languishing soul and trembling heart are what "the Lord will give you there" in exile. Sorrow here is not a natural consequence of circumstances but a condition God gives. At the redemptive extreme, God puts his servant to grief so that his soul-offering may justify many (Isa 53:10 — the servant's grief is not incidental but appointed for redemptive purpose). The movement from God is purposive throughout: even the sorrow God gives as punishment is given with an end in view.

**Movement toward God**

The characteristic's movement toward God is most clearly evidenced in the penitential and pastoral registers. Godly sorrow moves in a sequence that the evidence describes with unusual precision: letter → grief → godly sorrow → repentance without regret → earnestness, indignation, fear, longing, zeal (2Cor 7:8–11). The grief is the catalyst for each stage that follows. It is worth noting that this sequence requires the grief to be directed toward God rather than toward self — godly grief produces reformation because it is oriented toward the moral and relational reality that God takes seriously; worldly grief, directed toward self, produces only the sorrow of regret that does not transform.

Jesus's grief in Gethsemane moves toward God in its own way: "My soul is very sorrowful, even to death; remain here, and watch with me" (Mat 26:38). The soul-sorrow is named before God; the request to watch alongside accompanies it; the prayer that follows (Mat 26:39 and following) is the Gethsemane movement toward the Father. Even at the most extreme inner depth, the soul's sorrow moves toward rather than away from God.

**Movement between persons**

Sorrow and inner grief also moves between persons: given through pastoral grief (Paul's apostolic grief over the Corinthians in 2Cor 2:4), received through relationship (the mother's grief from her foolish son, Pro 10:1; the disciples' grief from Jesus's announcement in Mat 26:22), and transmitted through word (the Corinthians receive grief through Paul's letter, 2Cor 7:8). The enabling condition for this person-to-person movement is genuine relational bond: the deeper the bond, the more directly the other's state produces grief in the person who loves them. The blocking conditions are attachment to something other than the relationship — wealth in the rich young man's case (Mat 19:22), arrogance in the Corinthians' case (1Cor 5:2).

**Modes**

Sorrow and inner grief operates in two structurally distinct modes. In its passive mode it is a condition the person is in — sorrow lodged in the heart all day long (Psa 13:2), grief melting the soul (Psa 119:28). In this mode the characteristic is received and carried rather than exercised. In its generative mode it becomes an active force that reshapes the inner life — godly grief producing earnestness and zeal (2Cor 7:11), sorrow producing the lament form (Jer 9:1). The distinction between these modes is morally significant: sorrow that remains passive and inward does not produce the same outcome as sorrow that is oriented toward God and allowed to move outward into reformed will and action.

**Formation**

Sorrow and inner grief participates in formation over the long arc in a way that the more acute characteristics do not. The recurring pattern of distress → prayer → deliverance across Israel's covenantal history (Deu 4:30; 2Ch 15:4; Hos 5:15) shows grief functioning as a formative instrument across generations — not once but repeatedly, pressing the will toward God in cycle after cycle of adversity and return. The wise heart's choice to dwell in the house of mourning (Ecc 7:2, 7:4) shows grief as a discipline chosen for its formative effect: regular encounter with grief and mortality produces the wisdom that the house of feasting does not.

---

### 5.5 Anguish and Distress

Anguish and distress moves most characteristically in a single direction: from the person toward God. Of all the characteristics in this family, this one has the largest evidenced body of movement — fifty-six verses in the tsar/tsa.rah corpus alone — and the movement is almost entirely constituted by the distress-drives-prayer pattern. "In my distress I called upon the Lord; to my God I cried for help. From his temple he heard my voice, and my cry to him reached his ears" (Psa 18:6). Distress arrives; the person cries; God hears from his temple and answers. This three-stage sequence is not merely repeated but is the structural form of the covenantal exchange at the level of grief.

What the evidence reveals about this movement is that distress is purposively designed: it is meant to move through to God rather than to remain as a closed inner condition. Job 15:24's image — "distress and anguish terrify him; they prevail against him, like a king ready for battle" — names distress at its most overwhelming: a force that defeats the will's autonomous resistance. But even this extreme form is not the end state: the evidence consistently frames distress as what activates the turn toward God.

**Movement from God**

God extends distress to the person as covenantal discipline. Deu 4:30 frames this with clarity: "When you are in tribulation... you will return to the Lord your God and obey his voice." The distress is not named as accidental but as the means by which God turns the will of the wandering person back toward himself. The movement from God is therefore purposive: God permits or sends distress in order that the will may be turned. 2Ch 15:4 and Hos 5:15 confirm the same pattern — divine-permitted distress producing Godward movement in Israel across the whole sweep of her covenantal history.

**Movement at spirit level**

Anguish and distress is the characteristic in this family that most clearly reaches spirit level — and spirit-level anguish has a distinctive movement pattern. Job 7:11 names it: "I will speak in the anguish of my spirit; I will complain in the bitterness of my soul." When distress reaches the spirit, it can no longer be restrained inwardly — it must express outwardly. The sequence is: anguish arrives at spirit level → restraint becomes impossible → outward speech erupts. Paul's anguish over Israel has a different movement at its depth: "I have great sorrow and unceasing anguish in my heart" (Rom 9:2) — sustained, unresolved, held in the heart as an ongoing internal state rather than erupting outward. The spirit-level and heart-level modes of anguish move differently: the spirit-level mode compels outward expression; the heart-level mode can be held within, sustained over time without resolution.

**Formation**

Anguish and distress participates in formation through the covenantal discipline pattern. The recurring sequence of adversity → distress → turning to God → seeking and finding (Deu 4:30; 2Ch 15:4; Hos 5:15) shapes the inner life over the long arc of covenant history. Each cycle of distress that drives the person toward God deepens the habit of Godward turning. Suffering deepens anguish: accumulated losses compound inner distress (Jer 45:3), and the covenantal discipline of divinely-governed suffering tests and produces Godward movement. The distress-prayer-deliverance sequence leaves the person not merely relieved but formed in the habit of bringing the inner life's most acute states to God.

Anguish and distress does not operate in the giving direction toward other persons in its primary evidence. It is a characteristic that is received and experienced, then directed toward God — not extended from person to person in the way that empathic grief is. The evidence is silent on person-to-person transmission for the anguish registers.

---

### 5.6 Groaning and Sighing

Groaning and sighing moves in the most direct path to God of any characteristic in this family: inner suffering → somatic groan → rises to God → divine hearing and response. This three-stage movement is established most clearly in the Exodus pattern: "the people of Israel groaned because of their slavery and cried out for help. Their cry for rescue from slavery came up to God" (Exo 2:23). The groan is involuntary — it escapes from the inner person under the weight of suffering — but it rises. And God receives it as covenantally significant: "And God heard their groaning, and God remembered his covenant with Abraham, with Isaac, and with Jacob" (Exo 2:24). The groan activates the covenant memory; it is not a meaningless physiological event but a cry that reaches the covenant God.

The heart is the named origin of the groan in its most immediate expression. Psa 38:8 — "I am feeble and crushed; I groan because of the tumult of my heart" — gives the movement its most direct local form: the heart's turmoil produces the groan, which then rises outward and upward. The groan is what the tumultuous heart sounds like when it can no longer remain inward.

**The Spirit's groaning**

The most theologically significant instance of groaning and sighing moves in the reverse direction — from the Spirit within the believer toward God. Rom 8:26 — "the Spirit himself intercedes for us with groanings too deep for words" — names the Spirit joining the believer's spirit at the point where human language has failed. The movement is: believer's weakness → human prayer fails → Spirit steps in → Spirit's own groanings rise to God. The Spirit does not prompt the believer to groan on his own; he himself groans in the believer's place, carrying the inner life's most inexpressible cry to the Father. This is the Spirit's groaning, not the believer's — though it happens within the believer's inner life.

**The creation's groaning**

The whole creation's groaning in Rom 8:22 extends the movement to the cosmic scale: "For we know that the whole creation has been groaning together in the pains of childbirth until now." The groaning is universal and shared — the entire created order joins in the cry. But the direction is eschatological: the groaning is the labour-pain of a creation in travail toward its coming birth. The movement is forward as well as upward — toward the future redemption that the creation's groan anticipates. This is the one instance in this family of characteristics where the groaning itself is both pain and expectation: it is not a closed cry of hopelessness but a travail directed toward what is coming.

**Movement between persons**

Groaning and sighing moves between persons in the register of apostolic travail. Paul's groaning on behalf of the Galatians — "my little children, for whom I am again in the anguish of childbirth until Christ is formed in you!" (Gal 4:19) — is an extension of grief from person to community as an act of love. The travail-form of this movement is generative and directed toward an outcome: not merely grieving for the Galatians but labouring for their formation. Conscience-driven sighing also moves from within the person outward in a morally significant direction: those who sigh and groan over the abominations committed (Eze 9:4) are expressing, through their groan, a moral perception of what is happening around them. The groan marks them as morally alive.

**Formation and deposit**

Suffering produces groaning and deepens it: accumulated losses compound the inner anguish that the groan expresses. Sustained groaning leaves somatic deposit — bones wasting under prolonged sighing (Psa 102:5), bodily strength depleted (Psa 31:10) — and these deposits feed back into deeper inner depletion: the body's deterioration deepens the sense of inner exhaustion, which can deepen the groaning further. This negative feedback is the characteristic's most acute developmental pattern, and the evidence is clear that divine intervention — or the grief running its natural course — is required to interrupt it. Job captures the state of saturation: "For my sighing comes instead of my bread, and my groanings are poured out like water" (Job 3:24). When sighing has replaced food, grief has occupied every available capacity of the inner and outer person.

---

### 5.7 Pain and Inner Ache

Pain and inner ache works differently from the other characteristics in this family because its primary evidence for movement is silent in most of the directions that the other characteristics evidence clearly. The evidence is explicit about what pain is and what it does to the inner person; it is largely silent about pain as a vehicle for movement between persons or as a catalyst for transformation in the way that distress and sorrow are.

The characteristic's movement is primarily receptive: pain arrives and the person receives it. The servant in Isa 53:3 is "a man of sorrows, and acquainted with grief" — the pain is the defining condition of his identity, not the result of his own action but the load he carries in his vocation. Dan 6:20 shows the king crying out "in a tone of anguish" as he approaches the den: the pain of uncertainty and fear is expressed outward as a vocal release, but the movement is not toward God as petition — it is toward Daniel as anxious question. Pain here is expressed rather than offered.

**God's purposive framing**

The evidence's clearest movement claim for pain is God's purposive relation to it. Job 5:18 — "He wounds, but he binds up; he shatters, but his hands heal" — frames pain as something God both permits and resolves, with the same hand doing both. Pain is not outside God's governance; it is within it. This means pain's movement is not only from circumstance to person but from God (who permits or sends it) through circumstance to person, and then — if the pain is held within the covenantal relationship — back toward God in the lament register.

Jer 15:18 shows this movement pressed to its limit: "Why is my pain unceasing, my wound incurable, refusing to be healed? Will you be to me like a deceitful brook, like waters that fail?" The lament is addressed directly to God — pain moving outward as speech to God. The direction is Godward, but the content is accusatory: the person demands to know why the one who should heal has apparently become unreliable. This is the honest form of pain's movement toward God — not suppressed but named, not resolved but spoken. The evidence affirms this movement as legitimate: the lament is addressed to God rather than away from him.

**Silences**

The unceasing pain register and the birth-pangs register do not produce evidenced transformation, do not describe a before-and-after sequence, and do not narrate mechanisms of change. They describe the condition of pain — what it is to carry it, what it does to the person who carries it — without narrating it as a transformative force. This silence is meaningful: not every form of pain in Scripture is presented as producing growth. Some pain is simply pain — endured, borne, and awaiting the promise of its future end. The servant's pain is redemptive, but that redemptive function is not extended in the evidence to pain generally. The unceasing pain of Jer 15:18 and the pain of Isa 13:8's birth-pangs are real and serious, but the evidence does not narrate them as formative or productive.

Somatic deposit is the one developmental consequence the evidence traces for this characteristic. Sustained grief leaves the body marked, and the body's marking feeds back into the inner person's sense of depletion. The purposive framing of Job 5:18 holds this deposit within a frame of divine intention: the hand that permits the wound also heals it.

---

### 5.8 Bitterness of Soul

Bitterness of soul is, among all the characteristics in this family, the one with the least evidenced movement. It arrives; it settles; it saturates. The verse evidence describes it as a sealed condition rather than a dynamic one — a state the inner person is in rather than one that moves in any particular direction.

**Movement from God**

The primary movement of bitterness in the evidence is from God to the person. Job 9:18 states it plainly: "he will not let me get my breath, but fills me with bitterness." God fills; the person is filled. Lam 3:15 confirms the same: "He has filled me with bitterness; he has sated me with wormwood." The divine filling is total — the person is saturated, with no room for relief. God is the active party; the person is the receiver. The bitterness comes from outside, from above, as a condition God imposes through his dealings with the person.

Esau's foreign wives make life bitter for Isaac and Rebekah — "and they made life bitter for Isaac and Rebekah" (Gen 26:35). The bitterness here comes from human relationships, not directly from God — but it results in the same condition: a pervasive inner quality that colours the whole experience of life. Bitterness can be inflicted relationally as well as divinely.

**The sealed condition**

Once bitterness is in place, the evidence does not describe it moving anywhere. Pro 14:10 — "The heart knows its own bitterness, and no stranger shares its joy" — is the defining statement of bitterness's characteristic posture: sealed within the heart, inaccessible to others, known only to the one who carries it. It does not rise toward God as a groan; it does not overflow outward as tears; it does not take social form as mourning. It sits in the heart.

This sealed quality is the characteristic's most significant feature in terms of working pattern. A foolish son is "a grief to his father and bitterness to her who bore him" (Pro 17:25) — the bitterness is the mother's inner condition as a result of her son's failure. It is given to her by his actions; she receives it; and there the narrative ends. The evidence does not describe what she does with it, how it resolves, or how it moves. The bitterness is named and the evidence closes.

**Silences**

The bitterness corpus is silent on transformation: no sequence of inner states is described, no mechanism of change narrated, no formation arc evidenced. It is silent on the giving direction: bitterness can be inflicted by one person on another (Gen 26:35; Pro 17:25; Col 3:19), but the primary evidence describes it as received rather than given. It is silent on the human-to-God movement: bitterness does not appear in the evidence as something the person brings to God in prayer in the way that distress drives prayer.

What the evidence leaves open is whether bitterness is always an endpoint or whether it can be a transitional state that the other expressions of grief eventually address. The verse evidence examined here describes the received form — bitterness as something that arrives and settles; what happens after bitterness has settled is a question the evidence in this particular corpus leaves without answer.

Col 3:19's warning — "do not be harsh with them" — carries a practical implication: bitterness can be prevented at its relational source. The one who would not embitter another withholds the harshness that produces it. This is the one instance in the evidence where movement around bitterness is possible — not resolving it once it has arrived, but not creating it in the first place.

## 5. How each characteristic works

Each of the characteristics in this study has its own pattern of movement — how it comes to the person, how it passes between persons, how it is formed, and where it is resisted. This chapter follows each characteristic through those movements, from its origin to its expression, from its initial reception to its long-term effect.

---

### 5.1 Wisdom as holistic inner character and orientation

Wisdom moves downward before it moves outward. Its fundamental direction is from God into the person — and only once it has been received and has settled as constituted character does it begin to express itself through speech, governance, and life. As Jam 1:5 states: "If any of you lacks wisdom, let him ask God, who gives generously to all without reproach, and it will be given him." The motion is one of asking and receiving — wisdom belongs first to God (Dan 2:20) and is given to the human person who lacks and asks.

The giving takes more than one form. Where Jam 1:5 names the response to individual request, the Bezalel narrative names a sovereign bestowing: the craftsmen were filled with the Spirit of God and given wisdom for the construction of the sanctuary — not because they asked but because God's purpose required it (Exo 31:3). And where Psa 111:10 says "all those who practice it have good understanding," a third mode appears: covenantal formation through the sustained practice of the fear of the Lord. The gift comes in response to request, through sovereign commissioning, and through a life of covenantal orientation — three different giving-mechanisms converging on the same result, wisdom taking up residence in the person.

Once given, wisdom expresses itself outward. As Pro 16:23 states: "The heart of the wise makes his speech judicious and adds persuasiveness to his lips." The speech is not the giver of wisdom but its vehicle — wisdom already present in the heart governs what comes out of the mouth. This is wisdom's primary mode of human-to-human transmission: the wise person gives wisdom through the quality of their speech and life, not through mystical impartation. The wise person who continues to hear and increase in learning (Pro 1:5) is both receiver and giver — receptive to more wisdom from those who have it, and transmissive to those who hear them.

The communal dimension of this movement is named at Phil 2:2: "complete my joy by being of the same mind, having the same love, being in full accord and of one mind." Froneō as shared inner orientation is wisdom working horizontally across a community — the same quality that the individual exercises becomes the fabric of communal life when it is shared. Wisdom is not only a person-to-person characteristic; it constitutes communities.

The movement from the human person toward God takes the form of froneō-orientation: "those who live according to the Spirit set their minds on the things of the Spirit" (Rom 8:5). This is not primarily an act of asking but an act of orientation — the Spirit-minded person's whole inner directional constitution is turned toward God, and this orientation is itself the fundamental human-to-God wisdom-movement. The Spirit's prior work enables this orientation; the person's will maintains it.

Opposition to wisdom's movement operates along two axes. The first is self-attribution: "Do you see a man who is wise in his own eyes? There is more hope for a fool than for him" (Pro 26:12). Self-attributed wisdom is the primary internal blockage — a person convinced they already possess wisdom cannot receive it, and this inner foreclosure is more serious than simple ignorance. The second is orientation-inversion: those whose inner orientation is toward the wisdom of this age (1Cor 1:21) are operating within a framework that reverses the value of divine wisdom. They encounter wisdom from another and it registers as foolishness. James 3:15 names the third form: earthly, natural, demonic wisdom — a counterfeit transmitted through adversarial spiritual activity. Wisdom's downward movement is thus opposed from within (self-sufficiency), from without (worldly orientation), and from spiritual powers (adversarial counterfeit).

Formation happens in sequence. The fear of the Lord is the starting point: Pro 9:10 names this as "the beginning of wisdom," and the sequence is not arbitrary — reverential acknowledgment of God's authority is the inner posture that opens the person to receive and accumulate wisdom. From that starting point, wisdom grows: the path of the righteous is like the light of dawn, growing progressively brighter (Pro 4:18). Formation is slow and cumulative rather than sudden — the exception being direct divine bestowing (Solomon's prayer answered), which is sovereign and instantaneous.

Suffering is not absent from wisdom's formation. Psalm 119:71 names the formative role of affliction: "it is good for me that I was afflicted, that I might learn your statutes." Affliction enforces learning that ease does not require; wisdom grows partly through what the person is forced to encounter in difficulty. The cross itself is the most extreme expression of this: what the world reads as folly is, in fact, the wisdom of God (1Cor 1:24). The wisdom disclosed at the cross is not produced by suffering but revealed through it — the deepest wisdom concealed in the most apparently disqualifying form.

Wisdom's modes span the immediate to the sustained. The immediate mode is the specific wise act — the judicious word, the right governance decision, the creative devising of Bezalel in the moment of construction. The sustained mode is wisdom as constituted character — the progressive deepening that makes the person reliably, habitually wise rather than occasionally insightful. The immediate act of wisdom is possible without deep character; the sustained character makes the wise act the natural expression of who the person has become. The eschatological form is Christ himself: "Christ the wisdom of God" (1Cor 1:24) is the one in whom wisdom reaches its fullest expression, and the present formation of wisdom is the formation of the person into the pattern of the one who embodies it.

### 5.2 Understanding as inner perceptive faculty

Understanding moves from God toward the person through two distinct actions: placing and opening. The placing is named at Job 38:36: "Who has put wisdom in the inward parts or given understanding to the mind?" — a question whose answer is God alone. Understanding is not self-generated; it is placed in the person as a faculty, given at creation, fitted to the human design. The opening is named at Luk 24:45: "Then he opened their minds to understand the Scriptures." Placing gives the faculty; opening activates it for its fullest function. The disciples had understanding as a created capacity all along; what they lacked was the opening that only Christ could perform. In the resurrection context, this opening is definitional — the faculty that was placed at creation now receives the fullest disclosure of what it was placed to grasp.

What this divine-to-human movement reveals about God's disposition is the movement it produces. Understanding opened in the disciples immediately generates mission: their minds opened → Scripture comprehended → they are sent out to preach repentance for the forgiveness of sins (Luk 24:46-49). Understanding is not given for its own sake; it is opened so that the person can move. The divine act of understanding-giving always appears to have an outward-directed purpose.

God's understanding, by contrast, does not move toward any purpose beyond itself. Isa 40:28 names the difference: "The Lord is the everlasting God, the Creator of the ends of the earth. He does not faint or grow weary; his understanding is unsearchable." His understanding is not a faculty that can be opened or trained; it is the inexhaustible comprehension of the Creator, self-sustaining and unbounded. The same family of words names both, but the divine instance is attribute and the human instance is gift.

Between persons, understanding moves through sustained reasoned engagement. The dialegō of Act 24:25 — Paul reasoning before Felix about righteousness, self-control, and coming judgment — is understanding given through argument: the one who understands transmits through sustained discourse, and the hearer is reached through the sustained engagement of argument with argument. Felix was alarmed. The understanding offered through Paul's reasoning reached him; he simply did not follow it to its conclusion. This is understanding's characteristic human-to-human movement: the teacher who has received opens a path through argument and exposition; the hearer stands at the threshold of reception and may or may not enter.

The blocking of understanding is named at Isa 6:10: "Make the heart of this people dull, and their ears heavy, and blind their eyes; lest they see with their eyes, and hear with their ears, and understand with their hearts, and turn and be healed." The judicial withholding of understanding is a divine action in response to prior hardening — God seals the faculty that the people have already begun closing. This is meaningful: understanding can be withheld by God as a consequence of the person's own refusal to attend. The non-receiving inner state is not merely passive; it has a history. The hears-but-does-not-understand state of Isa 6 is the sustained outcome of repeated choices to hear without heeding.

Formation follows two patterns. The first is training through constant practice: "those who have their powers of discernment trained by constant practice to distinguish good from evil" (Heb 5:14). Understanding grows through disciplined exercise of the perceptive faculty — solid food for the mature, not milk for infants. The sequence is: undiscerning state → training through sustained practice → trained faculty capable of moral discrimination. This formation is gradual, proportional, and volitionally maintained. The second pattern is instantaneous and divine: Christ's opening of the disciples' minds at Luk 24:45 is not the result of their training but a definitive act from outside. The two patterns are not in competition; one names the human process, the other the divine sovereign action.

Modes of understanding reflect its dual character. As a faculty, understanding operates habitually — the discerning person habitually perceives what is required, habitually governs well, habitually makes meaning communicable to others (1Ki 3:9; Neh 8:8). As a specific act, understanding is the moment of grasping: the moment the disciples' minds opened, the moment Felix became alarmed, the moment the people in Nehemiah's time understood the reading. The immediate response to received understanding is consistently cognitive opening — a new capacity to grasp what was previously closed. The sustained effect, when understanding is properly formed, is a moral faculty trained to distinguish good from evil. Present understanding in part anticipates its eschatological fullness: "now I know in part; then I shall know fully" (1Cor 13:12). The present perceptive faculty, however well formed, is partial in what it reaches; the eschatological form is comprehensive.

### 5.3 Knowledge as inner content and covenantal knowing

Knowledge moves in more directions than any other characteristic in this study. God's knowing of Israel moves toward them as election: "You only have I known of all the families of the earth; therefore I will punish you for all your iniquities" (Amo 3:2). Israel's knowing of God moves toward him as pursuit: "Let us know; let us press on to know the Lord; his going out is sure as the dawn; he will come to us as the showers, as the spring rains that water the earth" (Hos 6:3). God discloses himself in historical acts so that the nations may know: "so that all the peoples of the earth may know that the hand of the Lord is mighty, that you may fear the Lord your God forever" (Jos 4:24). Each direction is distinct — elective, pursuitful, testimonial — and together they describe a characteristic that is constitutively relational rather than merely cognitive.

The human-to-God direction of knowledge is volitional pursuit. Hos 6:3's "let us press on" is a communal decision as much as an individual one — the knowing of God is something a people can enter together, pressing forward into the relationship initiated by God's prior elective knowing. What this pressing reveals is the nature of the relationship: it is one that can be deepened, that rewards sustained movement toward it, and that is met by God's own faithful self-disclosure ("he will come to us"). The person who presses to know the Lord discovers that God moves toward the one who moves toward him.

The human-to-human transmission of knowledge takes the form of teaching. The wise lay up knowledge (Pro 10:14), and the lips of the wise spread what they have accumulated. Knowledge passed between persons is not mystical impartation but instruction — the accumulated inner holding of one person made available to another through speech and testimony. Job's assertion of knowing his Redeemer (Job 19:25) is also a form of transmission: conviction spoken through affliction reaches others because it is borne out under the conditions that test whether the knowing is real.

Opposition to knowledge comes from the most consequential adversarial act in Scripture's record: Gen 3's serpent offers the knowledge of good and evil to the woman, and the offering is adversarial in its structure — presenting what God has placed off-limits as something to be taken. The adversarial transmission of moral knowing does not simply provide information; it constitutes the person differently through the taking. At the other end, culpable ignorance is the sustained human resistance: "being ignorant of the righteousness of God, and seeking to establish their own, they did not submit to God's righteousness" (Rom 10:3). This is not passive uninformedness but structural ignorance that has generated its own substitute — a self-righteousness that occupies the inner space that knowledge of God's righteousness should fill.

The stakes of knowledge's absence are named plainly at Hos 4:6: "My people are destroyed for lack of knowledge; because you have rejected knowledge, I reject you from being a priest to me." The not-knowing here is not mere uninformedness but covenant-breach — the people's destruction follows their rejection of the knowledge that would have sustained the relationship with God. Knowledge is not optional in the covenantal relationship; it is the substance of which that relationship is made. Its absence is the relationship's dissolution.

Formation of knowledge follows both covenantal and experiential paths. The covenantal path: encounter with God's self-disclosure → volitional pursuit → relational knowing → orientation-formation across a life. Pro 1:7 names the condition: "The fear of the Lord is the beginning of knowledge; fools despise wisdom and instruction." Not merely the starting point but the governing orientation — knowledge that grows in the fear of the Lord is knowledge that remains properly related to its object. The experiential path is represented supremely by Job: conviction-knowing deepened through extreme suffering (Job 19:25). The affliction does not produce the knowing but tests and makes explicit what was already there — Job's knowledge of his Redeemer is forged in the furnace of suffering without being generated by it.

The eschatological horizon of knowledge is the most explicit of any characteristic in this study. Hab 2:14 names it: "For the earth will be filled with the knowledge of the glory of the Lord as the waters cover the sea." The present covenantal knowing — pursued, tested, accumulated, occasionally destroyed — is moving toward a future in which it saturates everything, leaves nothing outside its reach, covers the earth as waters cover the sea. The covenantal knowing that is now partial, vulnerable, and particular in its scope becomes universal, inescapable, and comprehensive. What is now rejected by some will then be inescapable for all.

The modes of knowledge span the full range from immediate to eschatological. Immediate: Noah reading the returning dove and knowing the waters had subsided — practical situational knowing that registers and acts (Gen 8:11). Sustained: the settled conviction of Rom 8:28, already held and not let go. Relational: the pressing on of Hos 6:3, a sustained volitional posture across a life. Eschatological: the filling of the earth at Hab 2:14, the universal knowing that is the destiny of the whole creation. Knowledge does not operate in one mode; it operates across all of them simultaneously, with each mode carrying the others in potential.

### 5.4 Discernment and practical judgment

Discernment moves from God toward the person as both crediting and enabling. The paradigmatic divine-to-human discernment act in Scripture is at Gen 15:6: "And he believed the Lord, and he counted it to him as righteousness." God's act of crediting — the moral reckoning that assesses Abraham's faith and credits it as righteousness — is the ultimate form of divine discernment applied to the human person. The human did not evaluate himself; God evaluated him, and the evaluation was constitutive. It established his standing. From the other side, the Servant of Isa 52:13 receives sa.khal as a divinely-aligned quality: "Behold, my servant shall act wisely; he shall be high and lifted up, and shall be exalted." The discernment that enables the Servant's wise and effective action is given in the context of divine purpose, not self-developed.

The human person's movement toward God through discernment takes the form of self-examination. "But if we judged ourselves truly, we would not be judged" (1Cor 11:31). The diakrinō here is directed inward — the person applies the discriminating faculty to themselves before God. This is honest accountability: the person who self-examines does so in the presence of the one who already knows and who has established the standard. The inner posture required is willingness to assess without softening the assessment. The immediate consequence is freedom from judgment — self-discernment averts what external judgment would otherwise impose.

Between persons, discernment moves through evaluation and its expression. The prudent person (a.rum) governs what they give in social contexts — knowing what to reveal and what to hold, calibrating their assessment to the relational situation. The receiving side is named through the ta.am figure of Psa 34:8: "Oh, taste and see that the Lord is good! Blessed is the man who takes refuge in him!" Active testing rather than passive reception — the person who receives discernment from another does not simply accept it wholesale but brings their own evaluative capacity to bear. This makes discernment's human-to-human movement an exchange between two evaluative faculties rather than the simple transmission of a verdict.

Opposition to discernment is named in its most consequential form at Gen 3:1: "the serpent was more crafty than any other beast of the field." The adversarial perversion of discernment — a.rum turned toward cunning — is the mechanism of the fall. The same inner quality of shrewd perceptiveness that serves wise navigation of reality can be turned to serve deception. What makes this distinctive is that the adversarial form is not the absence of discernment but its redirection. The serpent is genuinely perceptive; it perceives the woman's vulnerability and the precise point at which the prohibition can be reframed as deprivation. Discernment weaponised is more dangerous than simple ignorance, because it knows where the pressure points are.

The cognitive-argumentative form of opposition appears at Rom 2:15: "their conflicting thoughts accuse or even excuse them." The logismos — the inner moral argumentation that runs alongside conscience — can operate in both directions: accusing the person toward truthful self-assessment, or excusing them away from it. The inner tribunal that discernment constitutes can be manipulated by the person themselves, turning the evaluative faculty toward self-justification rather than self-examination. This is discernment's internal perversion: the same capacity that enables self-examination serves self-exculpation when directed away from God.

Formation of discernment follows the consistent pattern named at Heb 5:14: "those who have their powers of discernment trained by constant practice to distinguish good from evil." The training is not abstract; it is the repeated exercise of evaluative judgment in concrete situations, forming the perceptive faculty through use. The sequence: undiscerning state → training through sustained self-examination and moral engagement → formed capacity for reliable judgment. The person who self-examines repeatedly (1Cor 11:31) develops the habit of self-knowledge; the person who repeatedly acts wisely (Isa 52:13) deepens the sa.khal that produces wise action. Over time the characteristics of reliable judgment, self-awareness, and effective action become constituted rather than occasional.

The eschatological dimension of discernment is concentrated in its two poles: the Servant's exaltation (Isa 52:13 — wise effective action producing high and lifted up) and Abraham's credited standing (Gen 15:6 — divine crediting of faith establishing righteous standing). Both are partial eschatological forms: the present-exaltation-through-wise-action anticipates a final vindication; the present-credited-righteousness anticipates a final declared standing before God. The present exercise of discernment reaches toward the form in which divine evaluation is final rather than penultimate.

Discernment's modes span from the immediate perceptive act — the taste of Psa 34:8, the moment of recognising what is right — to the sustained character of the person who has been formed through repeated self-examination and wise action into someone whose discernment is reliable and habitual. The immediate response to activated discernment is consistently action-directed: self-judgment (1Cor 11:31), effective action (Isa 52:13), taste-based confirmation (Psa 34:8). The sustained effect is a constituted quality — the person whose evaluative faculty has become an instrument of reliable moral perception. The characteristic grows from act to habit to character through disciplined use.

### 5.5 Deliberative planning, counsel, and purposive intent

Purposive intent operates at the junction of two sovereignties — divine and human — and the movement between them is the characteristic's defining territory. God's purposive intent moves irresistibly: "The Lord of hosts has sworn: 'As I have planned, so shall it be, and as I have purposed, so shall it stand'" (Isa 14:24). There is no conditional element, no possibility of override. The divine purpose moves from inner resolve to historical outcome without hindrance. Human purposive intent moves with genuine force but within the boundaries of a sovereignty that governs what it does not determine. The human heart plans; God establishes the steps. Both are real; neither collapses into the other.

The divine purpose moves toward the human person in two forms. The first is the irresistible sovereign will that governs events: at Rev 17:17, God places deliberate purpose into the hearts of the kings — "God has put it into their hearts to carry out his purpose by being of one mind and handing over their royal power to the beast, until the words of God are fulfilled." The kings' genuine deliberate purpose is simultaneously the instrument of a purpose they did not choose. The second is salvific willing: "The Lord is not slow to fulfill his promise as some count slowness, but is patient toward you, not wishing that any should perish, but that all should reach repentance" (2Pe 3:9). Divine purposive intent toward the human person in this mode is desire-shaped, patient, and directed toward the person's repentance and life.

Between persons, purposive intent moves through the counselor-counselee relationship. "The counsel that Ahithophel gave was as if one consulted the word of God; so was all the counsel of Ahithophel esteemed, both by David and by Absalom" (2Sa 16:23). The counselor gives inner wisdom in formed counsel; the counselee receives direction that resolves uncertainty. What makes good counsel transmissible is that the counselor has formed within themselves the deliberate wisdom that they then offer in speech. The precondition of genuine giving is prior formation: the counselor must first have become someone whose purposive judgment is reliable before that judgment can benefit another.

The relationship between human purposing and divine sovereignty is stated most directly at Jer 29:11: "For I know the plans I have for you, declares the Lord, plans for welfare and not for evil, to give you a future and a hope." The plans are God's — formed within his own sovereign counsel and directed with a welfare that human purposing can neither guarantee nor replicate. Yet they are plans for the exiles, not instead of them. The divine purposive intent does not abolish human planning; it governs what human planning cannot secure on its own. The person who plans within the framework of God's known purpose is the one whose planning is oriented toward what is real.

The blocking of purposive intent is named through Pharaoh: "Pharaoh turned and went into his house, and he did not take even this to heart" (Exo 7:23). The refusal to set the heart — to direct purposive attention toward what is requiring response — is the blocking mechanism in its starkest form. Pharaoh does not simply fail to plan; he actively refuses to attend. The inner state of the person who refuses counsel is self-determined autonomy: the purposive faculty closed to external wisdom, turned fully inward, immune to the signs that should redirect it. The apostolic instruction of Rom 13:14 — "make no provision for the flesh, to gratify its desires" — names the same purposive capacity redirected: foresight (pronoia) as forward-provision planning refused toward the flesh, and by implication redirected toward what is of God. Purposive intent is a neutral capacity that takes its moral character from where it is pointed.

Formation of purposive intent follows the pattern of deliberate practice and received wisdom. Foresight (pronoia) develops through observation and learning — the prudent person who observes the ant and plans accordingly (Pro 6:6 proximate) is the one whose foresight capacity deepens through sustained attention to reality. Counseling skill develops with sustained exercise and Spirit-guidance — Paul's considered judgment offered to the community (1Cor 7:25) is the apostolic form of this. The sequence: awareness of what is required → deliberate forming of purpose → expressed in counsel or action → governed by God's establishing of the outcome. Over time the person who deliberately plans and takes counsel becomes someone whose purposing is trustworthy and whose planning reliably serves those it is directed toward.

The sanctification dimension of purposive intent is located in its communal form. Spirit-guided deliberate judgment for the formation of community (gnōmē in 1Cor 7:25) is counsel in service of others' growth — the person's purposive capacity directed outward toward the community's good. The eschatological dimension is clear: God's irresistible purpose (Isa 14:24) is the engine that drives history toward its completion. The present exercise of human purposive intent — deliberate, submitted to God's governance, directed toward genuine good — is oriented within the larger movement of divine counsel that nothing can finally resist. Human purposing in the present anticipates its fullest form in alignment with divine purpose already determined.

Modes: purposive intent operates as a single volitional act (boulomai — the specific decision), as considered resolution held over time (gnōmē — the formed judgment maintained), as irrevocable unified purposing (za.mam — Lam 2:17, the carried-out purpose), and as foresight (pronoia — the forward-looking provision for what is coming). The immediate response to good counsel received is clarity of path — the counselee who was uncertain now has direction. The sustained effect is a life whose purposing is reliable, whose planning aligns with outcomes, and whose counsel others can trust as something close to consulting God himself.

### 5.6 Meditative and reflective inner activity

Meditation is initiated from outside the person before it becomes the person's own sustained activity. God provides the material that meditation dwells upon — his law given, his vision disclosed — and the meditative engagement is the creature's responsive dwelling on what God has given. The sequence is named in Psa 119:97: "Oh how I love your law! It is my meditation all the day." The law is received; love for the law arises; meditation is the form the love takes in sustained inner activity. What the meditation reveals about the person's relationship with God is not compliance but affection: "the meditative relationship is not mechanical but affective — the person returns to God's word because they love God."

At Dan 7:8, Daniel's sustained inner contemplation of the received vision — "I considered the horns" — represents the same movement in a different context. The vision is given; the meditative holding of the vision is the creature's active engagement with what has been disclosed. God gives what requires dwelling upon; the person dwells. The fact that the vision is terrifying (Dan 7:28) makes the meditative holding itself a form of faithfulness — keeping the vision in heart despite the distress it causes.

Between persons, meditative activity does not transmit directly. Its outputs — wisdom formed, love deepened, understanding surpassing teachers — can be given through other means, but the meditative act itself is inward. What the evidence shows is that the meditative inner state opens the person to receive disclosure from without: Joseph's meditative reflection on his situation (Matt 1:20) is the condition in which the angel's message arrives. The sustained inner reflection is not the giving mechanism but the receiving condition — the person who is already in sustained inner engagement with a problem is in the state most open to receiving what resolves it.

Meditation's perversion is not its absence but its redirection. Jesus names the corrupted form at Luk 5:22: "Why do you question in your hearts?" The Pharisees are reasoning within themselves — dialogizomai is active — but it is reasoning directed toward self-justification rather than formation. The meditative capacity is not absent; it is turned inward-upon-the-self rather than inward-toward-received-content. The rich fool of Luk 12:17 reasons in the same mode: self-enclosed deliberation about how to secure more for himself, with no reference beyond his own resources. The same inner activity, redirected, produces the opposite of what sustained God-directed meditation produces.

Heb 4:12 names the unique spiritual vulnerability of the meditative space: "the word of God is living and active, sharper than any two-edged sword, piercing to the division of soul and of spirit, of joints and of marrow, and discerning the thoughts and intentions of the heart." The thoughts and intentions produced by meditative activity — the enthumēsis and ennoia of the heart — are the inner objects the word discerns. Meditation produces what the word then penetrates. The inner life that meditation fills is not private; it is open to the one whose word reaches it. And through that same space, as Matt 1:20 shows, spiritual disclosure arrives — the meditative state is where the angel speaks.

Formation through meditation is cumulative and proportional. Psa 119:97-99 names the trajectory: all-day meditation on God's law produces understanding that surpasses teachers. The formation is not sudden but built through sustained returning — "all the day" is not an episode but a discipline. The sequence: receive God's law → love it → meditate on it repeatedly → grow in understanding and love. The sustained effect is a constituted love for what is meditated upon, a deepened capacity for contemplation, and understanding formed through the sustained engagement with what God has disclosed.

Distress intensifies rather than disrupts this formation. Daniel's keeping of the terrifying vision in his heart (Dan 7:28) is meditative holding under the pressure of what the vision reveals. The affliction is the occasion for deepened meditative engagement, not its termination. The person who can maintain sustained inner dwelling on what is disturbing has a meditative capacity that distress has not overcome. The meditative space is precisely where difficult content requires to be held, processed, and brought to resolution or faithful acceptance.

Meditation's eschatological horizon is notably absent. Unlike wisdom (whose eschatological form is Christ), knowledge (whose fullness fills the earth at Hab 2:14), or the logos (whose moral principle is the kingdom's law), meditation has no explicitly named eschatological destination in the evidence. This is consistent with meditation's character as a creaturely-responsive activity — the creature who dwells on what God gives. Whether this activity continues in its present form in the eschatological state the evidence does not say.

The modes of meditation range from the focused contemplative attention of Daniel's vision-considering to the sustained all-day dwelling of Psa 119:97, from the deliberative inner questioning of Luk 5:22 to the structured reflection of Matt 1:20. The immediate response to engagement in meditation is affective: "Oh how I love your law!" — love is not the conclusion of meditation but what arises in its beginning and sustains it throughout. The sustained effect is the formation of the person into someone whose love for what God has given has been deepened by continuous engagement with it.

### 5.7 Inner thought-content — the mind's formed thoughts

Thought-content moves from God toward the person as direct deposit: "God answered Solomon, 'Because this was in your heart, and you have not asked for possessions, wealth, honor, or the life of those who hate you, and have not even asked for long life, but have asked for wisdom and knowledge for yourself that you may govern my people'" (2Ch 1:11). The divine response to Solomon's prayer is the placement of knowledge-content in the person — the inner cognitive domain is enriched by what God gives. This is distinct from the giving of a faculty (understanding, §5.2) or a constituted character (wisdom, §5.1): it is the deposit of content in the mind that already has its faculties in place.

From God toward the person, thought-content also moves as divine attention: "How precious to me are your thoughts, O God! How vast is the sum of them!" (Psa 139:17). God's thoughts are directed toward the human person — re.a as the formed thoughts of God moving in the human's direction. The person's response to encountering God's thoughts directed at them is wonder and preciousness. Thought-content here moves not as instruction but as personal address: God's inner life directed toward the creature.

The movement toward God is the discipline of alignment: "taking every thought captive to obey Christ" (2Cor 10:5). The person brings the inner cognitive domain — the formed thought-content of the mind — into submission to Christ's authority. This is the volitional act of managing what the mind holds, directing formed thoughts away from opposition to God and toward obedience. The relationship revealed is one of cognitive submission: the whole inner cognitive domain placed under Christ's authority, even in its most intimate corners.

Thought-content does not transmit directly between persons. It is generated internally or placed by God; the human-to-human giving mode is absent from the evidence. What passes between persons is not thought-content as such but the characteristic that carries it — wisdom expressed in speech, knowledge transmitted in teaching, the logos received and then spoken. Thought-content is the interior holding that precedes and informs all of these, but the characteristic itself remains inward.

The adversarial movement against thought-content is the clearest in the study: "the god of this world has blinded the minds of the unbelievers, to keep them from seeing the light of the gospel of the glory of Christ, who is the image of God" (2Cor 4:4). The noēmata — the mind's formed thought-contents — are the specific target of adversarial blinding. The adversary does not attack the perceptive faculty directly but blinds the content the faculty holds, so that the inner cognitive domain cannot process the gospel's light. This is thought-content as the primary site of spiritual warfare within the inner person: the contested ground is what the mind holds.

The consequence of this blinding is not merely cognitive. Ungoverned thought-content becomes strongholds — entrenched cognitive structures that resist transformation and determine the person's responses from within. The volitional discipline of taking thoughts captive (2Cor 10:5) is the sustained action of clearing and aligning what the mind holds, replacing adversarially-influenced content with content obedient to Christ. This is thought-content formation in its most contested mode: not gradual accumulation but active governance under spiritual pressure.

Formation through the discipline of thought-governance shapes the inner cognitive domain toward Christlikeness. Taking every thought captive to obey Christ (2Cor 10:5) is the sanctification of the cognitive domain — the explicit goal named for this discipline is Christ's obedience, which is itself the pattern of Christlikeness. The sequence: thought-content present (received, placed, or adversarially introduced) → volitional assessment and capture → brought into Christ's obedience → inner cognitive domain aligned. Over time, the repeated practice of this discipline constitutes a person whose thought-content is habitually directed toward God rather than away from him. Sustained blindness, by contrast, hardens into a formed inability to see the gospel — thought-content blinded and held in blindness becomes increasingly structural.

The modes of thought-content reflect its character as formed inner objects rather than activities: content placed by divine gift (2Ch 1:11), content arising spontaneously in the mind as events (Psa 139:17 — thoughts as the experience of encounter), and content that is the target of adversarial action (2Cor 4:4). The characteristic has no direct eschatological trajectory in the evidence — consistent with its nature as an operational inner holding rather than a constituted characteristic moving toward completion.

### 5.8 Logos — the word as inner-being engagement

The logos moves from God toward the person with the force of what it is: "the word of the cross is folly to those who are perishing, but to us who are being saved it is the power of God" (1Cor 1:18). This is not movement that awaits human receptivity before it has effect — it is active, dividing, constitutive of the inner state of those it reaches. The same word that is power to the saved registers as folly to those who are perishing, not because the word changes but because the inner condition of the receiver determines how it is received. The word moves out from God and meets persons at the point of their deepest orientation.

The dwelling form of this movement is named at Col 3:16: "Let the word of Christ dwell in you richly, teaching and admonishing one another in all wisdom, singing psalms and hymns and spiritual songs, with thankfulness in your hearts to God." The word settles into the inner person — not a brief contact but a rich residence. What follows is communal: teaching, admonishing, singing, thankfulness. The word dwelling richly in the person immediately generates outward movement toward others. The interior dwelling produces communal formation; the word does not remain private.

The cleansing function of the logos is named at Jn 15:3: "You are already clean because of the word I have spoken to you." This is a past-and-ongoing divine act — Christ's spoken word constituting the person's inner purity. The logos here does not instruct; it cleanses. The word as constituting agent of the inner person's sanctification is distinct from the word as information-carrier; it is the word as the very means by which Christ forms the person from within.

Between persons, the logos moves through speech that is shaped by what dwells within. The one who has received the word richly gives through gracious, timely speech (Col 4:6) and through intelligible communication that reaches the inner person of the hearer: "I would rather speak five words with my mind in order to instruct others, than ten thousand words in a tongue" (1Cor 14:19). The five intelligible words that reach the hearer are worth more than ten thousand that do not — because what matters is not the volume of speech but its capacity to enter the other person's inner life and form something there. The precondition of genuine giving through the logos is prior reception: "let the word of Christ dwell in you richly" before you teach others.

The non-receiving inner state is named as spiritual blindness confirmed by the encounter: the person who meets the logos of the cross and does not receive it is categorised as "those who are perishing" — the encounter does not leave the person where it found them, it reveals and confirms where they already stand.

The logos operates as spiritual weaponry: the word of God is "the sword of the Spirit" (Eph 6:17), the instrument by which spiritual warfare is waged and strongholds are contested. The adversarial resistance to the logos is not passive; the word of the cross confronts what opposes it, and the word-as-weapon is the characteristic's most active mode in the domain of spiritual conflict. The logos that dwells richly in the person and forms them from within is the same logos that, when spoken, cuts through spiritual opposition.

Formation through the logos is cumulative and volitionally maintained. "Let the word of Christ dwell in you richly" (Col 3:16) is an imperative for sustained reception — not a single act but an ongoing posture of welcoming the word's dwelling. The word grows richer in its dwelling with sustained reception; the person who continues to receive is formed more deeply than the person who received once. The sequence: divine word sent → received openly → dwells richly → produces wisdom, teaching capacity, worship, and community formation. Each of these effects then feeds back into the person's receptivity to further dwelling. The loss mechanism is the word received but not held — the seed among thorns pattern, where reception happens but the word does not establish sustained residence.

The sanctification trajectory is explicit and Christological. The word cleanses (Jn 15:3), forms wisdom (Col 3:16), and its moral principle — "the whole law is fulfilled in one word: you shall love your neighbour as yourself" (Gal 5:14) — is the kingdom's governing norm. The eschatological form of the logos is the word of the cross now fully vindicated as the power of God, and the love-your-neighbour principle as the moral law of the renewed community. The present indwelling of the word anticipates the state in which the word's forming work is complete and its moral principle is the natural expression of what those it has formed have become.

The person who receives, holds, and is formed by the word — and then gives it through gracious speech — is one whose entire communicative life has been shaped by what they received. Movement, formation, and expression are the logos's characteristic arc within the person. Having followed each characteristic through that arc, the study can now ask how the characteristics within the family act on each other.

## 5. How each characteristic works

Each characteristic in this study moves in its own way — how it is received, how it is formed, how it is expressed, and how it deepens in the inner person over time. This chapter traces the working life of each characteristic in turn.


### 5.1 Humility — willed self-lowering

The dominant movement of humility — willed self-lowering is downward, and it is a movement the person initiates. As 2 Chronicles 7:14 frames it, the call is "if my people who are called by my name humble themselves, and pray and seek my face and turn from their wicked ways, then I will hear from heaven and will forgive their sin and heal their land." Humility is the first act in a sequence of inner movements toward God; it is not the passive result of circumstances but the opening volitional act. The person who humbles themselves sets in motion a chain of engagement — prayer, seeking God's face, turning — that together constitute the inner reorientation God requires before he forgives. Humility's downward movement is the precondition for every subsequent movement toward God.

**Movement: downward before God and others.** The evidence shows humility operating in both the vertical and horizontal registers of movement. In the vertical register, the person moves inwardly downward before God — as the tax collector in Luke 18:14 who "would not even lift up his eyes to heaven" and beat his breast in an act of inner abasement. This vertical movement toward God is what enables justification — "this man went down to his house justified." In the horizontal register, Philippians 2:3 names the movement outward toward others: "in humility count others more significant than yourselves." The cognitive-volitional act of re-ordering the self's estimate is the horizontal form of the same downward movement.

**Movement: the Christological paradigm.** Philippians 2:8 is the paradigmatic statement of humility's movement from the divine side: "he humbled himself by becoming obedient to the point of death, even death on a cross." Here the downward movement is maximal — from the form of God to the form of a servant, to death, to the cross. This establishes not only the pattern (divine-to-human direction) but also the claim that humility's downward movement is not loss but the way through which exaltation comes. The movement from humility to exaltation — established in Christ and extended to the believer in 1 Peter 5:6 ("humble yourselves under the mighty hand of God, so that at the proper time he may exalt you") — is the structural arc of humility's movement over time.

**Formation: how humility is shaped.** Humility is formed through sustained engagement with God's word as personally claiming. The Hebrew evidence shows that the humbling consistently accompanies the hearing and receiving of God's word — 2 Kings 22:19 shows a king's heart is tender because the word has struck it. Formation also occurs through the counter-pressure of circumstances: the kings who do not humble themselves accumulate guilt (2 Chronicles 33:23); the contrast shows that humility is not the default but the response to God's initiative, whether through word, circumstance, or the explicit call to bow down. The New Testament adds the dimension of the community of faith as the context of formation: Philippians 2:3 addresses the communal context — "count others more significant than yourselves" — placing the formation of humility in the texture of daily relational life.

**Opposition.** The evidence explicitly names what humility resists: pride (the spirit that self-elevates), conceit (the inflated self-estimate), and performed pseudo-humility (Colossians 2:18/23 — the appearance of humility without its inner reality). These operate against humility's formation and are themselves its structural opposite. The person who is self-enclosed is not available for humility's movement; the person who performs humility without its inner engagement has the form but not the characteristic.

**Sustained working.** Over time, voluntary acts of humility form the character quality of tapeinofrosunē — the mind of lowliness that becomes stable rather than episodic. The movement that begins as an act becomes a disposition, and the disposition becomes the settled orientation from which wisdom (Pro 11:2), contentment, corporate harmony (Eph 4:2), and divine favour follow. The characteristic works in the inner person by deepening from event into stable inner character.

Matthew 11:29 captures the telos of this working: "Take my yoke upon you, and learn from me, for I am gentle and lowly in heart, and you will find rest for your souls." The rest offered is not idleness but the internal peace of the person whose inner life is aligned with the posture that Jesus himself inhabits — the lowly heart as the source of genuine rest.

### 5.2 Lowliness — experienced and imposed state

Lowliness moves differently from humility because the initiating movement is not the person's own. The characteristic's working life begins with an external force — God, circumstances, affliction — that brings the person low, rather than with the person's own volitional descent. Psalm 107:12 shows the movement plainly: "he bowed their hearts down with hard labor; they fell down, with none to help." The bowing is God's act; the falling is its consequence; the inner person is the site of the landing. The working life of lowliness begins not with an act the person takes but with a condition the person receives.

**Movement: the pattern of divine lowering and lifting.** The evidence shows a consistent arc: God lowers, and the person who receives the lowering rightly finds themselves in the position from which God lifts. Luke 1:48 shows the pattern from the receiving side — Mary's lowly estate is the condition from which "from now on all generations will call me blessed." The lowliness is real, not performed; and it is the context for divine favour. The movement is not circular but purposive: the lowering is not the end but the preparation for the lifting.

**Formation: learning contentment through imposed state.** The formative dimension of lowliness appears most clearly in Philippians 4:12: "I know how to be brought low, and I know how to abound. In any and every circumstance, I have learned the secret of facing plenty and hunger, abundance and need." The passive voice — "to be brought low" — confirms that this is an imposed state. The formation that results is contentment — a learned capacity that Paul names as a "secret": something that is not obvious, that does not arise naturally, but that is formed through actual passage through the condition. Lowliness works as a formative agent: the person who has been brought low and has endured it with an open rather than resistant inner life comes out with a capacity for contentment that the person who has only known abundance does not have.

**Opposition.** What opposes the formative working of lowliness is inner resistance to the condition — the person who experiences lowliness but refuses it, who fights against it, who interprets it as injustice to be overcome rather than as a condition to be received. The evidence does not directly name this resistance, but it is implied by the contrast between the externally-imposed humbling that leads to dependence and the externally-imposed humbling that leads to no change (the kings who are lowered but do not genuinely receive it — 2 Chronicles 28:19 and the comparative cases — find no transformation in the condition).

**What is silent.** The formation of inner capacities through lowliness beyond contentment is not directly evidenced in the three key verses for this characteristic. The inner working that the evidence supports is: imposed condition → received with open inner life → formation of contentment and capacity for both-registers equanimity. The three verses do not trace a richer inner-working map.

Lowliness works in the inner person primarily as a formative pressure from outside that, when received rather than resisted, produces the inner capacity for endurance, contentment, and the quiet waiting that positions the person for God's lifting.

### 5.3 Submission — inner disposition of will

Submission — inner disposition of will — moves in two directions simultaneously: it is received from above (from the authority whose claim the person is placed under) and expressed outward through the pattern of life that willing obedience generates. The dominant movement in the evidence is the downward-inward movement of the will placing itself under: Romans 6:17 describes those who "have become obedient from the heart to the standard of teaching to which you were committed" — the teaching comes from outside; the heart receives it and places itself under it. The movement of submission is the will's placing of itself under what comes from above.

**Movement: hearing and placing-under.** The etymology of hupakoē — "under-hearing" — encodes the movement: the person receives what is spoken by authority and places themselves under it. This is the foundational movement of submission as inner disposition. Second Corinthians 10:5 shows the same movement at the cognitive level: "we destroy arguments and every lofty opinion raised against the knowledge of God, and take every thought captive to obey Christ." The cognitive movement is active but in the direction of submission — taking thoughts captive is an act that results in placing those thoughts under. The movement of submission is always this: the person's inner life brought into alignment with what is claimed over it from a legitimate authority.

**Formation: learned through suffering.** The formative dimension of submission is one of the most striking features of its working. Hebrews 5:8 places it at the centre: "although he was a son, he learned obedience through what he suffered." The inner disposition of will-yielding is not naturally given; it is formed through the will's costly engagement with hardship under authority. For the Son, this was the formation of incarnate obedience through suffering; for the believer, the analogous formation is the sustained practice of will-yielding in the face of what costs something. Philippians 2:12 shows the sustained character of this formation: "as you have always obeyed, so now, not only as in my presence but much more in my absence, work out your own salvation with fear and trembling." The obedience that holds in the absence of external accountability is obedience that has been genuinely formed.

**Opposition.** What opposes submission is the proud self-assertion that places personal judgment over the claim of legitimate authority — the inner refusal that sets what the person thinks or wants above what the authority requires. The evidence names this in Romans 6:12–16, where the person who yields to sin is named as sin's servant. What one yields to determines whose servant one is: the inner movement of submission defines the person's deepest allegiance. The opposition to genuine submission is not difficulty but the will's preference for its own authority over the authority of another.

**Modes: fear and trembling as the inner register.** Philippians 2:12's "fear and trembling" names the inner register in which submission works: not the terror of punishment but the sober, active, engaged conscience that recognises the gravity of what it is placed under. This is the affective tone of submission at its deepest — not casual compliance but engaged, serious inner alignment.

**Production.** Over time, submission as an inner disposition forms the person who is trustworthy to God in the absence of external accountability — the person whose yielded will has become the stable ground of reliable character. First Peter 1:22 shows what sustained obedience produces: "obedience to the truth" has "purified your souls for a sincere brotherly love." The inner formation of submission produces the purity of inner life from which genuine love of others grows.

### 5.4 Submission — relational pattern of obedience

Submission as a relational pattern of obedience works outward from the inner disposition into the actual structures of human life and history. The movement here is the inner disposition's extension into the world of relationships — households, communities of faith, the gospel's reach among nations, and ultimately the eschatological submission of peoples to Christ. Romans 1:5 names the widest horizon: "to bring about the obedience of faith for the sake of his name among all the nations." The apostolic mission is oriented toward producing a relational pattern of obedience in communities and peoples as the outward expression of what the gospel works inwardly.

**Movement: from inner to relational.** The characteristic works by bringing the inner disposition of will-yielding into expression in specific relational structures. Ephesians 6:1 names the most basic of these: "Children, obey your parents in the Lord, for this is right." The relational pattern is concrete — a child, a parent, a specific relationship — and the inner disposition finds its expression there. The "in the Lord" grounds it theologically: the relational pattern of obedience is not sociological conformity but the inner disposition's expression through a divinely-ordered relational structure.

**The gospel as the generative movement.** The most expansive instance of this characteristic's working is the gospel's movement through the nations. Romans 1:5 places the obedience of faith at the centre of the apostolic mission: the gospel proclaimed produces, in those who receive it, an inner-relational response that is named as obedience. This is not law-keeping but the whole-person response of faith that submits to the claim of the proclaimed Christ. The working of submission in this form is the movement of the gospel itself through human communities.

**Formation: the eschatological horizon.** Genesis 49:10 — "and to him shall be the obedience of the peoples" — shows that the relational pattern of obedience has an eschatological form: the full expression of this characteristic is the willing, inner yielding of allegiance by the nations to the coming ruler. This is the telos of the characteristic's working over time — not merely individual obedience in discrete relationships but the comprehensive relational submission of humanity to Christ. Hebrews 5:9 names the salvific ground of this: "he became the source of eternal salvation to all who obey him." Obedience in its relational form connects the person to salvation not as a meritorious act but as the ongoing posture of the will that receives what Christ offers.

**Opposition.** What opposes the relational pattern is the refusal to extend the inner disposition into actual relational structures — the person who has a nominal inner commitment but whose conduct in relationships does not express it. Second Thessalonians 1:8 names the most extreme form: those who "do not obey the gospel" — the relational pattern withheld from the most fundamental authority claim. The evidence also names the counter-case: Galatians 2:5, where submission is deliberately withheld from false teachers to protect the gospel. The relational pattern of obedience is not unconditional; it is directed toward legitimate authority, and the evidence shows it can and should be withheld where authority oversteps its bounds.

Over time, the relational pattern of obedience works at two scales simultaneously: in the particularity of individual relationships (household, community, teaching) and in the sweep of the gospel's movement through human history toward the eschatological submission of the peoples.

### 5.5 Contrition — crushed and broken spirit

Contrition moves differently from all other characteristics in this study because its primary movement is not one the person initiates but one they undergo. The evidence rests on a single key verse, and it is addressed from the outside — from the God who declares where he dwells: "I dwell in the high and holy place, and also with him who is of a contrite and lowly spirit, to revive the spirit of the lowly, and to revive the heart of the contrite" (Isa 57:15). The movement in this verse is from God toward the contrite person: it is God who draws near, who revives, who initiates the encounter with the crushed spirit.

**Movement: God toward the broken.** The characteristic's most fundamental movement is divine-toward-creaturely. Contrition does not move toward God in the same initiating way that humility does — it is the state that draws God to the person. Psalm 34:18 confirms the pattern: "the Lord is near to the brokenhearted and saves the crushed in spirit." The movement is consistently in this direction — the broken condition is the attractor; God's nearness and saving response follow. Contrition works in the inner person by making it the place where God comes.

**Formation: genuine confrontation as the origin.** What produces contrition — what causes the spirit to be crushed — is genuine confrontation with moral failure or with the holiness of God. The evidence does not address the mechanics of this confrontation in detail, but the nature of the terms is clear: dak.ka (crushed, ground fine) names the result of a force applied to the inner person. The crushing comes from the encounter with what is real — God's holiness, the gravity of moral failure — breaking through the inner person's self-sufficiency. Contrition cannot be manufactured; it is the result of genuine reckoning.

**The reviving response.** What contrition produces, via God's response, is reviving: "to revive the spirit of the lowly, and to revive the heart of the contrite." The characteristic works in the inner person not as an end state but as the condition that opens the person to God's restoring work. The crushed spirit is not the telos of contrition; the revived spirit-and-heart is. Contrition is the opening, not the arrival.

**What is silent.** The single verse base for this characteristic means that a fuller account of how contrition is formed, how long it lasts, how it deepens, or how it relates to other inner states over time is not available from the direct evidence. The inner working that the evidence supports is: genuine moral confrontation → crushed spirit and heart → draws God's nearness → reviving. Beyond this arc, the evidence does not go.

Contrition works in the inner person by receiving a force from outside that breaks through self-sufficiency, and by drawing to itself the reviving presence of the God who chooses the broken spirit as his dwelling place.

### 5.6 Meekness — calibrated restraint and gentleness

Meekness works at the interface between person and encounter — it is the inner quality that governs the quality of engagement rather than a state that operates apart from relational contact. Its working life is therefore visible primarily in the moment of encounter with another person in their weakness or failure. The evidence concentrates this in a single key verse: "He can deal gently with the ignorant and wayward, since he himself is beset with weakness" (Heb 5:2). The working of meekness begins in that "can" — it is a capacity that the inner person possesses, drawn upon in the moment of engagement.

**Movement: outward into encounter.** The movement of meekness is outward — from the inner capacity into the encounter with another. The metriopatheō terminology names a quality of dealing-with, not a state-at-rest. Meekness works by shaping the quality of outward engagement: measured rather than excessive, compassionate rather than severe, appropriately responsive rather than self-protective. The working is relational in its expression even though the capacity is inner in its seat.

**The grounding that makes the movement possible.** The inner working of meekness depends on its ground: the awareness of shared creaturely weakness. The high priest "can deal gently" because "he himself is beset with weakness." The meekness works through this awareness — the person who has genuinely reckoned with their own limitation finds, in that reckoning, the inner resource for compassionate engagement with another's limitation. The inner movement that produces outward gentleness is the inward acknowledgment of shared weakness.

**Volitional readiness.** The okneō-absence dimension — the absence of self-protective hesitation — shows that meekness works not only in the quality of engagement but in its promptness. The meek person does not delay when engagement is called for. The inner working here is a readiness that does not wait for assurance that the encounter is safe before moving toward the other. This promptness is itself evidence of an inner freedom from self-protection: the person who is genuinely meek is not calculating risk before responding.

**Formation and opposition.** How meekness is formed is not directly addressed in the evidence. The ground it rests on — awareness of shared weakness — is itself a formed inner awareness, suggesting that the formation of meekness is bound up with the formation of genuine self-knowledge. What opposes meekness is the two opposite distortions named in the evidence: disproportionate severity (engaging the erring with excessive force) and self-protective hesitation (delaying or withdrawing when engagement is called for). Both are failures of the inner capacity that meekness names.

Meekness works in the inner person by drawing on a grounded awareness of shared weakness and translating that awareness into measured, compassionate, prompt engagement with those who err or are weak.

### 5.7 Dignity — grounded moral gravity

Dignity works differently from the other characteristics in this study because it does not have an obvious initiating movement or a named formation process in the evidence. It is the settled inner quality that the three verses present as already operative in the person — the quality from which peaceful public life, orderly household, and credible teaching flow. The working of dignity is therefore the working of a character quality: it does not so much move as it is — and in being what it is, it produces the order around itself that the evidence names.

**Movement: from inner quality to social order.** The working of dignity is from inside outward — not as an act but as a steady expression of character into the social sphere. First Timothy 2:2 shows the outcome: "a peaceful and quiet life, godly and dignified in every way." The dignity that characterises the inner person produces a particular quality of public life — peaceful, ordered, non-agitated. First Timothy 3:4 shows the same outward expression in the household: the person of dignity has "children who are submissive and respectful in every way." The inner quality creates a social environment that reflects it. Titus 2:7 shows it in the teaching context: "show integrity, dignity" — the teaching is authenticated by the character of the teacher.

**No direct formation account.** The evidence for dignity does not address how it is formed — what produces semnotēs in a person, through what disciplines or experiences it develops. The three verses present it as a present quality to be exhibited, prayed for, and taught in, not as something traced through its formation. This is a genuine limit of the evidence base and should be acknowledged as such rather than filled with inference.

**Opposition.** What opposes dignity is not directly named in the three-verse corpus, but the evidence places it in contrast with proud self-display (its structural opposite in the wider cluster) and with moral frivolousness — the absence of the seriousness and gravity that semnotēs names. The working of dignity excludes the self-promoting, the lightweight, and the unstable self-presentation that would undermine the ordered social sphere its presence creates.

**Modes.** Dignity works in three modes according to the evidence: as the quality of the whole life (1Ti 2:2 — "in every way"), as the source of household order (1Ti 3:4), and as the authenticating character of teaching (Tit 2:7). These are not sequential but simultaneous: the person of dignity inhabits all three spheres at once.

Dignity works in the inner person as a stable character quality that expresses itself consistently into the social world — creating peace, order, and credibility not through effort but through the settled gravity of what the inner person is.

### 5.8 Willing-heartedness — the freely-moved spirit

The most distinctive feature of willing-heartedness's working is that it moves first — before the command, before the calculation, before the requirement. Exodus 35:5 establishes the pattern: "Whoever is of a generous heart, let him bring the Lord's contribution: gold, silver, and bronze." The call is an invitation, not a compulsion; what determines the response is the condition of the heart. The willing heart brings because it is willing — the inner movement generates the outward act without being dragged into it.

**Movement: self-initiated outward.** The characteristic works by a movement from within outward that is self-initiated. The willing-hearted person does not wait to be compelled; their inner disposition moves them first. The evidence in Exodus 35 — "every willing-hearted person came" (35:22); the willing-hearted brought skilled labour and offerings (35:21–22) — shows a community-wide pattern where the inner orientation of multiple people generates a shared contribution without coercion. The working of willing-heartedness at the community level is the aggregated movement of many freely-moved spirits toward a shared goal.

**Formation: the two-register dynamic.** Willing-heartedness works in two modes that the evidence distinguishes. In the first mode, the willing spirit is simply operative: the person whose heart is freely oriented toward God brings, comes, and serves without friction. In the second mode, the willing spirit has been diminished by moral failure and must be sought from God as a restorative gift: "Restore to me the joy of your salvation, and uphold me with a willing spirit" (Psa 51:12). The formation of willing-heartedness is therefore not only a matter of its original possession but of its restoration — the person who has lost the freely-moving spirit through moral failure can seek its return from God. This makes willing-heartedness one of the few characteristics in this study where the evidence directly names both the experience of having it and the prayer for its restoration.

**The joy connection.** Psalm 51:12 pairs the willing spirit with the joy of salvation: "Restore to me the joy of your salvation, and uphold me with a willing spirit." The two are companion gifts sought together, suggesting that willing-heartedness and joy are functionally linked in their working — the freely-moving spirit is also the joyful spirit. Where joy has been lost, the willing spirit has also been diminished; where both are restored, the person is again capable of freely-given devotion.

**Opposition.** What opposes willing-heartedness is compulsion and moral failure. Compulsion produces the appearance of giving without its inner reality; the person who brings because they must is not in the mode that Exodus 35 describes. Moral failure diminishes the willing capacity — Psalm 51:12 implies that David's sin has cost him something of the freely-moving spirit. The inner working of willing-heartedness is therefore fragile in one register: it can be diminished by what the person does with their moral freedom.

**Production.** Over time, the willing spirit generates the pattern of habitual generous offering, wholehearted skilled service, and sustained joyful devotion. The tabernacle-building context of Exodus 35 shows the productive output: a community whose collective willing-heartedness generates a dwelling for God. This is the most expansive sustained production of the characteristic — not merely individual acts of generosity but the construction of the place where God dwells among his people.

Willing-heartedness works in the inner person as the most self-initiated of the cluster's characteristics — the movement from within that does not wait to be pushed but springs from the heart's own freely-moved orientation toward God, and which must be restored when moral failure has diminished its spring.
